
I want to setup a site to explain the basics of health care. Both my son and daughter tell me that it is a complex subject. My goal is to look at how health care is provided across different countries in the world. What are the main areas that I should use for my comparison. I want to talk about diagnostics, treatment plans, how health insurance works in different countries, how premiums work, public versus employment based healthcare. What are the main ailments that make people go bankrupt. Speed of health care availability. Urban vs rural areas. How technology will change health care.

Response:

Yes. I think this could become a **very useful educational site**, especially if you avoid making it simply a catalog of diseases. The more interesting question is:

> **“When a person gets sick, how does the healthcare system around them actually work?”**

That gives you a framework for comparing the US, UK, Canada, India, Germany, Japan, Australia, Singapore, etc.

A good foundation is the WHO's six health-system building blocks: **service delivery, workforce, information, medicines/technology, financing, and governance**. ([World Health Organization][1]) But for a site aimed at ordinary people, I would reorganize these into a more intuitive structure.

## I would structure your site around 10 major questions

### 1. What happens when I get sick?

This should be the entry point.

Show the **patient journey**:

**Symptom → self-care → primary care → diagnostic tests → specialist → treatment → hospitalization → rehabilitation → long-term management**

Then compare countries.

For example:

| Stage             | USA                                 | UK                  | India                    | Germany                 |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------- |
| First contact     | PCP / urgent care                   | GP                  | GP / clinic / specialist | GP                      |
| Specialist access | Referral often depends on insurance | Usually GP referral | Often direct             | Usually GP/referral     |
| MRI/CT            | Often insurance authorization       | NHS pathway         | Public/private           | Insurance/public system |
| Surgery           | Hospital                            | NHS/private         | Public/private           | Hospital                |
| Payment           | Insurance + patient                 | Mostly taxation     | Mixed                    | Social insurance        |

This gives visitors an immediate mental model.

# 2. How is healthcare organized?

This is where you explain the **architecture of the system**.

I'd have a dedicated section called:

### **Who pays for healthcare?**

Explain the major models:

* **Tax-funded:** UK, NHS
* **Social insurance:** Germany, France, Japan
* **National health insurance:** Canada, Taiwan
* **Employer/private insurance:** United States
* **Mixed systems:** India, Australia and many others
* **Out-of-pocket-heavy systems**

But don't stop at labeling them.

Explain the money flow:

**Citizen → government/employer/insurance → healthcare fund → hospital/doctor → patient**

This is where healthcare becomes much less mysterious.

WHO describes healthcare financing in terms of **raising money, pooling it, and purchasing/payments for services**. ([World Health Organization][2])

# 3. What does healthcare actually cost?

This could become one of the strongest parts of your site.

Break cost into:

### What the patient pays

* Premium
* Deductible
* Copayment
* Coinsurance
* Prescription cost
* Hospital bill
* Specialist fee
* Diagnostic tests
* Emergency care
* Rehabilitation
* Long-term care

### What society pays

* Taxes
* Employer contributions
* Government subsidies
* Insurance contributions
* Out-of-pocket spending

Then introduce a fascinating concept:

> **Healthcare can be expensive even when the patient doesn't receive a bill.**

For example, a person in a tax-funded system may pay relatively little at the point of service but contribute considerably through taxation.

# 4. Insurance: the part most people find confusing

I'd give this its own major section.

Explain:

**Premium → deductible → copay → coinsurance → out-of-pocket maximum**

And then compare:

* Employer-sponsored insurance
* Individual insurance
* Government insurance
* Mandatory insurance
* Supplemental insurance
* Private insurance
* Insurance exclusions
* Pre-existing conditions
* Networks
* Prior authorization
* Referral requirements
* Formularies
* Insurance claims

You could create interactive examples:

> **“You have a $10,000 hospital bill. What do you actually pay?”**

Then let the visitor select:

**USA / Germany / UK / India / Canada**

That would make the differences dramatically easier to understand.

# 5. How do doctors diagnose and treat disease?

This should be your **clinical pathway** section.

Don't try to teach medicine in excessive depth. Instead explain the decision-making process.

### Diagnosis

**Symptoms → history → physical examination → differential diagnosis → tests → diagnosis**

Then explain:

* Blood tests
* X-rays
* CT
* MRI
* Ultrasound
* ECG
* Biopsy
* Genetic testing
* Endoscopy
* Pathology

Then:

### Treatment

**Diagnosis → treatment options → benefits/risks → shared decision → treatment → monitoring → adjustment**

This is an important distinction because healthcare isn't simply:

> "I have disease X → doctor gives medicine Y."

Modern medicine is much more about **probabilities, risk, evidence and trade-offs**.

# 6. Which diseases actually consume healthcare resources?

This is where your idea about **bankruptcy** becomes particularly interesting.

I'd divide illnesses into:

### Common but relatively inexpensive

* Minor infections
* Allergies
* Simple injuries
* Routine hypertension

### Common and expensive over time

* Diabetes
* Heart disease
* COPD
* Kidney disease
* Arthritis
* Dementia

### Less common but potentially financially catastrophic

* Cancer
* Major trauma
* Organ failure
* Premature birth/NICU
* Major neurological disease
* Complex surgery
* Long-term disability

And distinguish between:

**“What causes the most healthcare spending?”**

and

**“What causes individuals to experience financial catastrophe?”**

Those aren't necessarily the same thing.

This could become a very powerful visualization:

> **Disease → probability → treatment cost → duration → financial risk**

# 7. Access: can you actually get care?

This deserves a major section because **having insurance isn't the same as having access**.

Measure:

### Availability

* Number of doctors
* Number of nurses
* Hospitals
* ICU beds
* Specialists
* Diagnostic equipment

### Speed

* Primary-care appointment
* Specialist appointment
* MRI
* Surgery
* Cancer treatment
* Emergency care

### Geography

Compare:

**Urban → suburban → rural → remote**

And ask:

> How far does a person have to travel to see a doctor?

WHO explicitly considers access, coverage and quality central to universal health coverage, while also emphasizing primary care as the foundation of the system. ([World Health Organization][3])

---

# 8. Quality of healthcare

This is a dimension I would add strongly to your original list.

Don't equate **expensive healthcare with good healthcare**.

Measure:

* Mortality
* Preventable deaths
* Hospital infections
* Surgical complications
* Cancer survival
* Maternal mortality
* Infant mortality
* Readmission rates
* Medication errors
* Patient satisfaction
* Continuity of care

The OECD's current comparison framework is useful here: it looks at **health status, risk factors, access, quality, and health-system capacity/resources**, while also examining efficiency, equity, sustainability and resilience. ([OECD][4])

# 10. Who is responsible when things go wrong?

This is the **governance** component that most layperson healthcare explanations miss.

Explain:

* Government
* Hospitals
* Doctors
* Insurance companies
* Pharmaceutical companies
* Medical-device companies
* Regulators
* Employers
* Patients

And questions such as:

**Who approves a drug?**

**Who decides what insurance covers?**

**Who sets medical standards?**

**Who negotiates drug prices?**

**Who pays when something goes wrong?**

**Who decides whether an expensive treatment is worth paying for?**

This is where healthcare becomes a fascinating intersection of **medicine + economics + politics + technology + human psychology**.

### The key idea

I would **not** position the website as "medical advice."

I'd position it as:

> **"Understand how healthcare works before you need it."**

And underneath that, perhaps:

> **Medicine tells us how to treat disease. Healthcare tells us how society delivers that treatment.**

That distinction gives you an enormous amount of territory to explore.

The WHO framework is a particularly good foundation because it treats healthcare as a system rather than merely a collection of doctors and hospitals; importantly, WHO's newer work is also emphasizing the **relationships between the components**, rather than viewing them as isolated building blocks. ([AHPSR][5])

If you build this as a data-driven site, I'd go one step further: **create a standardized "Healthcare Country Scorecard" with perhaps 30–40 indicators**, so that a visitor can select two countries and see *exactly* how their systems differ. That could become the core intellectual property of the site.
