### Full article: Intestines

**What they do.** The intestines are a continuous tube — roughly 7–8 metres in total — folded neatly inside your belly. The small intestine does most of the work: its lining is covered in millions of tiny finger-like projections (villi) that absorb nutrients into the blood. Whatever's left passes to the large intestine (colon), which absorbs water and salts and shapes the remainder into stool. Living throughout the gut are trillions of bacteria — the gut microbiome — which help digest fibre, make some vitamins, and interact with the immune system. Research here is exciting but young: proven so far is that fibre feeds beneficial bacteria and fermented foods fit a healthy diet; most "gut reset" kits and direct-to-consumer microbiome tests go well beyond the evidence.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* IBS (irritable bowel syndrome) — cramping, bloating, and diarrhoea and/or constipation without visible damage to the gut. It's a disorder of gut-brain interaction — real and distressing, but it doesn't cause inflammation or raise cancer risk. IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) — despite the similar name, a very different condition: the immune system attacks the gut, causing visible inflammation and damage. Its two forms are Crohn's disease (can strike anywhere from mouth to anus, often in patches) and ulcerative colitis (continuous inflammation of the colon and rectum). IBD needs specialist treatment and can have serious complications. Coeliac disease — an immune reaction to gluten that damages the small intestine; managed by a strict gluten-free diet. Diverticular disease — small pouches in the colon wall that can inflame.
*Less common but serious:* Bowel obstruction — a blockage stopping the passage of stool and gas; an emergency. Colorectal cancer — one of the most common cancers worldwide, and highly treatable when caught early, which is why screening matters.

**Which doctors get involved.** A gastroenterologist diagnoses and manages intestinal conditions, including with colonoscopy. A colorectal surgeon operates — for IBD complications, obstructions, and bowel cancers. A dietitian works alongside your doctor on eating plans (especially for IBS and coeliac disease) — but diet advice should follow diagnosis, not replace it. Your primary care doctor is usually the first stop for bowel symptoms.

**Usual tests.** *Procedures:* colonoscopy — a flexible camera passed through the rectum to inspect the colon; polyps can be removed and biopsies taken during it. Capsule endoscopy — you swallow a pill-sized camera that photographs the small intestine. *Lab:* stool tests (blood, infection, inflammation markers), blood tests (anaemia, inflammation, coeliac antibodies). *Imaging:* CT or MRI scans to map inflammation or blockages.

**Keeping it healthy.** Fibre is the gut's best friend: vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and legumes feed both you and your microbiome. Drink enough water and stay active — both keep the bowels moving regularly. Don't smoke; smoking worsens Crohn's disease in particular. Take bowel screening when it's offered to you — it catches polyps before they turn dangerous. Know your family history: bowel cancer or IBD in close relatives changes when screening should start — ask your doctor.
*Myth box:* "You need to regularly 'clean out' your colon with flushes or detox kits." False — a healthy colon cleans itself, and aggressive "cleanses" can dehydrate you, disturb gut bacteria, and even injure the bowel. Persistent constipation deserves a doctor, not a kit.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: blood in the stool or black stools; a lasting change in bowel habits (new constipation, diarrhoea, or narrower stools); unexplained weight loss; persistent abdominal pain, cramping, or bloating; mucus in the stool. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: sudden severe abdominal pain with a swollen belly and no longer passing gas or stool (possible obstruction); vomiting blood or material like coffee grounds; heavy rectal bleeding; severe pain with fever and a rigid belly.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including a stool and diet diary, which genuinely helps) → physical exam → blood and stool tests → colonoscopy with biopsies, often the decisive test → CT/MRI if Crohn's or complications are suspected → diagnosis and treatment plan. IBS is diagnosed by symptom patterns after other causes are ruled out — there is no single IBS test, which is why the workup matters.

**Common treatment approaches.** IBS is managed with diet adjustments (such as a low-FODMAP plan under dietitian guidance), medicines that calm cramping or regulate bowel movements, and gut-brain approaches like stress management — treatment is highly individual. IBD is treated with anti-inflammatory medicines, immune-calming drugs, and biologic therapies that target specific immune pathways; surgery removes damaged sections when medicines aren't enough. Coeliac disease is managed with a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet. Bowel obstructions may settle with hospital care (resting the bowel, fluids) or need emergency surgery.
