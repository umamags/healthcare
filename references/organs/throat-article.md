# Throat — full article

**What it does.** The throat is a busy crossroads. The pharynx is the muscular tube that guides food and drink down to the esophagus, while the larynx — the voice box — guards the airway and holds the vocal cords, two bands of tissue that vibrate to produce voice. A flap called the epiglottis snaps shut over the windpipe each time you swallow, keeping food out of the lungs. The tonsils, clusters of immune tissue at the back of the throat, help fight germs — most actively in childhood.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Sore throat (pharyngitis) — pain, scratchiness, or irritation; most cases are viral, part of a cold. Tonsillitis — infected, swollen tonsils, common in children and teens. Strep throat — a bacterial sore throat (group A streptococcus) with sudden severe pain and fever, but usually no cough or runny nose. Laryngitis — inflamed vocal cords causing hoarseness or voice loss; often viral or from voice overuse. Acid reflux irritation — stomach acid reaching the throat, causing chronic throat-clearing, hoarseness, or a "lump" feeling.
*Less common but serious:* Epiglottitis — swelling of the epiglottis, mostly in young children: drooling, difficulty swallowing, and fever; an emergency. Peritonsillar abscess ("quinsy") — a pocket of pus beside a tonsil; severe one-sided throat pain with difficulty opening the mouth. Persistent swallowing difficulty (dysphagia) — many possible causes and always worth evaluating. Head and neck cancers — can present as hoarseness lasting weeks, a neck lump, or swallowing difficulty (see our cancer section).

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor handles straightforward sore throats. An ENT specialist (otolaryngologist) manages recurrent tonsillitis, voice disorders, swallowing problems, and throat surgery. A speech therapist rehabilitates voice and swallowing. Gastroenterologists may be involved when reflux is the driver.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* visual throat exam, feeling the neck for swollen glands. *Swab:* rapid strep test or throat culture — confirms bacterial infection before antibiotics are given. *Camera:* laryngoscopy — a thin flexible camera views the vocal cords and larynx; used for persistent hoarseness or swallowing issues. *Swallowing:* formal swallowing assessment or imaging if dysphagia persists.

**Keeping it healthy.** Don't smoke — tobacco irritates the throat and is a major risk factor for throat cancers. Avoid prolonged shouting or voice strain; rest your voice when hoarse. Stay hydrated. Treat acid reflux rather than living with it. Wash your hands often, since most sore throats spread as viral infections.
*Myth box:* "Every sore throat needs antibiotics." False — the majority are viral, and antibiotics do nothing against viruses. Taking them "just in case" doesn't speed recovery and drives antibiotic resistance. Antibiotics are for test-confirmed bacterial cases like strep throat, where they also help prevent rare complications.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: sore throat lasting more than a week, hoarseness lasting more than 2–3 weeks, difficulty swallowing, a lump in the neck, or tonsillitis that keeps coming back. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: trouble breathing or swallowing; a young child who is drooling, struggling to swallow, and feverish (possible epiglottitis — keep the child calm and upright, and don't try to look down the throat); sudden severe throat or tongue swelling, especially with allergy symptoms or after a sting.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → throat exam and neck check → rapid strep test or swab if bacterial infection is suspected → laryngoscopy for persistent hoarseness or swallowing difficulty → diagnosis and plan. Most simple sore throats are diagnosed clinically in one visit.

**Common treatment approaches.** Viral sore throats: rest, fluids, and pain-relief medication classes; honey can soothe adults (not for infants under one year). Strep throat: antibiotics — and it's important to finish the full course. Reflux-related irritation: lifestyle measures plus acid-reducing medication classes. Laryngitis: voice rest and hydration. Recurrent tonsillitis: tonsillectomy (tonsil removal) may be discussed when episodes are frequent. Voice and swallowing disorders: speech therapy. Abscesses: drainage plus antibiotics.
