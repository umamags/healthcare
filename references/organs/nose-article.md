# Nose — full article

**What it does.** The nose is the airway's front door and filter. Incoming air is warmed, moistened, and trapped of dust and germs by mucus and tiny hairs before it reaches the lungs. High inside the nasal cavity sits the olfactory region — smell receptors that also contribute heavily to taste (food seems bland when your nose is blocked). The nose drains the paranasal sinuses, air-filled cavities in the skull bones that lighten the skull and resonate the voice. When drainage blocks, sinuses become a common trouble spot.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Allergic rhinitis (hay fever) — the immune system overreacts to pollen, dust mites, or animal dander: sneezing, runny nose, itchy eyes. Sinusitis — inflamed sinuses causing facial pressure, congestion, and thick discharge; most cases are viral and resolve on their own. Nosebleeds — usually from the front of the nose (dry air, nose-picking, colds); common and usually minor. Deviated septum — the wall between nostrils is crooked; many people have a mild version with no symptoms.
*Less common but serious:* Chronic sinusitis — sinus inflammation lasting 12 weeks or more, sometimes with nasal polyps (soft, painless growths). Posterior nosebleeds — bleeding from deeper vessels; heavier and harder to stop. Persistent loss of smell — can follow infections or signal chronic sinus disease; occasionally linked to neurological conditions, so lasting loss deserves checking.

**Which doctors get involved.** An ENT specialist (otolaryngologist) handles structural problems, chronic sinusitis, polyps, and surgery. An allergist/immunologist manages allergy-driven rhinitis and testing. A primary care doctor handles straightforward colds, mild sinusitis, and nosebleed first aid.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* nasal examination, sometimes with a thin flexible endoscope to see deep passages. *Allergy:* skin-prick or blood tests to identify triggers. *Imaging:* CT scan of the sinuses — mainly for chronic sinusitis or surgical planning, not for routine colds. *Smell:* formal smell testing occasionally.

**Keeping it healthy.** Treat allergies properly instead of just enduring them — uncontrolled rhinitis often drives sinus problems. Saline rinses or sprays can ease congestion and wash out irritants. Don't smoke — it paralyzes the tiny hairs that clear mucus. Humidify very dry indoor air in winter. Blow gently, one nostril at a time; hard blowing can force mucus into the sinuses.
*Myth box:* "A sinus infection always needs antibiotics." False — most sinusitis is viral, and antibiotics don't touch viruses. Doctors reserve antibiotics for cases that look bacterial: symptoms beyond ~10 days without improving, severe symptoms, or a "double worsening" after initial improvement. Unnecessary antibiotics fuel resistance.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: congestion lasting more than ~10 days, sinus symptoms that keep returning, frequent nosebleeds, one-sided blockage that never clears, or loss of smell persisting beyond a cold. Emergency — seek emergency care or call your local emergency number: a nosebleed not stopping after ~20 minutes of proper pressure (sit upright, lean slightly forward, pinch the soft part of the nose — don't tilt your head back); difficulty breathing from nasal swelling or injury; heavy bleeding after a head injury.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → nasal exam (with endoscope if needed) → allergy testing when rhinitis is suspected → CT scan if sinusitis is chronic or surgery is being considered → diagnosis and plan. Most nosebleeds and colds are diagnosed clinically without any scans.

**Common treatment approaches.** Allergic rhinitis: avoiding known triggers, antihistamines, nasal steroid sprays, saline irrigation; allergy immunotherapy (desensitization) for severe cases. Sinusitis: rest, fluids, saline, and decongestants short-term; antibiotics only when a bacterial infection is diagnosed. Nosebleeds: pressure first aid; cautery or packing for recurrent ones. Structural issues: septoplasty (straightening the septum) or polyp removal surgery when they meaningfully block breathing or drive chronic infection.
