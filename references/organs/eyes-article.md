# Eyes — full article

**What they do.** Each eye is a precision optical instrument about the size of a table-tennis ball. Light enters through the cornea (the clear front window), passes through the pupil, and is focused by the lens onto the retina — a layer of light-sensitive cells lining the back of the eye. The retina converts light into electrical signals, and the optic nerve carries them to the brain, which assembles them into the images you "see." Tiny muscles constantly adjust the lens (focusing) and the pupil (light levels), and tears keep the surface clean and moist.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Refractive errors — the eye's shape doesn't focus light correctly: myopia (short-sightedness), hyperopia (long-sightedness), astigmatism (uneven curvature). Cataract — the lens clouds with age, blurring vision; extremely common in older adults. Glaucoma — damage to the optic nerve, often linked to high pressure inside the eye; early stages usually have no symptoms. Conjunctivitis ("pink eye") — inflammation of the eye's surface, often viral or allergic. Dry eye — poor tear quality or quantity; common with screens and aging.
*Less common but serious:* Age-related macular degeneration — the central retina deteriorates, blurring the center of vision. Diabetic retinopathy — diabetes damages the retina's blood vessels; a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults. Retinal detachment — the retina peels away; an emergency.

**Which doctors get involved.** An ophthalmologist is a medical doctor specializing in eyes — diagnoses conditions, prescribes medication, and performs surgery. An optometrist tests vision and prescribes glasses and contact lenses (their exact scope, including what they may treat, varies by country). An optician fits and dispenses glasses. Your primary care doctor may handle simple eye infections and diabetes-related referrals.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* visual acuity chart (the letter chart), refraction test ("which is clearer, one or two?"), slit-lamp exam (magnified view of the eye's structures). *Pressure:* tonometry — measures pressure inside the eye (glaucoma check). *Retina:* dilated retinal exam (drops widen the pupil so the doctor can see the retina), OCT scan (detailed cross-section images of the retina). *Fields:* visual field test (maps peripheral vision, often for glaucoma).

**Keeping them healthy.** Have regular eye exams — especially if you have diabetes or a family history of glaucoma, since both can damage vision silently. Wear sunglasses with UV protection. Follow the 20-20-20 rule during screen time. Don't smoke — smoking raises the risk of cataract and macular degeneration. Keep blood sugar and blood pressure controlled. Wear safety glasses for DIY, lab, or industrial work.
*Myth box:* "Eating carrots will fix poor eyesight." False — vitamin A supports eye health, but no food can correct myopia, astigmatism, or other refractive errors. Only glasses, contacts, or surgery change the eye's focusing.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: gradual blurring or vision changes, persistent dryness or irritation, a sudden increase in floaters or flashes. Emergency — seek emergency care or call your local emergency number: sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes; an eye injury or chemical splash (rinse immediately with plenty of clean water for 15–20 minutes, then get emergency care — don't wait to see if it feels better); a dark curtain, shadow, or veil spreading across your vision.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → visual acuity and refraction → slit-lamp exam → eye pressure check → dilated retinal exam → OCT or visual field testing if glaucoma, macular degeneration, or retinal disease is suspected → diagnosis and plan. Diabetic patients are typically offered regular dilated exams even with no symptoms, because retinopathy starts silently.

**Common treatment approaches.** Refractive errors: glasses, contact lenses, or laser refractive surgery. Cataract: surgery replacing the cloudy lens with an artificial one — one of the most common and successful operations in medicine. Glaucoma: pressure-lowering eye drops, laser treatment, or surgery to protect the optic nerve. Macular degeneration: injections that slow abnormal blood-vessel growth, plus lifestyle measures. Diabetic retinopathy: laser treatment, injections, and — fundamentally — blood sugar control. Infections: antibiotic or antiviral eye drops. Dry eye: artificial tears and lid care.
