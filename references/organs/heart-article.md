# Heart — full article

**What it does.** The heart is a muscular pump roughly the size of your fist. It beats around 100,000 times a day, pushing about 7,500 litres of blood through 100,000 km of vessels. The right side collects oxygen-poor blood and sends it to the lungs; the left side receives oxygen-rich blood and pumps it to the body. Four valves keep blood flowing one way, and an electrical conduction system (starting at the sinoatrial node — the heart's natural pacemaker) sets the rhythm. The coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself — when they narrow, the heart is starved of its own blood supply.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Coronary artery disease — plaque builds up inside coronary arteries, narrowing them; causes angina (chest pain on exertion). Heart attack (myocardial infarction) — a coronary artery blocks suddenly and heart muscle starts dying; a medical emergency. Heart failure — the pump weakens and can't keep up with the body's needs (it does not mean the heart has stopped). Arrhythmias — rhythm faults; atrial fibrillation (AFib), the commonest, raises stroke risk. Valve disease — valves narrow (stenosis) or leak (regurgitation).
*Less common but serious:* Congenital heart defects (present from birth) · cardiomyopathy (heart muscle disease) · endocarditis (infection of the inner lining/valves).

**Which doctors get involved.** A cardiologist diagnoses and manages heart conditions medically. An interventional cardiologist opens blocked arteries (angioplasty, stents). An electrophysiologist handles rhythm disorders. A cardiac (cardiothoracic) surgeon operates — bypasses, valve repair/replacement. Your primary care doctor usually coordinates and manages risk factors like blood pressure and cholesterol.

**Usual tests.** *Bedside:* blood pressure, ECG (the heart's electrical tracing). *Imaging:* echocardiogram (ultrasound of the heart's structure and pumping), stress test (ECG/exercise or imaging under exertion), coronary angiogram (dye X-ray of the arteries), CT calcium scan. *Monitoring:* Holter monitor (24–48 hour rhythm recording). *Blood:* troponin (rises in a heart attack), lipid/cholesterol panel, blood sugar.

**Keeping it healthy.** Move: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity weekly (brisk walking counts). Eat: plenty of vegetables and whole grains; less salt, sugary drinks, and saturated fat. Don't smoke — quitting helps at any age. Limit alcohol. Control the big three risk factors: blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar/diabetes. Sleep 7–9 hours. Know your family history — early heart disease in close relatives matters.
*Myth box:* "You'd feel it if your blood pressure were high." Usually false — hypertension is called the silent killer precisely because it typically has no symptoms. That's why it's measured, not felt.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: fluttering or racing heartbeat, swollen ankles or feet, unusual tiredness doing normal activities, waking at night short of breath. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: chest pain, pressure, heaviness, or tightness; pain spreading to the arm, neck, jaw, back, or stomach; sudden shortness of breath; breaking into a cold sweat or feeling nauseated along with chest discomfort; fainting or near-fainting. Chew nothing, drive nowhere — call first. Note: women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often have "atypical" heart attacks — breathlessness, fatigue, or upper-abdominal pain without dramatic chest pain.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* symptoms and history → physical exam → ECG and blood tests → echocardiogram and/or stress test → angiogram if blockages are suspected → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (chest pain):* straight to the emergency department → immediate ECG + troponin blood test → treatment begins before all results are back. Minutes matter because heart muscle dies quickly.

**Common treatment approaches.** Lifestyle change is the foundation for almost everything. Medication classes doctors commonly use: blood thinners/antiplatelets, cholesterol-lowering drugs (statins), blood-pressure medicines, drugs that ease the heart's workload in heart failure, and rhythm-control drugs. Procedures: angioplasty with stent (opening a blocked artery via a catheter), ablation (correcting faulty electrical pathways). Surgery: coronary bypass, valve repair or replacement. Devices: pacemakers, implantable defibrillators. Cardiac rehabilitation — supervised exercise plus education after a heart event — strongly improves recovery.
