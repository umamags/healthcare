# Ears — full article

**What they do.** The ear does two distinct jobs. For hearing: the outer ear funnels sound waves down the canal to the eardrum, three tiny middle-ear bones amplify the vibrations, and the cochlea — a fluid-filled spiral in the inner ear — converts them into electrical signals for the auditory nerve. For balance: the vestibular system, also in the inner ear, senses head movement and position using fluid and tiny crystals. Damage anywhere along this chain affects hearing, balance, or both.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Age-related hearing loss — gradual decline, often starting with high frequencies; very common in older adults. Noise-induced hearing loss — loud sound damages the cochlea's hair cells permanently. Otitis media — middle-ear infection, very common in children (ear pain, fever, sometimes fluid behind the eardrum). Swimmer's ear — outer-canal infection after moisture exposure. Earwax blockage — wax builds up and dulls hearing. Tinnitus — ringing, buzzing, or hissing with no external source; often linked to hearing loss.
*Less common but serious:* Ménière's disease — inner-ear disorder causing vertigo attacks, fluctuating hearing, and tinnitus. Perforated eardrum — a tear from infection, pressure changes, or injury. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss — rapid, unexplained hearing loss; an emergency. Cholesteatoma — an abnormal skin growth in the middle ear that can damage structures if untreated.

**Which doctors get involved.** An ENT specialist (otolaryngologist) diagnoses and treats ear diseases medically and surgically. An audiologist performs hearing tests and fits hearing aids. A primary care doctor handles simple infections and referrals. Balance disorders may also involve neurologists or vestibular physiotherapists.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* otoscopy — looking at the canal and eardrum with a lighted instrument. *Hearing:* audiometry — you respond to tones at different pitches and volumes; tympanometry — measures eardrum movement and middle-ear pressure. *Balance:* positional and movement tests for vertigo. *Imaging:* CT or MRI occasionally, for suspected growths or complications.

**Keeping them healthy.** Protect your ears from loud noise — earplugs or earmuffs at concerts, near machinery, or with firearms; keep personal audio at moderate volume. Never push cotton buds or objects deep into the canal. Dry ears gently after swimming. Treat ear infections promptly rather than waiting them out. If you find yourself asking people to repeat themselves or turning the TV up, get a hearing check — untreated hearing loss is linked to social withdrawal and faster cognitive decline.
*Myth box:* "Cotton buds are the safe way to clean ears." False — they push wax deeper, can scratch the canal, and can even perforate the eardrum. Ears usually clean themselves; wax only needs professional removal when it causes blockage.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: gradual hearing loss, persistent ringing, repeated infections, ear pain with fever, or dizziness that interferes with daily life. Emergency — seek same-day emergency care or call your local emergency number: sudden hearing loss in one or both ears (treatment is most effective within days, so don't wait); severe pain, bleeding, or discharge after an ear injury; swelling and tenderness behind the ear with fever (possible mastoid infection). Dizziness combined with weakness, speech difficulty, or facial droop suggests stroke — call emergency immediately.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → otoscopy → hearing test (audiometry) and tympanometry → diagnosis. Vertigo gets positional testing to identify the cause. Imaging is reserved for suspected growths, complications, or unexplained findings.

**Common treatment approaches.** Infections: antibiotic ear drops or oral antibiotics for bacterial infections; many childhood middle-ear infections are monitored first. Hearing loss: hearing aids are the mainstay; cochlear implants for severe to profound loss. Tinnitus: no cure in most cases, but management helps — treating the underlying hearing loss, sound therapy, and counseling. Ménière's: medications for vertigo episodes, dietary measures, and procedures in persistent cases. Earwax: softening drops and professional removal. Perforated eardrum: usually heals on its own with the ear kept dry; surgery if it doesn't.
