# Vulva — full article

**What it does.** The vulva is the external female genitals: the fleshy folds called labia (outer and inner), the clitoris at the top, and the openings of the urethra and vagina between the labia. Its jobs are protection and sensation. The labia shield the delicate openings from friction and infection, and the clitoris — packed with nerve endings — exists purely for sexual pleasure. One common confusion is worth clearing up first: many people use the word "vagina" for this whole area, but the vagina is the internal passage. The part you can see is the vulva. Vulvas vary enormously in size, shape, and colour, and almost all of that variation is normal.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Irritation and contact allergy — scented soaps, bubble baths, laundry detergents, pads, and "intimate" sprays are frequent culprits behind itching and soreness. Bartholin's cyst — a painless swelling near the vaginal opening where a small fluid gland gets blocked; if it becomes infected it turns into a painful abscess. Ingrown hairs and minor skin infections around hair removal.
*Less common but serious:* Lichen sclerosus — a long-term skin condition causing itchy, pale, thinner patches; without treatment the skin can scar and narrow the vaginal opening. Lichen planus — a related inflammatory condition that can also affect the vagina and mouth. Genital warts — soft growths caused by the virus HPV. Vulvar cancer — rare, but its early signs (a persistent sore, lump, or changed patch of skin) are easy to mistake for a minor skin problem, which is why non-healing changes deserve a check.

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor or gynaecologist is the usual first stop. Because the vulva is skin, a dermatologist is often involved in conditions like lichen sclerosus. Some hospitals have vulvar specialists or combined vulvar clinics for stubborn or complex cases, and a gynaecologist leads if cancer is suspected.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* a careful visual examination of the vulvar skin, sometimes with magnification. *Lab tests:* swabs if infection is possible. *Procedures:* a skin biopsy — a tiny sample of skin removed under local anaesthetic — is the key test when a patch doesn't heal or a condition like lichen sclerosus or cancer needs confirming. Most vulvar diagnosis starts with simply looking properly, which is why persistent symptoms shouldn't be self-treated for months without an exam.

**Keeping it healthy.** Wash with plain water only, or use a simple soap substitute; pat dry rather than rubbing. Skip scented products, wet wipes with fragrance, and vaginal deodorants. Wear breathable cotton underwear and change out of sweaty or damp clothing. Use condoms to lower the risk of HPV and other STIs, and keep up HPV vaccination where it's offered — it protects against the wart-causing and most cancer-causing strains. Most of all, learn your own normal: a quick look with a mirror now and then makes real changes much easier to spot.
*Myth box:* "Itching down there always means a yeast infection, so antifungal cream is the automatic answer." False. On the vulva, irritation from products, eczema, and lichen sclerosus are just as common as thrush — and steroid-avoiding or antifungal creams won't fix them. Worse, months of self-treating can delay spotting a skin condition that needs proper care. Persistent itching deserves an examination, not another tube of cream.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: itching or soreness lasting more than a couple of weeks; a lump or swelling; skin becoming paler, thicker, redder, or changing texture; a sore, crack, or wart-like growth that doesn't heal; pain during sex at the vaginal opening. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: a swelling near the vaginal opening that becomes rapidly more painful with fever and spreading redness (a Bartholin's abscess that may need draining the same day), or any rapidly spreading red, hot, very painful genital swelling with fever.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including every product and cream you've been using) → visual exam → swabs if infection is suspected → skin biopsy for persistent patches, sores, or lumps → diagnosis and a treatment plan. Vulvar conditions are frequently diagnosed by appearance plus biopsy rather than blood tests.

**Common treatment approaches.** Removing the irritant and using bland moisturisers/emollients for irritation · antifungal medicines for confirmed thrush · prescription topical corticosteroid ointments as the mainstay for lichen sclerosus and lichen planus · warm sitz baths and antibiotics for a small Bartholin's abscess, or a minor drainage procedure if it's large or keeps returning · wart treatments applied in clinic or prescribed for home use · surgery (and sometimes other cancer treatments) for vulvar cancer. Doctors commonly consider biopsy before long-term treatment whenever the diagnosis is uncertain.
