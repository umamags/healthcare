# Spinal cord — full article

**What it does.** The spinal cord is a bundle of nerves about as thick as a finger, running from the brainstem down inside the protective column of vertebrae. It is the information highway of the body: movement commands travel down it from the brain, and sensations — touch, pain, temperature, position — travel up it. Some quick reflexes (like jerking your hand from heat) are handled by the cord itself without waiting for the brain. The level of any damage matters enormously: an injury high in the neck affects far more of the body than one in the lower back. Nerves branch off between each vertebra to serve specific regions, which is why a pinched nerve in the neck causes arm symptoms while one in the lower back causes leg symptoms.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Herniated ("slipped") disc — the soft center of a spinal disc bulges out and presses on a nearby nerve, often causing sciatica: sharp, shooting pain down the leg. Spinal stenosis — the spinal canal narrows, often with age; typically causes leg pain, numbness, or heaviness when walking that eases with rest. Muscle or ligament strain — the cause of most back pain; painful but not dangerous, and not actually a cord problem.
*Less common but serious:* Spinal cord injury — trauma from road accidents, falls, or sports can bruise, compress, or sever the cord, causing paralysis below the injury level (injuries are described as "complete" or "incomplete"). Cauda equina syndrome — the bundle of nerves at the cord's lower end gets compressed; a surgical emergency. Spinal tumours and spinal infections — rare, but can damage the cord if not treated.

**Which doctors get involved.** A neurologist assesses nerve function. A neurosurgeon or orthopedic spine surgeon operates on the spine. A physiotherapist leads rehabilitation and strengthening. A pain specialist helps with persistent nerve pain. Emergency and trauma teams handle acute spinal injuries.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* neurological exam — muscle strength, reflexes, and sensation in the limbs. *Imaging:* MRI — the key test for discs, the cord, and nerves; CT or X-ray — better for bones and fractures. *Nerve tests:* nerve conduction studies or EMG in selected cases. Note: most simple back pain needs no scan at all — imaging is for nerve symptoms, trauma, or pain that won't settle.

**Keeping it healthy.** Stay active — movement, including walking and core-strengthening exercise, protects the spine. Lift with your legs, keeping the load close. Keep a healthy weight to reduce load on discs. Don't smoke — discs in smokers get poorer blood supply and degenerate faster. Set up your workstation so you're not hunched for hours.
*Myth box:* "A slipped disc always needs surgery" and "bed rest is best for back pain." Both false. Most herniated discs improve without surgery, and prolonged bed rest usually makes back pain worse, not better. Surgery is reserved for specific situations — such as cauda equina syndrome or persistent nerve compression that doesn't respond to conservative care. This is also why back-pain advice online needs skepticism: it's one of the most misinformation-heavy areas in medicine.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: back or neck pain with numbness, tingling, or weakness in an arm or leg; pain that hasn't improved after a few weeks; pain following a fall or accident. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: new difficulty controlling bladder or bowels, numbness around the genitals or inner thighs (the "saddle" area), or rapidly worsening leg weakness. Together these can signal cauda equina syndrome, where emergency surgery within hours can prevent permanent damage.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** History and physical/neurological exam → MRI if there are nerve symptoms, trauma, or red flags → diagnosis and plan. For straightforward muscle-strain back pain, doctors often diagnose clinically and skip imaging entirely. After serious trauma, the spine is stabilized first and imaged urgently.

**Common treatment approaches.** Most cases: staying active, physiotherapy, heat or cold, and short-term pain-relief medication classes (such as anti-inflammatories); structured exercise programs for persistent pain. Selected nerve pain: targeted injections. Surgery (discectomy, laminectomy, spinal fusion) for specific indications like cauda equina syndrome or nerve compression that doesn't improve. Spinal cord injury: emergency stabilization, surgery to relieve pressure on the cord, then long rehabilitation — physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and assistive devices.
