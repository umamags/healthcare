# Feet

**What they do.** Again, honesty first: the foot isn't one organ. Each foot contains 26 bones — a quarter of your skeleton — arranged into arches that act as shock absorbers, plus ligaments, tendons, and nerves. The Achilles tendon, connecting calf to heel, is the strongest tendon in the body. With every step, your feet accept your full body weight, adapt to uneven ground, balance you, and spring you forward. They do this thousands of times a day, usually without thanks, which is why small problems here affect the whole body — knees, hips, and back compensate when feet hurt.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Plantar fasciitis — inflammation of the thick band of tissue under the foot; classic symptom is sharp heel pain with the first steps in the morning. Bunions — the big-toe joint drifts outward, forming a bony bump that rubs in shoes. Athlete's foot — a fungal infection causing itchy, scaly skin, especially between the toes; thrives in warm, damp shoes. Ingrown toenails — the nail edge digs into the skin, causing pain and sometimes infection. Ankle sprains — stretched or torn ligaments from a twist; the most common sports injury of the foot. Corns and calluses — thickened skin from pressure and friction.
*Diabetes-related foot problems (important):* Long-term high blood sugar can damage foot nerves (neuropathy), dulling sensation — so injuries go unfelt — and narrow blood vessels, slowing healing. An unnoticed blister can become an ulcer, then an infection. This chain is preventable, which is why foot care is a core part of diabetes care worldwide.
*Less common but serious:* Stress fractures (tiny cracks from overuse, common in runners) · Achilles tendon rupture (a sudden "pop" at the back of the ankle) · gout (sudden, intensely painful big-toe joint inflammation) · infected foot ulcers in diabetes, which need urgent treatment.

**Which doctors get involved.** A podiatrist is the foot specialist — handling nail problems, skin conditions, orthotics, and routine diabetic foot screening. An orthopedic foot & ankle surgeon operates on bunions, fractures, tendon ruptures, and severe deformities. Your primary care doctor manages sprains and refers onward. If you have diabetes, your diabetes care team (doctor, nurse, dietitian) should check your feet at regular visits — ask if they don't.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* looking at alignment, pressing tender spots, watching how you walk. *Imaging:* X-ray (fractures, bunions, arthritis) · ultrasound (tendons, plantar fascia) · MRI (stress fractures, complex injuries). *Diabetes foot checks:* the monofilament test — a thin fiber pressed on the sole to check sensation — plus pulse checks for circulation; quick, painless, and done in clinic.

**Keeping them healthy.** Wear shoes that fit and support — enough room for toes, cushioning under the heel, and replace them when the tread and support wear out. Keep feet clean and dry, and change socks after heavy sweating, to discourage fungal infection. Trim toenails straight across rather than curved, to prevent ingrowns. Build up walking or running distances gradually, not in sudden jumps. If you have diabetes: inspect both feet daily (soles included — use a mirror), never walk barefoot even indoors, moisturize dry skin but not between the toes, and have a professional trim difficult nails.
*Myth box:* "Foot pain is just part of getting older." False — and a costly belief, because it stops people seeking help. Most foot pain has an identifiable, treatable cause at any age, from footwear changes and exercises to minor procedures.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: heel or arch pain lasting more than a couple of weeks; a bunion that's enlarging or increasingly painful; any blister, cut, or sore on the foot that isn't healing (urgent if you have diabetes); recurring ingrown toenails; numbness or tingling in the feet. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: sudden inability to bear weight after a twist, fall, or "pop" (possible fracture or tendon rupture); any foot wound in a person with diabetes with spreading redness, swelling, warmth, or fever; a foot that turns pale, cold, or numb after injury (possible circulation problem). These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor, urgently in the emergency cases.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → physical exam and gait observation → X-ray for bone questions, ultrasound or MRI for soft tissue → diagnosis. Many foot conditions (plantar fasciitis, bunions, athlete's foot) are diagnosed clinically on the spot. For diabetes, diagnosis is about screening: regular foot exams catch nerve and circulation changes before injuries happen.

**Common treatment approaches.** Rest, ice, and activity adjustment calm most overuse injuries. Supportive footwear, insoles, or custom orthotics redistribute pressure. Stretching and physiotherapy help plantar fasciitis and Achilles problems. Medication classes: antifungals for athlete's foot; anti-inflammatory drugs for pain; antibiotics for infected ingrown nails or ulcers. Minor procedures: ingrown nail edge removal, corn paring. Surgery is reserved for cases that don't respond — bunion correction, Achilles repair, fracture fixation. For diabetic foot ulcers: careful wound care, offloading (keeping pressure off the wound, sometimes with special boots or casts), antibiotics for infection, and procedures to restore circulation when needed.
