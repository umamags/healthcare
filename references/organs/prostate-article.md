# Prostate — full article

**What it does.** The prostate is a small gland, about the size and shape of a walnut, found only in men. It sits just below the bladder and wraps around the urethra — the tube that carries urine out of the body. Its job is to make seminal fluid, the liquid part of semen, which nourishes sperm and helps carry them during ejaculation. Muscles in the prostate help push this fluid out. Because the gland surrounds the urine tube like a ring around a straw, anything that makes it swell or grow tends to squeeze the tube — which is why prostate problems so often show up as urinary symptoms. The prostate grows slowly throughout adult life, and speeds up again from middle age onward.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Benign prostatic enlargement (BPH) — non-cancerous growth of the gland with age; very common in older men. It is not cancer, and it does not turn into cancer, though the two can exist at the same time. It causes a weak stream, difficulty starting, dribbling, and frequent trips to the toilet, especially at night. Prostatitis — inflammation of the prostate, sometimes caused by bacterial infection; it can cause pelvic or perineal pain, pain when passing urine, and sometimes fever. Some types come and go over months.
*Less common but serious:* Prostate cancer — one of the most common cancers in men. It often grows slowly and may cause no symptoms early on, which is why testing decisions are individual (see below). Advanced disease can spread, most often to bones.

**Which doctors get involved.** A urologist specialises in the prostate and urinary system and handles most prostate diagnosis and treatment. An oncologist leads cancer care when prostate cancer is advanced or needs drug or hormone treatment. Your primary care doctor usually manages early urinary symptoms and the testing conversation, and refers you onward.

**Usual tests.** *Review and exam:* a symptom review (how much the urinary symptoms bother you) and a digital rectal exam — a gloved finger in the back passage to feel the size and surface of the prostate. *Lab tests:* urine tests to rule out infection; the PSA blood test. PSA is a protein the prostate makes; levels rise with enlargement, infection, and age as well as cancer — so PSA is not a cancer test by itself, and whether to test at all is a decision to discuss with your doctor, weighing your age, family history, and preferences. *Imaging:* ultrasound (sometimes through the rectum) to measure the gland; prostate MRI for a detailed picture when cancer is a concern. *Procedures:* biopsy — small tissue samples, usually guided by MRI or ultrasound — is how prostate cancer is confirmed.

**Keeping it healthy.** No habit is proven to prevent prostate problems, but staying active, keeping a healthy weight, and not smoking support urinary and general health. Don't quietly put up with worsening urinary symptoms — effective treatments exist, and symptoms deserve a check to rule out other causes. If close relatives had prostate cancer, tell your doctor; family history matters in testing decisions.
*Myth box:* "A high PSA means you have cancer." No. PSA goes up with benign enlargement, prostatitis, a urinary infection, even recent ejaculation or a long bike ride. Many men with a raised PSA do not have cancer, and some cancers occur with a normal PSA. A raised result usually means more checks — repeat testing, MRI, or biopsy — not a diagnosis. (And the flip side: BPH does not turn into cancer; the two are separate conditions.)

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: a weakening stream, straining or difficulty starting, needing to pass urine much more often at night, blood in urine or semen, or persistent pain in the pelvis or perineum (the area between the scrotum and back passage). These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: complete inability to pass urine (painful and needs urgent draining), or high fever with prostate or pelvic pain and feeling very unwell — a possible severe prostate infection.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → urine test and rectal exam → PSA blood test if appropriate → ultrasound or MRI if needed → biopsy only when cancer is suspected → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (can't pass urine or severe infection):* straight to the emergency department → a catheter to drain the bladder, blood and urine tests, and antibiotics if infection is the cause.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the condition and how much symptoms bother you. For BPH: watchful waiting for mild symptoms; medication classes such as alpha-blockers (relax the prostate and bladder neck) and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (slowly shrink the gland); procedures or surgery that remove or widen the blocked tissue when medicines aren't enough. For prostatitis: antibiotics for bacterial types, pain relievers, and time for chronic types. For prostate cancer: options range from active surveillance (close monitoring of slow-growing cancers) to surgery, radiotherapy, and hormone-blocking therapy — chosen by cancer stage, PSA, and personal preference.
