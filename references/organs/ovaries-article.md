# Ovaries — full article

**What they do.** The ovaries are two almond-sized glands that sit on either side of the uterus, connected to it by the fallopian tubes. They have two jobs. First, eggs: a girl is born with all the eggs she will ever have, stored in tiny sacs called follicles. From puberty, roughly one egg matures and is released each month — this is ovulation — and travels down the tube toward the uterus. Second, hormones: the ovaries make estrogen and progesterone, the hormones that drive the menstrual cycle, prepare the womb for pregnancy, and also influence bones, skin, mood, and the heart. At menopause (usually in the late 40s or early 50s) the egg supply runs low, hormone levels fall, and periods stop.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Functional cysts — fluid-filled sacs that form as part of normal ovulation; most disappear on their own within a few cycles and cause no symptoms. PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome) — a hormone imbalance causing irregular periods, excess hair or acne, and difficulty conceiving; despite the name, the "cysts" are actually many small immature follicles. Endometriomas — cysts filled with old blood, caused by endometriosis growing on the ovary; they can cause chronic pelvic pain.
*Less common but serious:* Ovarian torsion — the ovary twists on its blood supply, cutting off circulation; intensely painful and a surgical emergency. Premature ovarian insufficiency — the ovaries stop working well before age 40, causing early menopause symptoms and infertility. Ovarian cancer — often silent in its early stages; when symptoms do appear they are vague (see warning signs), which is why it is frequently found late.

**Which doctors get involved.** A gynaecologist is the main specialist for ovarian cysts, pain, and surgery. A reproductive endocrinologist (fertility specialist) handles PCOS, premature ovarian insufficiency, and conception problems. A gynaecologic oncologist treats ovarian cancer, usually with a combination of surgery and chemotherapy. Your primary care doctor often spots the first clues and arranges the ultrasound.

**Usual tests.** *Imaging:* pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound — the key test; it shows cyst size, type, and blood flow; CT or MRI if more detail is needed. *Blood tests:* hormone levels (for PCOS or early menopause), pregnancy test, and tumour markers such as CA-125. *Procedures:* laparoscopy (keyhole surgery) to look at the ovaries directly, treat cysts, or take tissue samples. An honest caveat about CA-125: it is a marker, not a screening test (see the myth box below).

**Keeping them healthy.** No habit can guarantee healthy ovaries, but these help: keep a healthy weight and stay active (this genuinely improves PCOS hormone balance), don't smoke (smoking brings menopause earlier), and keep routine gynaecology appointments. Know your family history: ovarian cancer and breast cancer can share inherited gene changes (such as BRCA1/BRCA2), and strong family history is worth discussing with a doctor, who may suggest genetic counselling. Most importantly, because there is no reliable screening test, know the symptom pattern below and act on it if it persists.
*Myth box:* "A CA-125 blood test can screen for ovarian cancer." False. CA-125 rises in many harmless conditions — endometriosis, fibroids, infection, even during a period — and it can be normal in early ovarian cancer. Doctors use it as one clue among several, never as a stand-alone screen.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon if these persist most days for a few weeks: bloating, feeling full quickly or losing appetite, pelvic or abdominal pain, needing to urinate urgently or often. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they are common and usually have harmless causes — but persistent ones mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: sudden, severe one-sided pelvic pain, especially with nausea, vomiting, or feeling faint — this can be a twisted ovary (torsion) or a ruptured cyst, and a twisted ovary needs surgery within hours to save it.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → pelvic exam → ultrasound → blood tests (including CA-125 where relevant) → CT/MRI or laparoscopy if the picture is unclear or surgery is planned → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (sudden severe pain):* straight to the emergency department → urgent ultrasound to check blood flow to the ovary → emergency keyhole surgery if torsion is suspected.

**Common treatment approaches.** Many functional cysts need no treatment at all — just a follow-up scan ("watch and wait"). Medication classes doctors commonly consider: pain relievers, hormonal contraceptives (sometimes used to prevent new cysts forming), hormone-balancing medicines and fertility medicines for PCOS, and hormone replacement therapy for premature ovarian insufficiency. Surgery: keyhole removal of cysts (cystectomy) or, when necessary, of an ovary; emergency surgery to untwist a torted ovary. For ovarian cancer, treatment usually combines surgery with chemotherapy, planned by a gynaecologic oncologist.
