# Hands

**What they do.** Let's be honest up front: the hand isn't a single organ. Each hand packs 27 bones, a network of joints, rope-like tendons that connect to muscles in the forearm, and three major nerves — into a structure small enough to fit in a glove, yet capable of both crushing grip and threading a needle. Tendons glide through lubricated tunnels; the median nerve passes through a narrow passage at the wrist called the carpal tunnel. This compact engineering is why hands are both marvelously capable and vulnerable to overuse, compression, and injury.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Carpal tunnel syndrome — the median nerve gets squeezed inside the carpal tunnel, causing numbness and tingling in the thumb, index, and middle fingers, often worse at night; gripping weakens over time. Osteoarthritis at the base of the thumb — wear-and-tear where the thumb meets the wrist, aching during pinching and gripping. Trigger finger — a finger tendon thickens or its tunnel narrows, so the finger catches or locks when bent, then snaps straight. Tendon injuries — cuts or sudden overload can fray or snap tendons. Fractures and sprains — from falls, sports, or jams (the "boxer's fracture" of the hand bone below the little finger is a classic).
*Less common but serious:* Rheumatoid arthritis — the immune system attacks the small joints of the hands symmetrically; needs different treatment from osteoarthritis. Deep infections — even small cuts and human bites can seed serious hand infections. Dupuytren's contracture — thickened palm tissue slowly pulls fingers into a bent position.

**Which doctors get involved.** An orthopedic hand surgeon treats fractures, tendon injuries, and performs operations like carpal tunnel release. A rheumatologist manages rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory joint diseases — see them when multiple joints are swollen without injury. A hand therapist or physiotherapist designs splints and rehabilitation exercises that are often as important as any procedure. A neurologist sometimes helps with nerve problems, and your primary care doctor is the usual starting point.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* range of motion, grip strength, and provocative tests (tapping the wrist or holding it bent to reproduce carpal tunnel tingling). *Imaging:* X-ray (fractures, arthritis) · ultrasound (tendons gliding in real time) · MRI (complex injuries, hidden fractures like the scaphoid). *Nerve tests:* nerve conduction study, which measures how well signals travel through the median nerve — the key confirmatory test for carpal tunnel syndrome.

**Keeping them healthy.** Break up repetitive work — alternate gripping, typing, and resting; no single posture for hours. Keep wrists neutral (straight) rather than bent up or down during long tasks, and use ergonomic tools where you can. Build grip strength gradually with squeezes and stretches rather than sudden heavy loading. Protect your hands deliberately: work gloves for manual jobs, guards for sports, and never catch a falling heavy object with an open hand if you can avoid it.
*Myth box:* "Carpal tunnel syndrome is caused by typing." Typing is one possible contributor among many — wrist anatomy, pregnancy, diabetes, thyroid problems, and arthritis all play roles, and many cases have no single identifiable cause. Blaming the keyboard alone misses the bigger picture (and the fix is rarely just a new keyboard).

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: a finger that catches, clicks, or locks; persistent numbness or tingling in the fingers; hand or wrist pain and stiffness lasting weeks; numbness that wakes you at night (classic carpal tunnel). Emergency — call your local emergency number now: a deep cut with numbness beyond it, or a finger you suddenly cannot bend or straighten (a cut tendon or nerve needs repair within days, and sooner is better); a finger or hand that looks deformed after a jam, fall, or blow; bleeding that doesn't stop with firm, sustained pressure; rapidly increasing pain, swelling, or redness after a bite or puncture wound. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor, urgently in the emergency cases.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → physical exam with specific hand tests → often a diagnosis right there, since many hand conditions are clinical → X-ray if fracture or arthritis is suspected → nerve conduction study for carpal tunnel, ultrasound or MRI for tendon questions. Hand problems are among the most "hands-on" diagnoses in medicine — the exam itself does most of the work.

**Common treatment approaches.** Activity modification and rest come first for overuse problems. Splinting — especially a night wrist splint for carpal tunnel — is a simple approach doctors commonly recommend. Hand therapy: targeted exercises, and custom splints. Medication classes: anti-inflammatory drugs for pain and swelling; corticosteroid injections into the carpal tunnel, a trigger finger's tendon sheath, or an arthritic joint. Surgery, when needed: carpal tunnel release (cutting the ligament roof to free the nerve), trigger finger release, fracture fixation with pins or plates, and tendon repair. Most hand surgery is day surgery, but the rehabilitation afterward determines the result.
