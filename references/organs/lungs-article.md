### Full article: Lungs

**What they do.** Each breath pulls air down the windpipe into branching airways that end in hundreds of millions of tiny air sacs — alveoli. Their combined surface area is enormous, letting oxygen pass into the blood while carbon dioxide passes out to be exhaled. The diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs, powers most of breathing. You take tens of thousands of breaths a day without thinking about it — until something goes wrong, when every breath reminds you.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Asthma — airways narrow and inflame, often triggered by allergies, exercise, or colds; typically starts in childhood but can appear at any age, and symptoms come and go. COPD — long-term damage to airways and air sacs, most often from years of smoking or indoor smoke (such as cooking-fire smoke); breathlessness that slowly worsens. Pneumonia — infection that fills air sacs with fluid or pus; can be bacterial, viral, or fungal. Bronchitis — inflammation of the airways, usually with a persistent cough.
Tuberculosis (TB) — a bacterial infection spread through the air when someone with active TB coughs. It usually affects the lungs and is curable — but only with several months of antibiotics taken exactly as prescribed; stopping early breeds drug-resistant TB. TB remains one of the world's biggest infectious killers, and a cough lasting more than 2–3 weeks, especially with weight loss, night sweats, or fever, should always be checked.
*Less common but serious:* Lung cancer — strongly linked to smoking but also occurs in non-smokers (air pollution, radon, secondhand smoke, and workplace dusts contribute). Pulmonary embolism — a blood clot lodging in the lungs; an emergency.

**Which doctors get involved.** A pulmonologist (respiratory specialist) diagnoses and manages lung diseases. A thoracic surgeon operates on the lungs. An infectious disease specialist guides TB and complex pneumonia treatment. An allergist helps with allergic asthma. Your primary care doctor often spots problems first and manages mild asthma.

**Usual tests.** *Bedside:* pulse oximetry (a fingertip clip measuring blood oxygen), listening to the chest. *Function:* spirometry — you blow hard into a machine; the key test for asthma and COPD. *Imaging:* chest X-ray (first look), CT scan (detailed view). *Lab:* sputum test (checking coughed-up mucus for TB or other infections), blood tests. *Procedures:* bronchoscopy — a thin camera passed into the airways to look around or take samples.

**Keeping them healthy.** Don't smoke or vape, and avoid secondhand smoke — the single biggest favour for your lungs. Where you can, limit time in heavily polluted or dusty air (masks help on bad-air days). Exercise regularly; it strengthens breathing muscles even though it doesn't change lung size. Get flu and pneumonia vaccines where they're offered — respiratory infections hit damaged lungs hardest. If you're ever treated for TB, finishing every dose of the months-long course protects both you and your community from drug-resistant strains.
*Myth box:* "Only smokers get lung cancer." False — a meaningful share of lung cancers occur in people who never smoked. Air pollution, radon gas, secondhand smoke, and workplace dusts all contribute. Non-smokers with persistent lung symptoms deserve the same prompt check-up.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: a cough lasting more than 3 weeks; wheezing or a whistling sound when breathing; coughing up small streaks of blood; a cough with unexplained weight loss, night sweats, or persistent fever (classic TB flags). Emergency — call your local emergency number now: severe breathlessness, or being unable to finish a sentence; coughing up a lot of blood; bluish lips, face, or nails; sudden sharp chest pain with breathing difficulty (possible pulmonary embolism).

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* symptoms and history (including smoking, exposures, and contacts) → physical exam and pulse oximetry → spirometry and chest X-ray → CT, sputum tests, or bronchoscopy for detail → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (severe breathlessness):* straight to the emergency department → oxygen and monitoring first, tests in parallel. TB diagnosis specifically leans on sputum testing and chest imaging, and close contacts may be tested too.

**Common treatment approaches.** Asthma is managed with inhalers — quick-relief (bronchodilator) inhalers for attacks and daily preventer (usually inhaled corticosteroid) inhalers to keep airways calm — plus avoiding triggers. COPD treatment centres on stopping smoke exposure, bronchodilator inhalers, and pulmonary rehabilitation (supervised exercise and education); oxygen therapy helps advanced cases. Bacterial pneumonia is treated with antibiotics; TB with a combination of antibiotics taken for several months, never stopped early. Lung cancer treatment — surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and newer targeted or immune therapies — is covered in our cancer section.
