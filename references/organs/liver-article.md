### Full article: Liver

**What it does.** The liver is the largest internal organ — about the size of a football — and the body's chemical processing plant, handling around 500 jobs. It filters blood coming from the digestive tract, removing toxins, old blood cells, and the breakdown products of medicines and alcohol. It makes bile, a fluid that helps digest fats. It stores energy as glycogen and releases it between meals. It produces proteins essential for blood clotting and immune function. Remarkably, it's the only internal organ that can regrow: even after losing a large portion, it can grow back — though repeated injury eventually overwhelms this ability.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Fatty liver disease — fat builds up in liver cells, closely tied to excess weight, diabetes, and high cholesterol. Note: in 2023 the medical community renamed it from NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) to MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) — you'll see both names in articles and on test reports for a while. Hepatitis — liver inflammation, most often from viruses: hepatitis A (usually spread through contaminated food or water; typically resolves on its own), hepatitis B and C (spread through blood and body fluids; can become chronic and silently damage the liver over decades).
*Less common but serious:* Alcohol-related liver disease — ranges from fatty liver to severe inflammation and scarring; it can affect anyone who drinks heavily over time. This is a medical condition, not a moral failing, and effective help exists — no shame in seeking it. Cirrhosis — permanent scarring after years of injury from any cause; the liver stiffens and struggles to work. Liver cancer — often develops in livers already damaged by cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B.

**Which doctors get involved.** A hepatologist specialises in the liver. A gastroenterologist covers the liver plus the digestive tract and often manages liver disease too. An infectious disease specialist guides hepatitis treatment. A transplant surgeon becomes involved when the liver is failing and transplantation is considered.

**Usual tests.** *Blood:* liver function tests (enzymes and proteins that signal liver stress), hepatitis A/B/C blood tests. *Imaging:* abdominal ultrasound (first look at size, fat, and lumps), elastography — sometimes called FibroScan — which measures liver stiffness without a needle, CT or MRI for detail. *Procedure:* liver biopsy — a small tissue sample, usually via a needle — when the diagnosis is still unclear.

**Keeping it healthy.** Limit alcohol — and if you have any liver condition, avoid it entirely (ask your doctor; no judgment, just medicine). Keep a healthy weight through balanced eating and activity; fatty liver often improves with even modest weight loss. Get vaccinated against hepatitis A and B where available. Never share needles, razors, or toothbrushes — hepatitis B and C travel in blood. Tell your doctor about all medicines and supplements you take; "natural" doesn't mean liver-safe.
*Myth box:* "Liver detoxes and cleanses flush toxins from your liver." False — the liver is the detox organ; it detoxifies itself around the clock. No juice, tea, or supplement has been shown to "cleanse" it, and some "detox" products have themselves caused liver injury. The best liver cleanse is the habits above.

**Warning signs.** Liver disease is often silent until advanced — another reason risk factors (weight, alcohol, hepatitis exposure) matter more than waiting for symptoms. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (jaundice); dark urine with pale stools; persistent, unexplained fatigue; swelling of the belly or ankles; itchy skin with no visible rash. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: vomiting blood; black, tarry stools (digested blood); sudden confusion, personality change, or extreme drowsiness in someone with known liver disease; severe abdominal pain with fever.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms (if any) and history — including alcohol, medicines, weight, and exposures → physical exam → liver blood tests → ultrasound → elastography to gauge scarring → biopsy or advanced imaging if the picture is unclear → diagnosis and a management plan. Because early liver disease rarely hurts, it's often found through blood tests done for other reasons.

**Common treatment approaches.** Fatty liver (MASLD) is managed mainly with sustained weight loss, activity, and control of diabetes and cholesterol — no magic pill. Alcohol-related disease starts with stopping alcohol, with medical support for withdrawal if needed. Hepatitis B is controlled with antiviral medicines; hepatitis C can now usually be cured with a course of antiviral tablets. Cirrhosis complications are managed medically — for example, medicines that reduce fluid build-up — while the underlying cause is treated. When the liver fails despite treatment, liver transplantation is the option doctors consider; it's major surgery with lifelong follow-up.
