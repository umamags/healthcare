# Vagina — full article

**What it does.** The vagina is a muscular, elastic passage, roughly 7–10 cm long, that connects the uterus (womb) to the outside of the body. It has three everyday jobs: menstrual blood flows out through it each month; it receives the penis during vaginal sex; and during childbirth it stretches remarkably to become the birth canal. Its most underappreciated feature is that it mostly looks after itself. The walls produce natural moisture, and colonies of helpful bacteria (mainly lactobacilli) keep the environment mildly acidic, which discourages harmful germs. A small amount of clear or whitish discharge is normal — it's the vagina's own cleaning system at work — and it changes naturally across the menstrual cycle.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Yeast infection (thrush) — an overgrowth of a fungus normally present in small amounts; causes itching, soreness, and thick white discharge. Bacterial vaginosis (BV) — the helpful bacteria are outnumbered by other bacteria; the main sign is a thin discharge with a fishy smell, often with no itching. Vaginal dryness — falling oestrogen around and after menopause thins and dries the tissues (sometimes called vaginal atrophy), which can make sex uncomfortable; dryness can also occur while breastfeeding or with some medicines.
*Less common but serious:* Sexually transmitted infections — trichomoniasis, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and herpes can all affect the vagina, sometimes with no symptoms at all. Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — infection spreading upward to the uterus and tubes; can cause lasting damage if untreated. Pelvic organ prolapse — weakened pelvic floor muscles let the bladder, uterus, or bowel bulge into the vaginal wall; more common after childbirth and menopause.

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor handles most vaginal symptoms first and can test and treat common infections. A gynaecologist takes over for persistent, recurrent, or structural problems such as prolapse. A pelvic floor physiotherapist supports prolapse and pelvic pain with exercises and rehabilitation — a support role rather than a diagnosing one.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* pelvic (internal) exam, looking at the vagina and cervix and checking discharge. *Lab tests:* vaginal swabs (examined for yeast, BV, and trichomoniasis), STI tests (swab or urine, for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and others), vaginal pH measurement (a quick acidity check that helps separate BV from thrush). Blood tests are used for infections such as HIV or syphilis when relevant.

**Keeping it healthy.** Leave the inside alone — no douching, no internal washes. Wash only the outside (the vulva) with water or a mild, unscented cleanser. Use condoms with new partners to lower STI risk, and attend STI testing after unprotected sex. Keep up cervical screening (smear/Pap tests or HPV tests) as offered by your local programme — it examines the cervix at the top of the vagina and prevents cervical cancer.
*Myth box:* "The vagina needs douching or 'feminine hygiene' washes to stay clean and fresh." False. The vagina is self-cleaning. Douching washes away the protective bacteria, disturbs the natural acidity, and is linked to *higher* rates of bacterial vaginosis, pelvic infection, and difficulty getting pregnant. Any mild natural odour is normal; a strong or fishy smell is a symptom to get checked, not something to mask.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: discharge that changes in colour, amount, consistency, or smell; itching, burning, or soreness; pain during sex; bleeding after sex or between periods; sores or blisters. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking through protection quickly, or any heavy bleeding in pregnancy); severe pelvic pain with fever, which can signal pelvic inflammatory disease; or high fever with a rash, dizziness, or fainting while using a tampon — possible toxic shock syndrome, a rare but dangerous reaction. Remove the tampon and get emergency help at once.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including sexual history and contraception) → pelvic exam → swabs and a pH check → STI tests if there's any risk → diagnosis and treatment. Most common vaginal infections are identified the same day; swab results may take a few days. If symptoms keep coming back, the doctor looks for an underlying cause such as diabetes, a skin condition, or a partner who also needs treatment.

**Common treatment approaches.** Antifungal medicines for thrush (creams, vaginal tablets, or oral tablets) · antibiotics for bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, and bacterial STIs (partners are treated too for STIs) · antiviral medicines for herpes outbreaks · vaginal moisturisers and local oestrogen for menopause-related dryness · pelvic floor physiotherapy, support pessaries, or surgery for significant prolapse. Because thrush, BV, and STIs can look alike but need different treatment, doctors commonly consider a test before treating when the picture isn't clear.
