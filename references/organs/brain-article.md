# Brain — full article

**What it does.** The brain is the body's control center: a roughly 1.3 kg organ that runs everything from conscious thought and memory to movement, the senses, and automatic jobs like breathing and heartbeat. Three main parts share the work: the cerebrum (thinking, memory, voluntary movement), the cerebellum (balance and coordination), and the brainstem (breathing, heart rate, blood pressure — the functions you never think about). Tens of billions of neurons communicate through electrical and chemical signals. The brain is protected by the skull, layered membranes called meninges, and a "blood-brain barrier" that filters what passes from blood into brain tissue. Despite its size, it uses about a fifth of the body's energy.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Stroke — blood supply to part of the brain is cut off, either by a clot (ischemic stroke) or a bleed (hemorrhagic stroke); a medical emergency where minutes matter. Migraine — severe, often one-sided headache with nausea and sensitivity to light, sometimes preceded by visual "aura." Epilepsy — recurrent seizures caused by abnormal electrical activity. Dementia — progressive decline in memory and thinking that interferes with daily life; Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause and mainly affects older adults. Parkinson's disease — tremor, stiffness, and slowed movement linked to loss of dopamine-producing cells.
*Less common but serious:* Brain tumours — growths that can be benign or cancerous (see our cancer section). Traumatic brain injury — from falls, road accidents, or sports; ranges from concussion to severe injury. Meningitis/encephalitis — infection or inflammation of the brain's coverings or tissue; fever with stiff neck and confusion is an emergency.
*A note on mental health:* depression, anxiety, and similar conditions involve the brain, but they are diagnosed and treated mainly by psychiatrists and psychologists — a different specialty from neurology, though the two often collaborate.

**Which doctors get involved.** A neurologist diagnoses and medically treats brain and nervous-system disorders. A neurosurgeon operates — on tumours, bleeds, and severe injuries. A psychiatrist handles mental-health conditions. After events like stroke, rehabilitation specialists (physiotherapists, speech therapists, occupational therapists) help recovery.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* neurological exam — reflexes, strength, coordination, speech, and memory. *Imaging:* CT scan (fast; good at showing bleeds — usually first in suspected stroke), MRI (detailed images of tissue, tumours, and small strokes). *Electrical:* EEG — records the brain's electrical activity, mainly for epilepsy. *Fluid:* lumbar puncture (spinal tap) — analyzes cerebrospinal fluid for infection or inflammation. *Blood:* tests to rule out conditions that mimic neurological disease.

**Keeping it healthy.** What's good for the heart is good for the brain: regular exercise, and controlled blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar (all major stroke and dementia risk factors). Don't smoke; limit alcohol. Protect your head — helmets for cycling and contact sports, seatbelts always. Stay mentally engaged and socially connected. Get hearing loss treated — midlife hearing loss is linked to higher dementia risk. Sleep 7–9 hours.
*Myth box:* "We only use 10% of our brain." False — brain scans show virtually all of it is active over the course of a day. Different regions just do different jobs.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: headaches that are new, frequent, or changing in pattern; memory problems interfering with daily life; tremor, stiffness, or noticeably slowed movement; a first seizure, even if you feel fine afterwards. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: **FAST** — Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call. Also sudden "worst headache of my life," a seizure lasting more than ~5 minutes or repeated seizures, head injury with confusion or vomiting, or sudden confusion.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* symptoms and history (often with input from family) → neurological exam → CT or MRI → EEG or lumbar puncture if indicated → diagnosis and plan. *Emergency path (suspected stroke):* straight to the emergency department → immediate CT scan → clot-busting treatment or clot removal within a tight time window. Specialists say "time is brain" — delays cost brain tissue.

**Common treatment approaches.** Stroke: clot-busting drugs, mechanical clot removal, then rehabilitation. Epilepsy: anti-seizure medications; surgery in selected cases. Parkinson's: medications that replace or mimic dopamine; deep brain stimulation in selected cases. Migraine: acute relief plus preventive medications and trigger management. Dementia: medications that may ease symptoms (there is no cure for Alzheimer's yet), plus practical support and planning. Brain tumours: surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy. Traumatic brain injury: emergency care, monitoring, and rehabilitation (physio, speech, occupational therapy).
