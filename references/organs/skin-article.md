### Full article: Skin

**What it does.** Skin is the body's largest organ — roughly 2 square metres in an adult — and its first line of defence. It keeps germs and harmful substances out, holds water in, and helps control body temperature through sweat and blood flow. It's also a sense organ, packed with nerve endings for touch, pressure, heat, cold, and pain. Skin constantly renews itself: the surface you see today will be replaced over the coming weeks.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Acne — blocked, inflamed oil glands; most common in teenagers but adults get it too. Eczema (atopic dermatitis) — dry, itchy, inflamed patches; often starts in childhood and runs in families with asthma and hay fever. Psoriasis — an immune-driven condition causing thick, scaly patches; not contagious. Fungal infections — ringworm (a ring-shaped rash; despite the name, no worm involved) and athlete's foot; thrive in warm, moist skin. Hives — itchy raised welts, often from allergies.
*Less common but serious:* Skin cancer — including melanoma, the most dangerous type, which can spread if caught late. Pressure ulcers (bedsores) in people who can't move much.

**Which doctors get involved.** A dermatologist diagnoses and treats skin, hair, and nail conditions, medically and with minor procedures. Your primary care doctor handles straightforward rashes and infections. An allergist helps when hives or eczema have allergic triggers. A surgeon removes skin cancers and suspicious moles.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* visual skin check, often with a dermatoscope (a handheld magnifier that reveals subsurface patterns). *Lab:* skin biopsy — a small sample examined under a microscope; the definitive test for suspicious moles. Patch testing — small amounts of possible allergens taped to the back to find contact-allergy triggers. Skin scraping or culture to confirm fungal infections.

**Keeping it healthy.** Sun protection is the single biggest thing: seek shade midday, wear hats and covering clothing, and use sunscreen on exposed skin, reapplied through the day. Moisturise dry skin, especially after washing. Cleanse gently; harsh scrubbing damages the skin barrier. Don't pick or squeeze spots — it drives infection deeper and scars. Check your skin periodically, including your back, scalp, and soles of feet, using the ABCDE rule for moles: Asymmetry, uneven Border, Colour variation, Diameter growing, Evolving over time. Any "yes" deserves a doctor's look.
*Myth box:* "People with darker skin don't need sun protection." False — the risk of skin cancer is lower but very real, and melanomas in darker skin are often found later, when they're harder to treat. That's one more reason to check.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: a mole matching any ABCDE change; a rash that doesn't improve with basic care; persistent itching that disturbs sleep; a sore that won't heal within a few weeks. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: skin redness that spreads rapidly, especially with fever, pain, or swelling (possible cellulitis, a bacterial skin infection that can turn serious fast); signs of a severe allergic reaction — swelling of the lips or tongue, trouble breathing, widespread hives with dizziness or faintness.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → visual exam, often with dermatoscopy → biopsy of anything suspicious → patch testing or cultures when allergy or fungus is suspected → diagnosis and treatment plan. Most skin diagnoses are made by eye; the biopsy is the backstop for anything uncertain.

**Common treatment approaches.** Moisturisers and gentle skin care are the base for eczema and dry skin. Medicated creams are the mainstay for most conditions: corticosteroid creams calm inflammation, antifungal creams clear fungal infections, and vitamin-A-derived creams treat acne. Tablets are used for widespread or severe disease — including antibiotics, antifungals, and immune-calming medicines for psoriasis. Light therapy helps some psoriasis and eczema. Suspicious or cancerous moles are cut out surgically — usually a small procedure under local anaesthetic. Severe allergic reactions are treated as emergencies with injectable adrenaline.
