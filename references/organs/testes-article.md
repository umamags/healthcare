# Testes — full article

**What they do.** The testes (testicles) are two oval organs, each about the size of a small plum, held in the scrotum — the pouch of skin behind the penis. They have two jobs: making sperm, continuously, in tiny coiled tubes inside each testicle; and making testosterone, the main male hormone, which drives puberty, sex drive, muscle and bone strength, and body hair. Sperm production is fussy about temperature: it works best a few degrees cooler than core body temperature, which is why the testes hang outside the body, and why the scrotum tightens or relaxes to adjust their temperature. After sperm are made, they pass into the epididymis to mature (see the sperm ducts page), and testosterone travels into the bloodstream.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Epididymitis and orchitis — inflammation of the sperm duct or the testicle itself, usually from infection (see the sperm ducts page). Varicocele — enlarged veins above the testicle, a bit like a varicose vein; often harmless, sometimes linked to lower fertility. Hydrocele — harmless fluid collecting around the testicle, causing painless swelling (see the scrotum page). Low testosterone — production falls with age or illness and can cause low sex drive, tiredness, and reduced muscle strength.
*Less common but serious:* Testicular torsion — the testicle twists on its cord, cutting off its blood supply; a surgical emergency, commonest in teenagers and young men. Testicular cancer — a lump or swelling in the testicle; unusual among cancers in that it is most common in younger men, roughly teens to forties, and it is highly treatable, especially when caught early. Undescended testicle — a testicle that didn't move down before birth; needs treatment in childhood because it raises later risks of fertility problems and cancer.

**Which doctors get involved.** A urologist deals with most testicle problems, from infections to surgery. An andrologist specialises in male fertility and hormone problems such as low testosterone. A uro-oncologist handles testicular cancer. Your primary care doctor is usually the first stop and refers you onward.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* feeling the testes and scrotum for lumps, swelling, and tenderness. *Imaging:* scrotal Doppler ultrasound — the key test; it shows lumps, fluid, and whether blood is flowing to the testicle. *Lab tests:* urine tests and STI tests when infection is suspected; tumour marker blood tests (AFP, hCG, LDH) when cancer is suspected; a testosterone blood test (taken in the morning) when hormone levels are in question. *Other:* semen analysis, which counts and checks sperm when fertility is a concern.

**Keeping them healthy.** Know what feels normal for you — the shape, size, and usual lumps (the epididymis is a normal soft lump at the back) — and report any change to a doctor. Advice on formal self-exam schedules varies, so think of it as familiarity, not a rigid routine. Wear a protective cup for contact sports. Practise safer sex to lower the risk of infections that can damage the testes. There is no diet or supplement proven to protect the testes.
*Myth box:* "Testicular cancer is an older man's disease." False — it is actually most common in younger men, from the teens through the thirties and forties. A painless lump at any age deserves a check, and younger men shouldn't assume they're too young.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: a new lump or swelling, one testicle getting bigger, firmer, or changing shape, a dull ache or feeling of heaviness in the scrotum. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: sudden severe pain in a testicle, especially with swelling, nausea, or vomiting. Treat it as torsion until proven otherwise — a twisted testicle can be saved if untwisted within hours, but can be lost if you wait overnight.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → physical exam → scrotal Doppler ultrasound → urine, STI, or blood tests as indicated → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (sudden severe pain):* straight to the emergency department → rapid exam and ultrasound → if torsion is confirmed or strongly suspected, surgery right away, because blood flow matters more than completing every test first.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the cause. Medication classes: antibiotics for bacterial infections, pain relievers and anti-inflammatory medicines, scrotal support and rest for inflammation. Procedures and surgery: emergency surgery to untwist a torsioned testicle (and secure both sides), surgery to remove a testicle when cancer is suspected (followed, if needed, by chemotherapy or radiotherapy), repair of a varicocele or hydrocele in some cases. For low testosterone, doctors look for a cause first; testosterone replacement is sometimes used, under supervision with regular blood tests.
