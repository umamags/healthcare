# Bones (the skeletal system)

**What they do.** Your 206 bones form the skeleton: the frame that holds your body upright, the armor that protects your brain, heart, and lungs, and the anchor points your muscles pull against to create movement. Inside many bones, marrow manufactures red blood cells. Bones are living tissue, not dry scaffolding — they constantly dissolve and rebuild themselves in a process called remodeling, and they store minerals like calcium that the body draws on when needed.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Fractures — a bone cracks or breaks, from a single impact (a fall) or repeated stress (stress fractures in runners). Osteoporosis — bones gradually lose density and become fragile; most common in older adults and in women after menopause. Osteoarthritis — the cartilage cushioning a joint wears down, causing pain and stiffness, typically in knees, hips, and hands.
*Less common but serious:* Rheumatoid arthritis — different from osteoarthritis: here the immune system attacks the joints themselves, often symmetrically (both hands, both wrists), and it needs different treatment. Bone infection (osteomyelitis) — bacteria reach bone through the bloodstream or a nearby wound. Bone cancer — rare; see our cancer section.

**Which doctors get involved.** An orthopedic doctor/surgeon handles fractures, sports injuries, and joint replacements. A rheumatologist manages rheumatoid arthritis and other autoimmune joint diseases — see them rather than an orthopedist when joints are inflamed without injury. Your primary care doctor coordinates osteoporosis screening and referrals. A physiotherapist guides rehabilitation after injury or surgery.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* checking movement, swelling, and tenderness. *Imaging:* X-ray (fractures, arthritis changes) · DEXA scan (measures bone density; the standard osteoporosis test) · MRI (stress fractures, infection, soft tissue around bone) · CT scan (complex fractures). *Blood:* calcium, vitamin D, and inflammation markers.

**Keeping them healthy.** Do weight-bearing exercise — walking, dancing, climbing stairs, light resistance work all signal bones to stay strong. Get enough calcium (dairy, leafy greens, fortified foods) and vitamin D (sensible sun exposure, fatty fish). Don't smoke — it weakens bones. Limit alcohol. Keep a healthy weight: extra weight stresses joints, while being underweight raises fracture risk. Prevent falls, the top cause of fractures in older adults: good lighting, handrails, sturdy shoes, and vision checks.
*Myth box:* "Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis." No good evidence supports this — studies following habitual knuckle-crackers found no higher arthritis risk. (It can annoy the people around you, though.)

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: joint pain, swelling, or morning stiffness lasting more than a few weeks; gradual loss of height or a stooped posture; back pain that doesn't ease with rest. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: a limb that looks deformed or out of place after an injury; bone poking through skin; inability to bear weight or move a limb after trauma; a hand or foot that turns pale, cold, or numb below an injury (possible circulation damage). These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor, urgently in the emergency cases.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* symptoms and history → physical exam of the joint or limb → X-ray → DEXA scan if osteoporosis is suspected, or MRI for what X-rays can't show → diagnosis and plan. *Emergency path (injury):* immobilize the limb where it is → emergency department → X-ray → splint, cast, or surgery depending on the break. Don't try to straighten a deformed limb yourself.

**Common treatment approaches.** Fractures: immobilization in a cast or splint for simple breaks; surgery with plates, screws, or rods for complex or displaced ones; then physiotherapy to restore strength. Osteoporosis: calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and bone-strengthening medication classes (such as bisphosphonates) that doctors commonly consider for higher-risk patients. Osteoarthritis: activity modification, physiotherapy, pain-relief and anti-inflammatory medication classes, and joint replacement surgery when damage is severe. Rheumatoid arthritis: disease-modifying drugs that calm the immune attack — early treatment matters greatly. Bone infection: prolonged antibiotics, sometimes surgery to remove infected tissue.
