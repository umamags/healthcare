# Fallopian tubes — full article

**What they do.** The fallopian tubes are two narrow tubes, each about 10 cm long, running from the top of the uterus out toward the ovaries. They are not attached directly to the ovaries — instead, each tube ends in delicate finger-like fringes (fimbriae) that hover over the ovary and sweep a newly released egg into the tube. Inside, tiny hair-like structures (cilia) and gentle muscle contractions move the egg toward the uterus over several days. Fertilisation — the moment a sperm meets the egg — usually happens inside the tube, and the fertilised egg then continues into the uterus to implant. So the tubes are both a meeting place and a transport route: if they are blocked or damaged, egg and sperm may never meet, or a pregnancy may implant in the wrong place.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Blockage — scarred or closed tubes, usually from past infection or endometriosis; a major cause of infertility, often with no symptoms at all. Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — infection spreading from the vagina/cervix up into the tubes (salpingitis) and nearby organs, causing pelvic pain and fever; the scarring it leaves behind is the main threat to fertility. Hydrosalpinx — a blocked tube that fills with fluid and swells; it can reduce fertility and lower IVF success.
*Less common but serious:* Ectopic pregnancy — a fertilised egg implants in the tube instead of the uterus. The tube cannot stretch, so as the pregnancy grows it can rupture and cause dangerous internal bleeding; this is a life-threatening emergency. Tubal cancer — genuinely rare; most "tubal" cancers are now thought to start in the tube's fringe end and spread to the ovary.

**Which doctors get involved.** A gynaecologist investigates tubal infection, pain, and ectopic pregnancy, and performs tubal surgery. A fertility specialist (reproductive endocrinologist) leads the workup when blocked tubes are part of difficulty conceiving, and advises on IVF — which bypasses the tubes entirely. Your primary care doctor or a sexual-health clinic usually handles STI testing and early PID treatment.

**Usual tests.** *Lab tests:* pregnancy test (hCG) — the essential first test whenever ectopic pregnancy is possible; STI swabs and blood tests. *Imaging:* pelvic/transvaginal ultrasound (first-line for pain or suspected ectopic); HSG (hysterosalpingography) — a dye is passed through the cervix and X-rays show whether it flows through the tubes, proving they are open. *Procedures:* laparoscopy (keyhole surgery) — the most direct way to see the tubes, sometimes with dye testing in the same operation.

**Keeping them healthy.** Almost everything here is about preventing infection. Use condoms with new or non-exclusive partners, test regularly for sexually transmitted infections, and make sure partners are treated too. Chlamydia — the commonest culprit — frequently causes no symptoms at all while quietly scarring the tubes, so testing matters even when you feel fine. Seek care promptly for pelvic pain, unusual discharge, or fever: PID treated early does far less damage than PID treated late. After any pelvic infection, attend follow-up checks.
*Myth box:* "You'd always know if you had chlamydia." False. Most people with chlamydia have no symptoms, yet untreated infection is a leading cause of blocked tubes and infertility. A simple test — not symptoms — is how you know.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: ongoing pelvic pain, pain during sex, unusual discharge, fever with pelvic pain, or trying to conceive for a year without success (six months if you are over 35). These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: a missed period or positive pregnancy test together with one-sided pelvic pain, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness, or fainting. That combination can mean an ectopic pregnancy, which can rupture and bleed internally.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → pregnancy test (always first when pregnancy is possible) → pelvic exam and STI tests → ultrasound → HSG or laparoscopy for a fertility workup → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (suspected ectopic):* straight to the emergency department → pregnancy test, blood hCG levels, and urgent ultrasound → treatment begins immediately if a ruptured ectopic is suspected, because internal bleeding can become life-threatening quickly.

**Common treatment approaches.** Antibiotics are the mainstay for PID and tubal infection, given early and sometimes in hospital. Approaches doctors commonly consider for ectopic pregnancy: a medicine (methotrexate, a drug that stops the pregnancy tissue growing) when it is caught early and stable, or surgery — usually keyhole — to remove the pregnancy, sometimes with the tube. For blocked tubes: surgery to open or repair a tube in selected cases, removal of a fluid-filled tube (often before IVF), and IVF itself, which works around the tubes rather than through them. Tubal cancer is treated similarly to ovarian cancer, with surgery and chemotherapy.
