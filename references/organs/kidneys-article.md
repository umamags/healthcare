# Kidneys

**What they do.** You have two kidneys, each about the size of a fist, tucked against the back wall of the belly below the ribs. Every day they filter an enormous volume of blood through roughly a million microscopic filters (nephrons) per kidney, pulling out waste products and excess fluid, which leave the body as urine. They also fine-tune the balance of salts and minerals like sodium and potassium, help control blood pressure, and produce hormones — including one that tells the bone marrow to make red blood cells and one involved in activating vitamin D for bones.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Chronic kidney disease (CKD) — a gradual, usually permanent decline in filtering ability, most often driven by diabetes or high blood pressure. Doctors describe it in stages 1 to 5: from mild (kidneys slightly affected, often only detectable on tests) through moderate and severe, to kidney failure (stage 5), where the kidneys can barely filter at all. Kidney stones — hard crystals that form in the kidney and cause excruciating pain when they move into the urinary tract. Urinary tract infections — usually start in the bladder, but if they climb to the kidney (pyelonephritis) they become serious.
*Less common but serious:* Acute kidney injury — a sudden drop in kidney function from severe dehydration, infection, certain drugs, or blocked urine flow; often reversible if the cause is treated quickly. Polycystic kidney disease — an inherited condition where fluid-filled cysts grow in the kidneys. Kidney cancer — see our cancer section.

**Which doctors get involved.** A nephrologist is the medical kidney specialist — they manage CKD, dialysis, and transplant care. A urologist is the surgical specialist for the urinary tract — they remove stones, relieve blockages, and operate on kidney tumors. See a nephrologist for failing filters; a urologist for stones and plumbing problems. Your primary care doctor handles the screening that catches kidney disease early (blood pressure, blood sugar, urine checks), and a dietitian often helps with the kidney diet in advanced CKD.

**Usual tests.** *Blood:* creatinine, a waste product used to calculate eGFR — an estimate of filtering ability and the main number doctors track in CKD. *Urine:* checked for protein or blood leaking through damaged filters. *Bedside:* blood pressure, since kidney disease and hypertension feed each other. *Imaging:* ultrasound (kidney size, stones, blockages) · CT scan (detailed stone mapping). *Rarely:* kidney biopsy — a tiny tissue sample, used when the cause is unclear.

**Keeping them healthy.** Control the two biggest threats: blood pressure and blood sugar — together they cause most CKD worldwide. Drink enough water through the day (pale-yellow urine is a practical guide; there's no magic number of glasses). Eat less salt. Don't smoke — it damages kidney blood vessels. Limit alcohol. Be cautious with NSAID painkillers (like ibuprofen): occasional use is fine for most people, but frequent or heavy use without medical advice can injure kidneys — ask a pharmacist or doctor about alternatives if you use them often.
*Myth box:* "You'd feel it if your kidneys were failing." Usually false. Early and middle-stage CKD typically has no symptoms at all — people feel fine while their filters quietly decline. That's exactly why blood and urine tests matter for people with diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of kidney disease.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: puffy ankles, feet, or face (fluid retention); foamy urine (possible protein leak) or blood in the urine; urinating far more or far less than usual; persistent fatigue with pale skin (possible anemia from failing kidneys). Emergency — call your local emergency number now: sudden severe pain in the side or back below the ribs, especially with vomiting or fever — a stone blocking urine flow with infection behind it can become dangerous fast; or little-to-no urine output combined with swelling, breathlessness, or confusion. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor, urgently in the emergency cases.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* risk factors or symptoms → blood pressure check → blood creatinine/eGFR and urine test → ultrasound if something needs a closer look → CKD stage assigned and monitored over time. Kidney disease is usually tracked across repeat visits, not diagnosed in one. *Emergency path:* suspected obstructing stone with fever, or sudden loss of urine output → emergency department → urgent imaging and blood tests → treatment to relieve the blockage or support the kidneys begins quickly.

**Common treatment approaches.** For CKD, treatment targets the causes: blood-pressure medicines (certain classes also protect the kidneys directly), blood-sugar control, diet changes (less salt, and protein/phosphorus limits in later stages), and regular monitoring. Kidney stones: pain relief, fluids, and watchful waiting for small stones; procedures to break up or remove larger ones (such as shock-wave lithotripsy or ureteroscopy) are approaches urologists commonly consider. Kidney infections: antibiotics. When kidneys reach failure, doctors discuss renal replacement options — dialysis (filtering the blood artificially) or kidney transplant — as part of careful, personal planning with the patient and family.
