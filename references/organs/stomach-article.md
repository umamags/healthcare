### Full article: Stomach

**What it does.** The stomach is a stretchy, muscular sac — about the size of your fist when empty, expanding to hold a meal. It churns food like a cement mixer while glands in its lining release hydrochloric acid and enzymes. The acid is strong enough to sting skin, yet the stomach protects itself with a thick mucus layer. Together they turn food into chyme, a thick liquid the intestines can process — and the acid kills many germs hitching a ride on food.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Acid reflux / GERD — stomach acid flows back into the food pipe, causing heartburn (a burning feeling behind the breastbone; despite the name, nothing to do with the heart) and an acid taste. Gastritis — inflammation of the stomach lining, causing upper-belly pain, nausea, or bloating. Stomach ulcers — open sores in the lining; most are caused by H. pylori bacteria or by regular use of NSAID painkillers (like ibuprofen), not by lifestyle alone. Indigestion (dyspepsia) — discomfort or fullness after eating, with no single cause.
*Less common but serious:* Stomach cancer — often silent early; persistent indigestion, weight loss, or anaemia in older adults deserves investigation. Bleeding ulcers — can be dangerous and need urgent care.

**Which doctors get involved.** A gastroenterologist diagnoses and treats digestive conditions, including with endoscopy. A general surgeon operates when needed — for complicated ulcers or stomach cancer. Your primary care doctor manages straightforward reflux and indigestion and refers onwards when symptoms persist.

**Usual tests.** *Procedures:* endoscopy (gastroscopy) — a thin, flexible camera passed through the mouth to inspect the lining; tissue samples (biopsies) can be taken painlessly during it. *Lab:* H. pylori breath, stool, or blood tests; blood tests for anaemia from slow bleeding. *Imaging:* abdominal ultrasound or CT scan when something beyond the stomach needs a look.

**Keeping it healthy.** Eat regular, unhurried meals and stop before you're overfull — smaller meals mean less reflux. Learn your triggers: spicy or fatty foods, coffee, alcohol, and fizzy drinks bother many people, but triggers are personal. Don't smoke — it weakens the valve that keeps acid down and slows ulcer healing. Limit alcohol, which irritates the lining. If you take NSAID painkillers regularly, ask your doctor about stomach-protecting options rather than stopping on your own. Losing excess weight and not lying down right after eating both ease reflux.
*Myth box:* "Stress causes ulcers." Mostly false — the great majority of ulcers come from H. pylori infection or NSAID painkillers. Stress can worsen the pain and slow healing, but it rarely creates an ulcer by itself. (This was only proven in the 1980s — before that, everyone blamed stress and spicy food.)

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor soon: heartburn more than a couple of times a week; gnawing or burning upper-belly pain; nausea, bloating, or feeling full after small meals; unexplained weight loss or fading appetite; black specks in vomit or stool. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds; black, tarry stools; sudden, severe abdominal pain — especially if the belly feels rigid (possible perforated ulcer, which needs emergency surgery).

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including medicines and painkiller use) → physical exam → H. pylori test for ulcer-type pain → endoscopy if symptoms persist, if you're over a certain age with new symptoms, or if there are alarm signs like weight loss or bleeding → biopsy results → diagnosis and treatment plan.

**Common treatment approaches.** Lifestyle measures come first for reflux: meal timing, trigger foods, weight, and raising the head of the bed. Acid-reducing medicines are the mainstay — from simple antacids to stronger classes (H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors) that cut acid production. H. pylori is cleared with a combination of antibiotics plus an acid-reducer, taken exactly as prescribed. Most ulcers heal with medicines; surgery is now rare and reserved for complications like perforation or uncontrollable bleeding.
