# Epididymis & vas deferens (sperm ducts) — full article

**What they do.** After sperm are made in the testicle, they enter the epididymis — a single, tightly coiled tube, several metres long if uncoiled, sitting on the back of each testicle. Sperm spend days to weeks there, maturing and learning to swim, and are stored there until ejaculation. From the epididymis, sperm travel up the vas deferens — a firm, cord-like tube you can actually feel through the scrotal skin — into the pelvis, where it joins the duct from the seminal vesicle to form the ejaculatory duct, emptying into the urethra. During ejaculation, muscles squeeze the vas deferens in waves to move sperm along quickly. Without these ducts, the testes could make sperm perfectly well but the sperm could never reach the outside — which is exactly the principle behind a vasectomy (see below).

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Epididymitis — inflammation of the epididymis, usually from infection, causing pain and swelling at the back of the testicle that builds over a few days. In younger men the cause is often a sexually transmitted infection such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea; in older men it's more often ordinary urinary bacteria, sometimes linked to prostate enlargement. Epididymal cysts and spermatocele — harmless fluid-filled swellings felt as a small lump separate from the testicle; common and usually painless. Blocked vas deferens — scarring from infection, injury, or surgery (or absence from birth) can block the duct and cause infertility even though sperm production is normal.
*Less common but serious:* Post-vasectomy pain syndrome — ongoing scrotal ache after a vasectomy; uncommon but real, and worth reporting rather than enduring. Epididymitis that is ignored can occasionally form an abscess or damage fertility, which is why prompt treatment matters.

**Which doctors get involved.** A urologist handles most problems with these ducts, including epididymitis, cysts, and vasectomy. An andrologist or fertility specialist takes the lead when blocked ducts are part of a couple's difficulty conceiving, since repair or sperm retrieval is specialised work.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* feeling the scrotum to locate exactly where pain or a lump sits — epididymis, testicle, or cord. *Lab tests:* urine tests and STI tests to find the infection behind epididymitis. *Imaging:* scrotal ultrasound to confirm inflammation, cysts, or blockages and to check the testicle itself. *Other:* semen analysis, which shows whether sperm are getting through; if sperm counts are zero or very low but hormones are normal, a blocked duct is one possible cause.

**Keeping them healthy.** The ducts' main enemy is infection. Protect against STIs (including using condoms and getting tested when partners change), and treat urinary infections promptly rather than letting them linger — bacteria can travel backwards along the vas deferens to the epididymis. Seek care early for scrotal pain or swelling: epididymitis treated in the first days usually settles completely.
*Myth box:* "A vasectomy lowers your masculinity or sex drive." False. A vasectomy cuts or seals the vas deferens so sperm can't pass — nothing else changes. The testes keep making testosterone, erections and sex drive are unaffected, and ejaculate looks and feels the same (sperm are a tiny fraction of its volume). It is a contraception method, not a change to your hormones.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: aching pain or swelling in the scrotum that builds over days, a new lump on the back of the testicle, burning or pain after passing urine, discharge, or difficulty conceiving after a year of trying. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: sudden severe scrotal pain (a twisted testicle — torsion — must be ruled out first; see the testes page), or scrotal pain with high fever and feeling unwell.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including sexual history and urinary symptoms — be candid; it changes the likely cause) → physical exam → urine and STI tests → scrotal ultrasound if the picture is unclear → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (sudden severe pain):* straight to the emergency department → rapid exam and Doppler ultrasound to exclude torsion before treating for infection.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the cause. For epididymitis: antibiotics matched to the likely cause (STI treatment in younger men, urinary-bacteria cover in older men), pain relievers and anti-inflammatory medicines, scrotal support, and rest; partners need testing and treatment too when an STI is involved. Epididymal cysts usually need no treatment at all; surgery is an option if one becomes painful or large. For blocked ducts: microsurgical repair in selected cases, or sperm retrieval combined with fertility treatment. Vasectomy itself is a short procedure, usually under local anaesthetic — and while reversal is sometimes possible, it should be chosen as a permanent method.
