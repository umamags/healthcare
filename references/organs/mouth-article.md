### Full article: Mouth

**What it does.** The mouth is where digestion begins. Lips and cheeks hold food in, teeth cut and grind it, and the tongue — a strong muscular organ — rolls it into a ball and pushes it toward the throat for swallowing. Saliva, made by glands around the mouth, moistens food, starts breaking down starches, and contains substances that fight germs and protect teeth. The tongue's taste buds detect sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and savoury flavours, and the tongue's precise movements shape most speech sounds.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Mouth ulcers (canker sores) — small, painful sores inside the cheeks or on the tongue; common and usually heal within two weeks. Cold sores — blisters on or around the lips caused by the herpes simplex virus; contagious, unlike canker sores. Oral thrush — a yeast infection causing creamy white patches; more common in babies, denture wearers, and people with weakened immunity. Dry mouth — too little saliva, often a medication side effect; raises the risk of tooth decay.
*Less common but serious:* Oral cancer — can appear as a sore, lump, or white/red patch that doesn't heal; risk is higher with tobacco and heavy alcohol use.

**Which doctors get involved.** A dentist spots most mouth problems first during check-ups. An oral & maxillofacial surgeon handles surgery — biopsies, removing suspicious growths, jaw problems. An ENT specialist (otolaryngologist) deals with the throat-side of mouth issues and some cancers. Your primary care doctor coordinates, especially when a mouth problem connects to a wider condition (like diabetes with thrush).

**Usual tests.** Oral exam (looking and feeling inside the mouth and neck) · swab or culture to identify thrush or other infections · biopsy — a tiny tissue sample — of any sore that doesn't heal · blood tests (for vitamin deficiencies or immune causes of recurring ulcers) · imaging such as X-ray or CT if cancer is suspected.

**Keeping it healthy.** Brush twice daily and gently clean your tongue — bacteria there cause much bad breath. Drink water through the day; saliva is your mouth's natural defence. Avoid tobacco in all forms and limit alcohol — the two biggest preventable risks for oral cancer. Eat a varied diet; recurring ulcers can sometimes signal low iron, folate, or B12 — worth mentioning to a doctor rather than self-treating. Keep dental check-ups even when nothing hurts.
*Myth box:* "All mouth sores are contagious." False — canker sores (ulcers inside the mouth) are not contagious at all. Cold sores on the lips are, because they're caused by a virus.

**Warning signs.** These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. See a doctor or dentist soon: any ulcer, sore, lump, or white/red patch not healed in 2 weeks; dry mouth that makes eating or speaking difficult; bad breath that persists despite good hygiene; numbness in the mouth. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: rapidly growing swelling of the mouth, face, or neck; trouble swallowing or breathing; bleeding from the mouth that won't stop.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → visual and touch exam of the mouth and neck → swab/culture if infection is suspected → biopsy of anything that doesn't heal → imaging if cancer is a concern → diagnosis and treatment plan. Most mouth issues are diagnosed quickly; only suspicious lesions take the longer path.

**Common treatment approaches.** Most mouth ulcers need no treatment beyond time, though gels or mouthwashes can ease pain. Antifungal medicines treat thrush. Antiviral medicines can shorten cold-sore outbreaks. Dry mouth is managed by treating the cause, saliva substitutes, and extra dental protection against decay. Suspicious growths are biopsied and, if cancerous, treated with surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy — our cancer section covers this journey in detail.
