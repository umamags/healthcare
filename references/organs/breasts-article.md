# Breasts — full article

**What they do.** Each breast is a mammary gland sitting on the chest muscle. Inside, clusters of tiny glands called lobules are where milk is made; a network of tubes called ducts carries milk to the nipple. The rest of the breast is fatty tissue and supportive ligaments, which give it size and shape — breast size has nothing to do with how much milk a person can make. Breasts respond to hormones all life long: they often swell and feel lumpy or tender before a period, grow during pregnancy, produce milk after childbirth (triggered by the hormones prolactin and oxytocin), and gradually change again at menopause, when gland tissue is partly replaced by fat. Because they change so much normally, the key health skill is knowing what is normal for you.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Benign lumps — most breast lumps are not cancer. Cysts are fluid-filled sacs that can appear and vanish with the cycle; fibroadenomas are firm, smooth, mobile lumps common in younger women. Cyclical breast pain — aching and tenderness linked to the menstrual cycle. Nipple discharge — often harmless, especially if milky or from both sides. Mastitis — a painful, inflamed area of the breast, usually during breastfeeding when milk gets trapped and bacteria enter; it can progress to an abscess (a pocket of pus).
*Less common but serious:* Breast cancer — the most common cancer in women worldwide; usually a painless lump or an area of thickening, which is why painless changes still need checking. Inflammatory breast cancer — a rare, fast-moving type causing redness, warmth, swelling, and dimpled "orange-peel" skin. Men can get breast cancer too, though it is rare — a lump in a man's breast deserves the same prompt check.

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor is the first stop for any breast change and does the initial examination. A breast surgeon manages lumps, biopsies, and operations. A radiologist performs and interprets mammograms, ultrasounds, and biopsies. An oncologist leads treatment if cancer is confirmed. A lactation consultant supports breastfeeding problems such as blocked ducts and early mastitis — a support role rather than a diagnosing one.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* clinical breast examination — the doctor feels the breasts and armpits. *Imaging:* mammogram (a low-dose breast X-ray, used for screening and investigation), breast ultrasound (especially useful in younger women and for telling a cyst from a solid lump), and breast MRI (used in selected situations, such as high-risk screening or planning surgery). *Procedures:* core-needle biopsy — a hollow needle removes a small sample of tissue for laboratory examination. This matters: imaging can raise or lower suspicion, but a biopsy is how a lump is actually confirmed as cancerous or benign.

**Keeping them healthy — and finding problems early.** Be breast aware: look and feel regularly, know your own normal, and report changes rather than waiting for the next check. Attend routine screening mammography if your local programme offers it — the ages and intervals differ between countries, so follow your local guidance. Keep a healthy weight, stay physically active, and limit alcohol; each lowers risk somewhat. There is no proven way to prevent breast cancer entirely, which is exactly why awareness and screening matter.
*Myth box:* "Underwire bras and antiperspirants cause breast cancer." False. Large studies have found no link between bras of any kind, or deodorants/antiperspirants, and breast cancer. Another myth worth dropping: "a lump that doesn't hurt can't be cancer." In fact, most early breast cancers are painless — never wait for pain before getting a lump checked.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon — within days, not months: a new lump or area of thickening; skin dimpling or an orange-peel texture; a nipple that has newly turned inward; nipple discharge, especially if bloody or from one side only; redness, warmth, or swelling of one breast; a new lump in the armpit; any change in breast size or shape that persists through a full cycle. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — most turn out to be benign — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency: true breast emergencies are rare. The exception is a hot, painful, red breast with fever or flu-like aching, most often while breastfeeding — possible mastitis or abscess, which needs medical care the same day. Call your local emergency number if you can't be seen promptly or feel very unwell.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** *Routine path:* a change is noticed (by you or on screening) → clinical exam → imaging (mammogram and/or ultrasound) → core-needle biopsy if anything is uncertain → laboratory result → diagnosis and plan. This exam–imaging–biopsy combination is sometimes called triple assessment, and a benign result at each step is reassuring. *Screening path:* a routine mammogram sometimes calls women back for extra views or an ultrasound; most of these recalls turn out not to be cancer.

**Common treatment approaches.** Many benign lumps need no treatment at all, or simple monitoring; a troublesome cyst can be drained with a needle. For breast pain, doctors commonly consider a well-fitting supportive bra and pain-relief medicines. Mastitis is treated with antibiotics, continued milk drainage (feeding or expressing), and drainage if an abscess forms. If cancer is confirmed, approaches doctors commonly consider include surgery (removing the lump or the whole breast), radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormone (endocrine) therapy, and targeted medicines — usually in combination, planned around the cancer's type and stage.
