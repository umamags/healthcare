# Uterus — full article

**What it does.** The uterus (womb) is a hollow, muscular, pear-shaped organ sitting low in the pelvis. It has three layers: an outer coat, a thick muscular wall (myometrium), and an inner lining called the endometrium. Each month, hormones signal the endometrium to thicken in case a fertilised egg arrives. If no pregnancy happens, the lining breaks down and leaves the body as a period. During pregnancy, the uterus stretches enormously — from about the size of a pear to holding a full-grown baby — while the placenta, attached to its wall, passes oxygen and nutrients to the baby. In labour, the muscular wall contracts in waves to open the cervix and push the baby out. After menopause, when periods stop, the uterus gradually shrinks.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Fibroids — benign (non-cancerous) growths in the muscle wall; very common, often symptom-free, but they can cause heavy periods and pressure. Endometriosis — tissue similar to the womb lining grows outside the uterus, causing pain, especially around periods. Adenomyosis — lining tissue grows into the muscle wall, making periods heavy and painful. Endometrial polyps — small, usually benign overgrowths of the lining that can cause irregular bleeding. Heavy menstrual bleeding — periods so heavy they disrupt daily life; it has many causes and is always worth investigating.
*Less common but serious:* Uterine prolapse — weakened pelvic-floor muscles let the womb slip down into the vagina, causing a dragging or bulging feeling. Endometrial cancer — cancer of the lining; its earliest sign is usually unusual bleeding, especially any bleeding after menopause.

**Which doctors get involved.** A gynaecologist handles most uterus problems — investigation, medication, and surgery. A gynaecologic oncologist specialises in cancers of the female reproductive organs. A fertility specialist (reproductive endocrinologist) helps when a uterus condition affects getting pregnant. A urogynaecologist focuses on prolapse and pelvic-floor problems. Your primary care doctor is usually the first stop and coordinates referrals.

**Usual tests.** *Physical exam:* pelvic exam, to feel the size and position of the uterus. *Imaging:* pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound — usually the first test, shows fibroids, polyps, and lining thickness; MRI for a more detailed map before surgery or when ultrasound is unclear. *Procedures:* hysteroscopy — a thin camera passed through the cervix to look inside the womb, often with treatment in the same visit; endometrial biopsy — a small sample of the lining checked under a microscope. One common confusion: a Pap test samples cells from the cervix, not the uterus — a normal Pap result says nothing about the womb lining.

**Keeping it healthy.** There is no special "womb diet," but general habits help: keep a healthy weight (excess weight raises the risk of endometrial cancer and worsens bleeding problems), stay active, and don't smoke. The most protective habit is attention: very heavy periods, bleeding between periods, and any bleeding after menopause are signals to get checked, not things to put up with. Keep up routine gynaecology visits even when you feel well.
*Myth box:* "Fibroids turn into cancer." Almost never. Fibroids are benign growths, and the rare cancer that grows in the womb muscle (sarcoma) does not usually develop from an ordinary fibroid. Doctors watch fibroids mainly because of bleeding, pressure, and fertility — not because they are expected to turn cancerous.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: periods that flood or need double protection, bleeding between periods or after sex, pelvic pressure or pain that keeps coming back, a feeling of something bulging into the vagina. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: bleeding that soaks a pad or tampon every hour for two or more hours, severe pelvic pain, feeling faint from blood loss, or any bleeding during pregnancy.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (your bleeding pattern matters — keep notes or a period diary) → pelvic exam → ultrasound → hysteroscopy and/or endometrial biopsy if the lining needs checking → diagnosis and treatment plan. *Emergency path (very heavy bleeding):* straight to the emergency department → assessment of blood loss, blood tests, and urgent treatment to stop the bleeding while the cause is investigated.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the condition, your age, and whether you want children. Medication classes: pain relievers (NSAIDs), hormonal medicines (including contraceptive pills and hormone-releasing intrauterine devices) to lighten bleeding, hormone-blocking injections for fibroids or endometriosis, and iron supplements when heavy bleeding causes anaemia. Procedures: hysteroscopic removal of polyps or small fibroids, endometrial ablation (destroying the lining to reduce bleeding). Surgery: myomectomy (removing fibroids while keeping the uterus), prolapse repair, and hysterectomy (removing the uterus) for severe symptoms or cancer. For endometrial cancer, treatment usually centres on surgery, sometimes with radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
