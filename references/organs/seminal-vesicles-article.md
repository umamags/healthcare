# Seminal Vesicles — full article

**What they do.** The seminal vesicles are a pair of small, wrinkled, tube-like glands, each a few centimetres long, sitting behind the bladder and above the prostate. Despite the name, they do not store sperm — sperm are made in the testes and mature in the epididymis. The seminal vesicles are fluid factories. At ejaculation they contract and empty their secretion into the sperm's path, contributing the largest share of semen volume — commonly around two-thirds or more. Their fluid is alkaline and rich in fructose, a sugar that nourishes sperm and helps them swim; it also contains substances that help semen clot briefly after ejaculation and then liquefy again. On each side, the duct of the seminal vesicle joins the vas deferens (the sperm duct) to form an ejaculatory duct, which passes through the prostate and opens into the urethra. Because they sit deep in the pelvis and work quietly, most people only hear of the seminal vesicles when something shows up in the semen — blood, pain, or a fertility test result.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Seminal vesiculitis — inflammation of one or both glands, usually caused by the same bacteria behind urinary and prostate infections, and often occurring together with prostatitis. It can cause deep pelvic or perineal pain, painful ejaculation, and blood in the semen. Blood in the semen (haematospermia) — alarming to see, but in most cases, especially in younger men, it comes from minor inflammation like this and settles on its own (see the myth box). Cysts — fluid-filled pockets in the gland, usually found by chance on scans; larger ones can cause pain or block the ejaculatory duct.
*Less common but serious:* Stones (calculi) — small calcified stones that can form in the glands and cause pain or blood in the semen. Congenital absence — some men are born with one or both seminal vesicles (and often the vas deferens on the same side) missing; because their fluid never arrives, semen volume is very low and infertility results. Seminal vesicle cancer — extremely rare; more often, the glands are affected by spread from a nearby cancer, such as prostate cancer.

**Which doctors get involved.** A urologist manages diseases of the seminal vesicles — infections, cysts, and stones. A fertility specialist or andrologist takes the lead when the problem presents as infertility or abnormal semen results, such as very low volume or absent fructose. A primary care doctor is often the first stop for blood in the semen and arranges the initial checks and referral.

**Usual tests.** *Lab tests:* semen analysis — measures volume, sperm numbers, and fructose (low volume with no fructose points toward the seminal vesicles or their ducts); urine and semen cultures to look for infection. *Exam:* a prostate (rectal) exam, during which an enlarged or tender seminal vesicle can sometimes be felt. *Imaging:* transrectal ultrasound — an ultrasound probe in the rectum sits very close to the glands and is the usual first scan; pelvic MRI gives a detailed picture when ultrasound is unclear or before surgery.

**Keeping them healthy.** There is no specific diet or exercise for the seminal vesicles — they benefit from looking after the whole urinary and reproductive tract. Get urinary and prostate symptoms treated rather than ignoring them; practise safer sex, since sexually transmitted infections can reach the prostate and seminal vesicles; and manage diabetes, which raises infection risk generally. Seek a semen analysis if you and a partner have been trying to conceive for a year without success, or sooner if semen volume is obviously very low.
*Myth box:* "Blood in the semen always means cancer." It usually doesn't. In most men — particularly those under 40 — blood in the semen is caused by minor inflammation or a burst tiny blood vessel and clears within a few weeks. It should still be checked by a doctor, and checked promptly if it keeps happening, if you're over 40, or if it comes with pain, fever, or urinary symptoms. Cancer is one of the least likely causes, but persistent cases need a proper look.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: blood in the semen that persists or keeps returning; painful ejaculation; a deep, dull pelvic or perineal ache; infertility or noticeably very low semen volume. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency: problems in the seminal vesicles themselves are rarely emergencies, but seek emergency care — call your local emergency number — for a high fever with pelvic pain and feeling very unwell (possible spreading pelvic infection), or if you become completely unable to pass urine.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including infections, urinary symptoms, and fertility) → prostate exam → semen analysis and urine/semen cultures → transrectal ultrasound → pelvic MRI if needed → diagnosis and plan. For a single episode of blood in the semen in a younger man with a normal exam, doctors may simply reassure and review; persistent, recurrent, or older-age cases get the full work-up above.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the cause. Bacterial inflammation is treated with antibiotics — sometimes a longer course, as for prostatitis — plus anti-inflammatory pain relief. Most single episodes of blood in the semen need no treatment beyond reassurance once checks are normal. Cysts or stones that cause ongoing pain or block ejaculation can be drained or removed with endoscopic or surgical procedures. When congenital absence causes infertility, sperm can usually be retrieved directly from the testis or epididymis for assisted conception, which a fertility specialist coordinates.
