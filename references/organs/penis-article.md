# Penis — full article

**What it does.** The penis is the male external sex organ, and it also carries the final stretch of the urethra, the tube that drains urine from the bladder. Its visible parts are the shaft (the body), the glans (the rounded head), the foreskin (a fold of skin covering the glans in uncircumcised men), and the urethral opening at the tip. Inside the shaft are columns of spongy tissue. During sexual arousal, signals from the nervous system open up the penile arteries so blood flows in faster than it drains away; the spongy tissue fills and expands, producing an erection. At climax, muscles contract to ejaculate semen — sperm mixed with fluids from the prostate and seminal vesicles — through the urethra. Because an erection is essentially a blood-flow event, erection quality is closely tied to the health of the heart and blood vessels.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Erectile dysfunction (ED) — persistent difficulty getting or keeping an erection firm enough for sex. It becomes more common with age and is often an early warning sign of blood-vessel problems, heart disease, or diabetes (see the heart page). Balanitis — inflammation of the glans and foreskin causing redness, soreness, and itching; triggers include irritation, poor or excessive washing, and yeast or bacterial infection, and it is more common with diabetes. Phimosis — a foreskin too tight to pull back over the glans; normal in young boys, but a problem if it persists or develops in adults. Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) — can cause sores, discharge, or warts; many have no symptoms at all (see safer sex, below).
*Less common but serious:* Peyronie's disease — scar tissue (a plaque) forms inside the shaft, causing a bend or curve during erection, sometimes with pain or shortening. Paraphimosis — a tight foreskin that has been pulled back gets stuck behind the glans and swells; an emergency. Priapism — a prolonged, often painful erection unrelated to arousal, which can permanently damage the tissue. Penile cancer — rare; often starts as a sore, lump, wart-like growth, or patch of changed skin on the glans or foreskin that doesn't heal.

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor is usually the first stop and can check the general causes of erection problems, such as diabetes and blood pressure. A urologist specialises in the urinary tract and male genitals and handles most penile conditions and surgery. An andrologist focuses on male sexual and reproductive health, including erection and hormone problems. A dermatologist deals with penile skin conditions. A sexual health (genitourinary medicine) doctor tests for and treats STIs.

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* physical examination of the penis and testes, plus blood pressure. *Blood tests:* blood sugar (diabetes check), testosterone, and cholesterol/lipid panel — because erection problems often reflect wider vessel health. *Imaging:* penile ultrasound, sometimes after a small injection that produces an erection, to check blood flow or map Peyronie's scar tissue. *Other:* STI tests (urine sample or swab) where relevant, and a small tissue sample (biopsy) of any suspicious skin change.

**Keeping it healthy.** Protect the blood vessels and you protect erections: don't smoke, stay active, keep blood pressure, cholesterol, blood sugar, and weight in check, and limit alcohol. Practise safer sex — condoms greatly reduce STI risk. Wash daily with water and mild soap if used; if you have a foreskin, gently pull it back, wash underneath, dry, and always replace it over the glans afterwards. Get to know what's normal for you and report new lumps, sores, or patches that don't heal.
*Myth box:* "Erectile dysfunction is just ageing, or it's all in your head." Usually false. Stress and anxiety can contribute, but in most men ED has a physical component — very often narrowed blood vessels — and it can appear years before other signs of heart disease. Treating it as a health signal, not an embarrassment, is how it gets fixed.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: erection problems that persist beyond a few weeks; a new curve or a felt lump in the shaft; a red, sore, or itchy glans; a foreskin that won't retract; discharge from the tip; any sore, lump, or colour change that doesn't heal within a few weeks. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: an erection lasting more than about 4 hours (priapism — delay can cause permanent damage); a foreskin stuck behind the glans with swelling (paraphimosis); a sudden snapping or popping pain during sex followed by rapid swelling and bruising (a "fracture" of the erect penis — actually a tear in its inner lining); or a severe injury or bleeding that won't stop.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history (including erections on waking, medicines, and general health) → physical exam → blood tests for sugar, testosterone, and cholesterol → penile ultrasound or STI tests if needed → diagnosis and plan. For a suspicious skin change, the key step is a biopsy. *Emergency path:* priapism, paraphimosis, and penile "fracture" go straight to the emergency department — these are treated on arrival because tissue damage is time-sensitive.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the cause. For erectile dysfunction: treating the underlying condition (diabetes, blood pressure), lifestyle change, and medicines that improve blood flow to the penis (a class called PDE5 inhibitors), hormone treatment if testosterone is genuinely low, vacuum devices, injections, or — in selected cases — a penile implant. For balanitis: gentle hygiene, antifungal or antibiotic creams, and treatment of underlying diabetes. For phimosis: steroid cream and gentle stretching, or circumcision if it persists. For Peyronie's: monitoring (it sometimes settles), injections into the scar tissue, or surgery for a stable, troublesome curve. For penile cancer: surgery is the mainstay, sometimes with radiotherapy or chemotherapy — details live in the cancer section.
