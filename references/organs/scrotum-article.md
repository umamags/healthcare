# Scrotum — full article

**What it does.** The scrotum is a pouch of skin and thin muscle (the dartos muscle) hanging behind the penis. Inside, a partition divides it into two compartments, one for each testis. Its job is protection and temperature control. Sperm are made best at a temperature a few degrees below core body temperature, so the scrotum constantly fine-tunes position: in the cold its muscles contract, wrinkling the skin and drawing the testes up toward the body's warmth; in heat they relax and let the testes hang lower to cool. It helps to know that the scrotum itself is mostly a container — pain, lumps, and swelling usually arise from what it holds: the testis, the epididymis (the coiled tube behind the testis), the veins of the spermatic cord, the tissues of the groin above it, or occasionally the scrotal skin itself. Those structures have their own pages (see testes); this page covers the sac and the swellings that show up in it.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Hydrocele — clear fluid collects in the sac around a testis, producing a smooth, usually painless swelling; very common in newborn boys (it usually resolves) and also seen in adults. Varicocele — the veins draining the testis become enlarged and twisted, typically on the left, feeling like a "bag of worms"; it may cause a dull ache and, in some men, reduced sperm quality. Epididymal cyst / spermatocele — a small, smooth, fluid-filled lump on the epididymis, separate from the testis itself; benign and very common with age. Inguinal hernia — a loop of bowel or fatty tissue pushes through a weak spot in the groin and can descend into the scrotum, causing a bulge that comes and goes, often bigger on standing or coughing. Skin problems — sweat-gland cysts, ingrown hairs, and dermatitis from heat, sweat, and friction.
*Less common but serious:* A trapped (incarcerated) hernia — a hernia that gets stuck and loses its blood supply; painful and dangerous. Fournier's gangrene — a rare, fast-spreading bacterial infection of the scrotal and genital skin, most often in men with diabetes or a weakened immune system; it is life-threatening and needs emergency surgery.

**Which doctors get involved.** A primary care doctor is the first stop for any scrotal lump, swelling, or pain and can usually tell a simple fluid collection from something that needs a specialist. A urologist specialises in the urinary tract and male genitals and manages most scrotal conditions. A general surgeon repairs hernias. Any sudden, severe scrotal pain bypasses all of this — it goes straight to emergency care (see the testes page on testicular torsion).

**Usual tests.** *Exam:* physical examination standing and lying down — fluid, cysts, hernias, and vein swellings each feel different, and a hydrocele typically lets light from a small torch shine through it. *Imaging:* scrotal ultrasound — the key test; it shows fluid, cysts, veins, and the testis itself clearly and painlessly. *Lab tests:* urine tests (for infection) and blood tests when infection or inflammation is suspected.

**Keeping it healthy.** Wear supportive underwear for sport and exercise, and a protective cup for contact sports. Keep the skin clean and dry, and change out of sweaty clothing — warm, damp skin is prone to irritation and fungal infection. Avoid very tight clothing if you find it uncomfortable. The most useful habit is familiarity: check during a warm shower now and then, learn what your scrotum normally feels like, and report new lumps, one-sided growth, or persistent heaviness rather than waiting.
*Myth box:* "A varicocele always causes infertility." False. Varicoceles are common, and most men who have one father children without difficulty. Doctors mainly investigate or treat a varicocele when it causes pain, when the testis on that side is smaller, or when a couple is having trouble conceiving and sperm quality is reduced.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: a painless lump or swelling; a feeling of heaviness or a dull dragging ache; one side gradually becoming larger than the other; persistent skin redness, itching, or a sore that doesn't heal. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — many scrotal swellings turn out to be harmless fluid or cysts — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: sudden severe pain in the scrotum (possible testicular torsion — see the testes page; hours matter); a groin or scrotal bulge that becomes painful and can't be pushed back, especially with nausea or vomiting (possible trapped hernia); rapidly spreading redness or darkening of the scrotal skin with fever and severe pain (possible Fournier's gangrene).

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Symptoms and history → physical exam standing and lying (and coughing, which makes a hernia bulge) → scrotal ultrasound to identify exactly what the swelling is → urine or blood tests if infection is suspected → diagnosis and plan. *Emergency path:* sudden severe pain or a painful trapped bulge goes straight to the emergency department — ultrasound and surgical assessment happen there, without waiting for a routine referral.

**Common treatment approaches.** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the cause. Many hydroceles, cysts, and varicoceles need no treatment at all — just monitoring and reassurance. Supportive underwear and pain-relief medicines help aching varicoceles. Surgery is the usual route when treatment is needed: draining and repairing a hydrocele, tying off varicocele veins (or blocking them via a catheter procedure), removing a troublesome cyst, or repairing a hernia. Infections are treated with antibiotics — and Fournier's gangrene is a surgical emergency, with urgent removal of dead tissue alongside strong antibiotics.
