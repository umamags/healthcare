# Cervix — full article

**What it does.** The cervix is the lower, narrow part of the uterus — its "neck" — sitting at the top of the vagina. A small central canal runs through it, connecting the vagina to the inside of the womb. The cervix produces mucus, and the mucus changes across the menstrual cycle: around ovulation it becomes clear and stretchy to help sperm through; at other times it is thicker and acts as a barrier. During pregnancy, a mucus plug seals the canal and helps protect the baby from infection. In labour, the cervix softens, thins, and dilates (opens) to about 10 cm so the baby can pass through. A doctor can see the cervix during a pelvic exam, which is why it can be sampled easily for screening tests.

**What can go wrong.**
*Common:* Cervicitis — inflammation of the cervix, often from a sexually transmitted infection such as chlamydia, sometimes from irritation; it may cause discharge or bleeding after sex, or no symptoms at all. Cervical polyps — small, soft growths on the cervix; almost always benign, but they can bleed. HPV infection — human papillomavirus is extremely common and spreads through sexual contact; most infections clear on their own within a year or two without causing any problem.
*Less common but serious:* Precancerous cell changes (CIN — cervical intraepithelial neoplasia) — long-lasting HPV infection can slowly change the cells of the cervix. These changes are not cancer, cause no symptoms, and are exactly what screening is designed to catch; they can be treated before cancer develops. Cervical cancer — develops over years from untreated precancerous changes, which is why vaccination plus regular screening prevents most cases.

**Which doctors get involved.** Screening (Pap and HPV tests) is usually arranged through primary care or a screening programme. A gynaecologist investigates symptoms such as abnormal bleeding. A colposcopist specialises in examining the cervix under magnification and treating precancerous changes. A gynaecologic oncologist treats cervical cancer. Sexual-health clinics handle testing and treatment for infections such as chlamydia.

**Usual tests.** *Screening tests:* the Pap test (smear) — a small sample of cervical cells is examined for abnormal changes; the HPV test — checks the same kind of sample for the high-risk virus types that cause most cervical cancers. Which test is used, and how often, depends on your local programme — follow its guidance. *Procedures:* colposcopy — the cervix is viewed through a magnifying instrument, sometimes with a small biopsy (tissue sample) taken; the biopsy confirms the grade of any cell changes. These tests check the cervix only — they say nothing about the uterus lining or ovaries.

**Keeping it healthy.** Three habits do most of the work. HPV vaccination: it protects against the virus types that cause most cervical cancers and is offered in many countries, usually in early adolescence — follow your local programme. Regular screening: screening finds cell changes years before they could become cancer, when treatment is simple; attend when invited, even if you feel perfectly well and even if you have been vaccinated. Safer sex and not smoking: condoms reduce (though don't completely eliminate) HPV transmission, and smoking makes it harder for the body to clear HPV and raises cancer risk.
*Myth box:* "An abnormal screening result means I have cancer." No. The large majority of abnormal results show early cell changes (CIN), not cancer — changes that can be monitored or removed in a short clinic procedure, precisely so cancer never develops. An abnormal result is the screening system working as intended.

**Warning signs.** See a doctor soon: bleeding after sex, bleeding between periods or after menopause, unusual discharge (watery, bloody, or with an odour), or pelvic pain. Early cervical cancer often causes no symptoms at all — another reason screening matters. These signs don't diagnose anything by themselves — they mean it's time to talk to a doctor. Emergency — call your local emergency number immediately: heavy bleeding, any bleeding during pregnancy, or severe pelvic pain with fever.

**How doctors reach a diagnosis.** Screening result or symptoms → repeat/HPV testing per local programme → colposcopy with biopsy if needed → the biopsy grade (CIN 1, 2, or 3, or cancer) determines the plan. *Emergency path (heavy bleeding):* straight to the emergency department → assessment and stabilising treatment first, investigation of the cause second.

**Common treatment approaches.** Mild cell changes (CIN 1) are often simply monitored, as many return to normal. Approaches doctors commonly consider for higher-grade changes: removing the affected area in a short procedure (such as a loop excision/LEEP or cone biopsy), which is usually both the test and the treatment. Cervicitis from infection is treated with antibiotics, with partners treated too. Polyps are usually removed in clinic and sent for checking. Cervical cancer treatment depends on stage and may combine surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, planned by a gynaecologic oncologist; early-stage disease found through screening is often curable with surgery alone.
