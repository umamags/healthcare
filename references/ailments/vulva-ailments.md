## Vulvar irritation and allergy
**Summary:** The skin of the vulva is thin and sensitive, and it reacts easily to scented washes, detergents, pads, and other everyday products. The result is itching, burning, and redness — uncomfortable, but usually easy to settle once the trigger is found and dropped.
**Image:** images/vulvar-irritation.jpg
**What it is:** This is contact dermatitis of the vulva — skin inflammation caused by something touching it. Irritant reactions come from harsh or heavily scented products; allergic reactions are the immune system responding to an ingredient such as a fragrance or preservative. The two feel much the same: itching, soreness, burning, and sometimes dry, cracked skin. Because it overlaps with thrush and other skin conditions, it's commonly misread.
**Why it happens:** Common triggers include scented soaps, shower gels, and "feminine hygiene" washes, bubble baths, scented or deodorised pads and liners, laundry detergents and fabric softeners left in underwear, wet wipes, and some creams and lubricants. Heat, sweat, and tight synthetic clothing make sensitive skin react more easily. Once the skin is irritated, scratching damages it further, which causes more itching — the itch–scratch cycle.
**Signs to watch for:** Itching that is often worse at night, burning or stinging — especially when urine touches sore skin — redness, dryness, and small cracks. Symptoms that begin after a new product or detergent, or that keep coming back, point towards this cause.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by examining the skin and asking about products, washing habits, and laundry products. A swab may be taken to rule out thrush or another infection. If a specific allergy is suspected, patch testing (small amounts of substances taped to the back for a few days) can identify it.
**How it's treated:** The most important step is finding the trigger and stopping it. Wash with plain water only — the vulva doesn't need soap or special washes — pat dry gently, and wear loose cotton underwear. A plain, unscented moisturiser or barrier ointment protects healing skin, and a doctor may prescribe a mild topical steroid for a short course to calm inflammation. Recovery takes a few weeks once the trigger is gone.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes: plain-water washing, unscented products everywhere near the area, cotton underwear, and rinsing new underwear before wearing it. One warning worth knowing: "thrush" that never clears despite repeated antifungal treatment is often this — or another skin condition — instead. If treatment isn't working, get examined rather than repeating the same cream.

## Lichen sclerosus
**Summary:** Lichen sclerosus is a long-term skin condition that causes itchy, pale or white patches on the vulva and makes the skin thinner and more fragile. It isn't contagious and it isn't caused by poor hygiene — and with the right treatment it can be well controlled.
**Image:** images/lichen-sclerosus.jpg
**What it is:** In lichen sclerosus, patches of vulvar skin become pale or white, thin, and slightly crinkly, sometimes described as looking like cigarette paper. The affected skin tears and bruises easily, and over time scarring can change the shape of the vulva — for example, the inner labia shrinking or fusing, or the opening narrowing. It most often affects women after the menopause, but can occur at any age.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't fully understood, but it is linked to the immune system — the body's defences mistakenly attack the skin — and it tends to run in families, alongside other autoimmune conditions. Let it be said plainly: lichen sclerosus is **not** an infection, it is **not** contagious, it cannot be passed to a partner, and it is **not** caused by poor hygiene or washing too little.
**Signs to watch for:** Intense itching (often the worst symptom), soreness and burning, white or pale patches, skin that splits or bleeds with gentle contact, and pain during sex or when passing urine if cracks are present. Some people have visible changes with little itching at all.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often by the appearance of the skin on examination. A skin biopsy — a tiny sample taken under local anaesthetic — confirms it, and is important if the diagnosis is uncertain or patches become thickened, lumpy, or ulcerated.
**How it's treated:** The main treatment is a strong (potent) topical steroid ointment, used exactly as directed — typically daily at first, then reduced to a maintenance schedule. Steroids on delicate skin worry many people, but here they are the correct, well-proven treatment, safe used as prescribed — untreated lichen sclerosus does far more damage than the ointment. Plain moisturisers help comfort, and established scarring can't be reversed, which is why starting treatment early matters.
**Can it be prevented?:** It can't be prevented, but its damage largely can be — with consistent treatment and follow-up. There is a small long-term risk of skin cancer developing in affected skin, so keep review appointments, continue maintenance treatment even when you feel fine, and report any lump, sore, or changing patch promptly so it can be biopsied.

## Bartholin's cyst and abscess
**Summary:** The Bartholin's glands sit either side of the vaginal opening and make lubricating fluid. If the duct blocks, fluid collects into a cyst; if the cyst gets infected it becomes an abscess — painful, swollen, and in need of same-day care.
**Image:** images/bartholins-cyst.jpg
**What it is:** A Bartholin's cyst is a fluid-filled swelling that forms when the gland's tiny duct becomes blocked. Most cysts are painless and may be noticed only as a soft lump on one side of the vaginal opening; some disappear on their own. An abscess is a cyst that has become infected — a pocket of pus that is painful, red, and tender. A very painful swelling with fever, or pain so severe you can't sit, is an emergency: call your local emergency number or get emergency care the same day.
**Why it happens:** The duct can block because of thickened mucus, swelling from irritation or infection, or for no clear reason. Abscesses develop when bacteria infect the trapped fluid; the germs involved are often ordinary skin and bowel bacteria, though sexually transmitted infections are occasionally responsible.
**Signs to watch for:** A cyst: a soft, painless lump low on one side of the vaginal opening. An abscess: a lump that becomes increasingly painful, tense, and red over a few days, with throbbing pain, trouble sitting, pain during sex, and possibly fever and feeling unwell.
**How doctors confirm it:** By examining the area — the position and feel of the swelling are usually enough to tell a cyst from an abscess. If an abscess is drained, a sample of the fluid may be tested. In women over about 40, or after the menopause, a sample of tissue from the lump is often biopsied to be safe, because a rare gland cancer can look the same.
**How it's treated:** Small, painless cysts often need no treatment beyond warm baths (sitz baths) a few times a day, which can help the duct open and drain. Antibiotics alone don't fix an abscess; it needs drainage, done under local anaesthetic, sometimes with a small catheter left in place for a few weeks so the duct heals open, or a procedure that stitches the gland open (marsupialisation). If cysts or abscesses keep returning, a specialist may suggest removing the gland.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably — duct blockages aren't caused by anything you did or didn't do. Treating a new painful swelling early, rather than waiting, makes treatment simpler and less painful.

## Genital warts
**Summary:** Genital warts are small fleshy growths on the vulva and around the anus, caused by common low-risk types of HPV — different types from the ones linked to cancer. They are treatable, often clear on their own, and the HPV vaccine covers the main wart-causing types.
**Image:** images/genital-warts.jpg
**What it is:** Genital warts are soft, flesh-coloured growths that may be single or clustered, flat or slightly raised like tiny cauliflower florets. They result from infection of the skin with certain types of human papillomavirus (HPV), a very common virus passed on by skin-to-skin sexual contact. The types that cause warts are classed as low-risk: they do not cause cervical or vulvar cancer — those are caused by different, high-risk HPV types, which the cervix file's HPV entry covers.
**Why it happens:** Infection happens through direct skin contact during sexual activity, often long before any wart appears — the gap can be weeks, months, or longer, so a wart doesn't mean a recent exposure or a partner's infidelity. Many people carry the virus without visible warts and can still pass it on. Condoms reduce the risk but don't remove it completely, because the virus lives on skin a condom doesn't cover.
**Signs to watch for:** Small bumps or clusters on the labia, around the vaginal opening, or around the anus. Warts are usually painless, though they can itch or bleed if rubbed. Any lump that is pigmented, hard, ulcerated, or keeps growing should be examined promptly rather than assumed to be a wart.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by looking — warts have a characteristic appearance. If the diagnosis is uncertain, a growth looks unusual, or treatment isn't working, a small biopsy settles it. A check for other sexually transmitted infections is often offered at the same visit.
**How it's treated:** Options include prescription creams you apply yourself, freezing (cryotherapy) done in the clinic, and minor removal procedures. Treatment removes the visible warts but doesn't eliminate the virus from the skin, so warts can return in the first few months — this is reactivation, not reinfection. The body's immune system usually clears or suppresses the virus over time, and many warts disappear on their own without any treatment.
**Can it be prevented?:** The HPV vaccine given before exposure covers the main wart-causing types as well as the main cancer-causing types, and it can still benefit people vaccinated later. Condoms lower the risk of transmission, and not smoking helps the immune system clear the virus.

## Vulvar cancer
**Summary:** Vulvar cancer is rare, but it does happen — usually as a slowly growing lump, sore, or changed patch of skin. Any change that persists should be examined and biopsied, because found early, this cancer is highly treatable.
**Image:** images/vulvar-cancer.svg
**What it is:** Vulvar cancer means abnormal cells growing out of control in the skin of the vulva. Most cases occur in older women, but it can occur younger. There are two main routes to it: one linked to high-risk HPV infection, and one arising in skin affected by lichen sclerosus. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Longstanding high-risk HPV infection is one cause, longstanding untreated lichen sclerosus the other. Smoking and a weakened immune system add risk. It is not contagious, and not caused by poor hygiene.
**Signs to watch for:** A lump or thickened patch, a sore or ulcer that doesn't heal, a patch that changes colour or texture, persistent itching that doesn't respond to treatment, bleeding, or pain. The key rule: any change still present after a few weeks deserves examination, not watchful waiting.
**How doctors confirm it:** Examination, then a biopsy — a small sample of the changed skin — the only way to be sure. If cancer is confirmed, scans (CT, MRI, or PET) check for spread, and the groin lymph nodes are assessed.
**How it's treated:** Surgery to remove the cancer is the main treatment, sometimes including removal or sampling of groin lymph nodes. Radiotherapy, sometimes with chemotherapy, may be added or used instead, depending on size and spread. Early-stage disease often needs surgery alone.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. HPV vaccination protects against the HPV-linked type; treating lichen sclerosus and keeping its follow-up appointments lowers risk on the other route. Knowing your vulva's normal appearance — and getting persistent changes biopsied early — is the strongest protection.
