## Acid reflux / GERD
**Summary:** Stomach acid flows back up into the food pipe (oesophagus), causing heartburn — a burning feeling behind the breastbone that, despite the name, has nothing to do with the heart. Occasional reflux is very common; when it happens often or starts to damage the food pipe, it's called GERD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease).
**Image:** images/acid-reflux.png
**What it is:** A ring of muscle at the bottom of the food pipe — the lower oesophageal sphincter — normally acts as a one-way valve, letting food in and keeping acid down. If it relaxes too often or weakens, acid splashes back up. Brief episodes are normal, especially after a large meal. GERD is diagnosed when reflux causes troublesome symptoms or complications such as inflammation of the food pipe (oesophagitis). This entry covers reflux from the stomach-and-food-pipe angle; if your main symptoms are in the throat or voice — hoarseness, throat clearing, a lump feeling — see the throat page as well.
**Why it happens:** Anything that raises pressure in the stomach or relaxes the valve: large or late meals, excess weight, pregnancy, smoking, alcohol, and fatty, spicy, or acidic foods (triggers vary from person to person). A hiatus hernia — where the top of the stomach pushes up through the diaphragm — makes reflux more likely. Some medicines also relax the valve.
**Signs to watch for:** Heartburn after meals or when lying down or bending over, an acid or bitter taste in the mouth, bringing food back up (regurgitation), and sometimes a chronic cough or worse symptoms at night. Chest pain should never be assumed to be reflux — pain that is heavy, crushing, or spreads to the arm or jaw could be the heart, so call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often from the symptom pattern alone, sometimes with a trial of acid-reducing medicine. Endoscopy (a thin camera passed down the throat) checks for inflammation or damage; longer acid-monitoring tests are sometimes used if the diagnosis is unclear or surgery is being considered.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider start with lifestyle: smaller meals, not eating late, avoiding personal triggers, losing excess weight, and raising the head of the bed for night-time symptoms. Medicines include antacids for quick relief and stronger acid-reducing classes (H2 blockers and proton pump inhibitors) taken for a defined course. Surgery to strengthen the valve is an option for selected people with severe or long-term reflux.
**Can it be prevented?:** Episodes can be greatly reduced — keeping a healthy weight, not smoking, eating earlier and smaller evening meals, and knowing your triggers all help. Long-standing untreated reflux can, in some people, change the lining of the food pipe (a condition called Barrett's oesophagus), which raises cancer risk slightly and needs monitoring, so frequent heartburn is worth discussing with a doctor rather than just enduring.

## Gastritis
**Summary:** Gastritis is inflammation of the stomach lining. It can come on suddenly or develop slowly over years, and it's usually painless or causes a dull upper-belly discomfort and nausea rather than dramatic symptoms.
**Image:** images/gastritis.jpg
**What it is:** The stomach lining is protected from its own acid by a mucus layer. When that protection is damaged or the lining is irritated, acid reaches the sensitive tissue beneath and the lining becomes inflamed — red, swollen, and sometimes eroded. Gastritis can affect part of the stomach or all of it. Over years, ongoing inflammation can thin the lining (called atrophic gastritis), which reduces acid and vitamin B12 absorption and slightly raises stomach cancer risk.
**Why it happens:** The two big causes are infection with H. pylori bacteria — a very common infection, often picked up in childhood — and regular use of NSAID painkillers (such as ibuprofen-type medicines), which strip away the lining's protection. Heavy alcohol use, severe illness or injury, and autoimmune conditions (where the immune system attacks the lining) are other causes.
**Signs to watch for:** Many people have no symptoms at all. When present: gnawing or burning discomfort in the upper belly, nausea, bloating, feeling full after small amounts, and loss of appetite. If the inflamed lining bleeds, vomit may contain blood or look like coffee grounds, and stools may turn black and tarry — that is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A test for H. pylori (breath, stool, or blood test) is often the first step. Endoscopy lets the doctor see the lining directly and take small tissue samples (biopsy), which confirms the type and cause of gastritis. Blood tests can show anaemia from slow bleeding or low vitamin B12.
**How it's treated:** Treatment targets the cause. H. pylori is eradicated with a combination of antibiotics plus an acid-reducing medicine, taken exactly as prescribed — stopping early lets the bacteria survive. NSAID-related gastritis usually means switching pain relief (with the doctor's help — don't just stop prescribed medicines) and adding acid-reducing medicines while the lining heals. Reducing alcohol and treating any underlying condition also matter.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Using NSAID painkillers only as directed, at the lowest effective dose, and asking about stomach protection if you need them regularly all lower the risk. Limiting alcohol helps. H. pylori spreads through close contact and contaminated food or water, so ordinary hygiene helps, though infection is hard to avoid entirely in regions where it's very common.

## Stomach ulcers (peptic ulcers)
**Summary:** A stomach ulcer is an open sore in the stomach lining (ulcers in the first part of the small intestine, just beyond the stomach, are close cousins and are covered here too). Most are caused by H. pylori bacteria or by NSAID painkillers — not, as once believed, by stress or spicy food alone.
**Image:** images/stomach-ulcers.png
**What it is:** Normally, mucus shields the stomach wall from acid. When that shield fails in one spot, acid digests a raw crater into the lining. Ulcers range from a few millimetres to a few centimetres across. Left untreated, an ulcer can eat deeper — bleeding if it reaches a blood vessel, or bursting through the wall entirely (perforation), which is a surgical emergency.
**Why it happens:** The two big causes are H. pylori infection — present in the stomachs of a large share of the world's adults, though only a minority develop ulcers — and regular NSAID painkiller use, which blocks the chemicals that maintain the mucus shield; the risk climbs in older adults and when NSAIDs are combined with blood thinners or steroids. Smoking slows healing and makes ulcers more likely to return. Stress and spicy food can aggravate pain but rarely create an ulcer by themselves.
**Signs to watch for:** A gnawing or burning pain in the upper belly is the classic symptom — with stomach ulcers it often worsens with food; with duodenal ulcers it may ease with food and bite back a few hours later or at night. Bloating, nausea, and feeling full quickly are common too, and some ulcers cause no pain at all until they bleed. Emergency signs — call your local emergency number: vomiting blood or material like coffee grounds, black tarry stools, or sudden severe abdominal pain (possible perforation).
**How doctors confirm it:** Endoscopy is the key test — the ulcer is seen directly and biopsies can check for H. pylori and, in the stomach, rule out cancer. Non-invasive H. pylori breath or stool tests are also used, including after treatment to confirm the infection is gone. Blood tests check for anaemia if bleeding is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider: acid-reducing medicines (usually a proton pump inhibitor) for several weeks to let the ulcer heal; if H. pylori is found, a course of combination antibiotics alongside the acid-reducer to eradicate it — finishing the full course is essential. NSAIDs are stopped or switched where possible, sometimes with a stomach-protecting medicine if they're still needed. Most ulcers heal with medicines alone; surgery is now uncommon and kept for complications such as uncontrolled bleeding or perforation.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely. Eradicating H. pylori prevents most recurrences. Use NSAID painkillers only as directed, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, and ask your doctor about stomach protection if you need them regularly — especially if you've had an ulcer before. Not smoking and moderating alcohol also help the lining stay intact.

## Indigestion (dyspepsia)
**Summary:** Indigestion is a pattern of symptoms — upper-belly discomfort, fullness, bloating, or burning after eating — rather than a single disease. It's extremely common and usually harmless, but certain "alarm features" mean it needs proper investigation rather than reassurance.
**Image:** images/indigestion.svg
**What it is:** Doctors use the word dyspepsia for pain or discomfort centred in the upper abdomen, often with early fullness during a meal, lingering fullness afterwards, bloating, belching, and nausea. In many people no structural problem is found even after testing; this is called functional dyspepsia, and it relates to how sensitively the stomach senses stretching and acid, and how it empties. In others, indigestion is the presenting symptom of reflux, gastritis, or an ulcer — covered in the other entries on this page.
**Why it happens:** Overeating, eating quickly, fatty or spicy food, coffee, alcohol, and fizzy drinks provoke symptoms in many people. NSAID painkillers, some antibiotics, and iron supplements can irritate the stomach. Stress and poor sleep lower the threshold — the stomach genuinely feels things more. Underlying causes such as H. pylori infection, reflux, or an ulcer explain a proportion of cases.
**Signs to watch for:** Typical indigestion comes and goes with meals and settles between episodes. The alarm features that change things — and mean seeing a doctor promptly for investigation, usually including endoscopy — are: unintentional weight loss, anaemia or unusual paleness/tiredness, trouble or pain when swallowing, persistent vomiting, vomiting blood or black stools, and new persistent symptoms starting at an older age (commonly used cut-offs are around 55–60). Indigestion with any of these should not be self-treated for months.
**How doctors confirm it:** History and examination first, including a review of medicines and painkiller use. A test for H. pylori (breath or stool) is a common early step, and treating it settles symptoms for some people. Endoscopy is arranged when alarm features are present, when symptoms persist despite treatment, or when they start at an older age. Blood tests check for anaemia.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include the simple measures: smaller, slower meals, easing off personal triggers, not eating late, and reviewing irritating medicines. If H. pylori is found, it's eradicated. Acid-reducing medicines (H2 blockers or proton pump inhibitors) are the main drug option; other medicines that help the stomach empty or calm its sensitivity are sometimes tried for functional dyspepsia. Just as important is a clear plan: which symptoms should settle, over what period, and what should trigger a return visit.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely — sensitive stomachs run in families — but episodes can be made rarer and milder: moderate meal sizes, fewer late meals, moderate alcohol and coffee, not smoking, and care with NSAID painkillers. The most important "prevention" is not missing the serious causes: take the alarm features seriously rather than masking them with long-term self-treatment.

## Stomach cancer
**Summary:** Cancer that starts in the stomach lining. Its early symptoms are vague and easily mistaken for ordinary indigestion, which is why it is often found late — and why persistent or alarm symptoms deserve a camera test rather than months of reassurance.
**Image:** images/stomach-cancer.png
**What it is:** Most stomach cancers are adenocarcinomas, arising from the gland cells of the lining, usually after years of chronic inflammation have gradually changed the lining's cells. It is among the more common cancers worldwide, with rates varying a lot by region — higher in parts of East Asia and Eastern Europe, lower in many Western countries. For the broader picture of cancer, screening, and treatment, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Long-standing H. pylori infection is the single biggest risk factor — chronic inflammation from the bacteria drives the slow change from gastritis to pre-cancerous lining changes to cancer in a small minority of infected people. Other risks: a diet heavy in salted, smoked, and preserved foods (and low in fresh fruit and vegetables), smoking, heavy alcohol use, obesity, and a family history of stomach cancer. Certain conditions — pernicious anaemia (autoimmune gastritis) and previous stomach surgery — also raise risk.
**Signs to watch for:** Early on, often nothing specific — indigestion-like discomfort, feeling full quickly, mild nausea, and loss of appetite that could easily be blamed on ordinary dyspepsia. Later signs include unexplained weight loss, persistent upper-belly pain, trouble swallowing (if the tumour is near the top of the stomach), vomiting, anaemia with tiredness and paleness, and black stools from bleeding. Any of these, persisting, means prompt medical assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** Endoscopy with biopsies is the key test — tissue samples give the definite diagnosis. CT scans (sometimes with PET) then show how far it has spread, and blood tests assess general health and anaemia. Because early symptoms mimic indigestion, the decision to scope persistent symptoms — especially with alarm features or in older adults — is what catches it earliest.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend heavily on stage. Surgery to remove part or all of the stomach is the main curative option for cancer that hasn't spread widely, often combined with chemotherapy before or after. For advanced cancer, chemotherapy and, in selected cases, targeted medicines or immunotherapy can slow the disease and ease symptoms. Care involves a team — surgeon, cancer specialist, dietitian, and gastroenterologist — and nutritional support matters greatly after stomach surgery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treating H. pylori infection reduces risk, particularly before long-standing lining changes develop. Eating less salt-preserved and smoked food and more fresh produce, not smoking, moderating alcohol, and keeping a healthy weight all help. There is no general screening programme in most countries, though some high-incidence regions do screen — which is another reason persistent indigestion with alarm features should always be checked.
