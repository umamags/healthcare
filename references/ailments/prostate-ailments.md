## Benign prostatic enlargement (BPH)
**Summary:** As men get older, the prostate commonly grows larger. This non-cancerous growth — benign prostatic enlargement, or BPH — squeezes the urine tube and causes urinary symptoms. It is not cancer, and having it does not turn into cancer.
**Image:** images/bph.jpg
**What it is:** The prostate sits just under the bladder and wraps around the urethra, the tube that carries urine out. From middle age onwards it slowly enlarges in most men. Because the growing tissue presses on the tube and the bladder outlet, urine has to push through a narrower passage. "Benign" means non-cancerous. BPH is extremely common with age and is a separate condition from prostate cancer — the two can exist in the same prostate, but one does not cause the other.
**Why it happens:** Enlargement is driven by ageing and male hormones. The prostate has two growth phases — a small one at puberty and a slow, steady one from about the mid-twenties onwards that speeds up in later life. Family history plays a part: men whose fathers or brothers needed treatment for enlargement are more likely to develop significant symptoms themselves.
**Signs to watch for:** A weaker, slower stream; straining or waiting for the flow to start; stopping and starting; needing to pass urine more often, including several trips at night; a feeling that the bladder hasn't emptied properly; and sometimes urgency or dribbling at the end. Symptoms range from a minor nuisance to a major disruption of sleep and daily life.
**How doctors confirm it:** A symptom review and examination, including a digital rectal exam to feel the size and surface of the prostate. Urine tests check for infection or blood, and blood tests may include PSA and kidney function. An ultrasound can measure prostate size and how much urine is left in the bladder after voiding. A flow test — passing urine into a measuring device — is sometimes used.
**How it's treated:** Mild symptoms are often managed with watchful waiting: regular review, drinking less in the evening, and cutting back on caffeine and alcohol. Medicines doctors commonly consider include tablets that relax the muscle in the prostate and bladder neck so urine flows more easily, and tablets that slowly shrink the prostate over months. If symptoms are severe, damage the bladder or kidneys, or medicines aren't enough, surgery to trim or remove the inner part of the prostate — usually through the urine tube, with no external cut — is an option.
**Can it be prevented?:** The enlargement itself can't reliably be prevented, but its impact can be reduced: staying active, keeping a healthy weight, and seeing a doctor about new urinary symptoms rather than just accepting them. **The emergency to know:** if you cannot pass urine at all, that is a medical emergency — call your local emergency number or get urgent care, as the bladder needs draining with a catheter.

## Prostatitis
**Summary:** Prostatitis means the prostate is inflamed. Sometimes a bacterial infection is the cause; often no infection is found. The acute form arrives suddenly with fever and feeling very unwell, while chronic forms cause ongoing pelvic pain and urinary symptoms that are harder to pin down and treat.
**Image:** images/prostatitis.jpg
**What it is:** Doctors group prostatitis into types. Acute bacterial prostatitis is a sudden infection of the gland. Chronic bacterial prostatitis is a lower-grade infection that keeps returning. The most common type of all is chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome, where pain and urinary symptoms persist but no infection can be found — the cause likely involves a mix of pelvic floor muscle tension, nerve sensitivity, and past inflammation rather than germs.
**Why it happens:** Acute infection usually comes from bacteria that normally live in the bowel or urinary tract reaching the prostate, sometimes after a urinary infection or a prostate procedure. Chronic pelvic pain types are less well understood — which is frustrating for patients, because it means there is often no single test that explains the symptoms and no single treatment that fixes them.
**Signs to watch for:** Acute prostatitis causes fever, chills, and feeling generally very unwell, with pain in the pelvis, groin, or lower back, pain or burning when passing urine, and needing to go often and urgently. Chronic types cause a dull ache in the perineum (between the scrotum and back passage), the testicles, or the tip of the penis, urinary frequency or hesitancy, and sometimes pain after ejaculation. Symptoms often come and go.
**How doctors confirm it:** A review of symptoms, urine tests, and a gentle examination — in acute prostatitis the prostate feels tender and swollen, so the exam is kept brief. Blood tests may show infection, and chronic cases sometimes involve testing urine samples taken before and after prostate massage. Scans are used if an abscess or another cause is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Acute bacterial prostatitis needs antibiotics promptly, sometimes given in hospital first, and the course is usually continued for several weeks because the gland is hard for antibiotics to reach. Chronic bacterial infection needs a longer course still. Where no infection is found, treatment focuses on pain relief, medicines that relax the prostate and bladder neck, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and managing flare-ups — honest expectations matter: improvement is often gradual rather than a complete cure.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably. Treating urinary infections promptly and staying well hydrated may help. **The emergency to know:** sudden prostate or pelvic pain with high fever and feeling very unwell needs same-day emergency care — call your local emergency number.

## Prostate cancer
**Summary:** Prostate cancer is a growth of abnormal cells in the prostate. It often causes no symptoms at all in its early stages, and many prostate cancers grow so slowly that they are monitored rather than treated straight away. Finding and managing it well depends on testing decisions made together with a doctor.
**Image:** images/prostate-cancer.svg
**What it is:** Cancer cells develop in the prostate tissue. Some prostate cancers are aggressive, but many are slow-growing and would never cause harm within a man's lifetime — this is why detecting one does not automatically mean immediate treatment. For the broader picture of cancer, screening, and treatment, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Age is the strongest risk factor — risk rises steeply in older men. Family history matters: a father or brother with prostate cancer raises risk, and inherited gene changes (including BRCA2) account for a small share of cases. Men of Black African or Caribbean ancestry have a higher risk. No single lifestyle cause has been proven.
**Signs to watch for:** Early prostate cancer usually causes none — this is the key difficulty. Urinary symptoms such as a weak stream or night-time trips are far more often caused by benign enlargement than by cancer, but they are still worth reporting. Later signs can include blood in the urine or semen, new erectile problems, and — if the cancer has spread — persistent bone pain, most often in the back, hips, or ribs.
**How doctors confirm it:** The PSA blood test measures a protein the prostate makes, but it is **not a cancer test by itself**: PSA rises with benign enlargement and with infection too, and it can be normal in a man who has prostate cancer. For that reason, whether to have a PSA test is a personal decision to discuss with a doctor, weighing your age, family history, and preferences. If testing is done and the result or examination raises concern, an MRI scan of the prostate is now commonly done before any biopsy, and helps target the biopsy — taking small tissue samples — to suspicious areas.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on how advanced and aggressive the cancer is: active surveillance (regular PSA tests, scans, and repeat biopsies, treating only if the cancer changes) for low-risk cancers; surgery to remove the prostate; radiotherapy, sometimes combined with hormone treatment that lowers testosterone; and, for cancer that has spread, hormone treatment and chemotherapy. The aim is to match treatment to the cancer's actual risk, avoiding both under-treatment and unnecessary side effects.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no proven way to prevent prostate cancer. Staying active, keeping a healthy weight, and not smoking support general health. The practical protection is awareness: know your family history, discuss PSA testing with your doctor rather than assuming it is or isn't needed, and report blood in urine or semen or new, persistent bone pain promptly.
