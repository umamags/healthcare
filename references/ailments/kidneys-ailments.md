## Chronic kidney disease
**Summary:** Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a gradual, usually permanent loss of the kidneys' filtering ability. It's most often caused by diabetes or high blood pressure, and it's usually silent until advanced — which is why regular blood and urine tests matter for people at risk.
**Image:** images/chronic-kidney-disease.png
**What it is:** Doctors describe CKD in stages 1 to 5, based mainly on eGFR, the blood-test estimate of how well the kidneys filter. In the early stages the kidneys are slightly damaged but still filter reasonably well, and damage may show only as protein leaking into the urine. Through the middle stages filtering falls further and complications begin. At stage 5 (kidney failure) the kidneys can barely filter at all, and waste and fluid build up in the body.
**Why it happens:** Diabetes and high blood pressure together cause most CKD worldwide — high blood sugar and high pressure gradually damage the tiny filters (nephrons). Other causes include inflammation of the filters, inherited conditions such as polycystic kidney disease, repeated infections, long-term blockage of urine flow, and long-term heavy use of certain medicines. Ageing also lowers filtering somewhat.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none until the disease is advanced — people often feel completely well while their kidneys quietly decline. Later signs can include persistent tiredness, swollen ankles, feet, or face, foamy urine (from protein leak), urinating more or less than usual, poor appetite, nausea, itching, and pale skin from anaemia.
**How doctors confirm it:** Blood creatinine used to calculate eGFR, a urine test for protein or blood, and a blood pressure check — repeated over at least a few months, because CKD means the changes persist. An ultrasound shows kidney size and rules out blockages. Doctors also look for the underlying cause, sometimes with more blood tests or, rarely, a kidney biopsy.
**How it's treated:** There's no way to restore lost filters, so treatment aims to slow the decline and manage complications. Approaches doctors commonly consider include blood-pressure medicines (certain classes also protect the kidneys directly), tight blood-sugar control in diabetes, less salt in the diet (with protein and phosphorus adjustments in later stages), medicines for anaemia and fluid build-up, and regular monitoring. At kidney failure, the options discussed are dialysis — filtering the blood artificially — or a kidney transplant, planned carefully with the patient and family.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly, yes. Controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, eating less salt, not smoking, staying a healthy weight, and avoiding frequent heavy use of NSAID painkillers without medical advice all protect the kidneys. For people with diabetes or high blood pressure, regular kidney screening catches damage early, when it's most manageable.

## Kidney stones
**Summary:** Kidney stones are hard crystals that form inside the kidneys from minerals in the urine. Small ones may pass unnoticed, but a stone moving into the narrow tube to the bladder causes some of the most severe pain known — and a stone that blocks urine flow while infection builds behind it is an emergency.
**Image:** images/kidney-stones.png
**What it is:** Stones form when urine contains high levels of certain minerals that crystallise, or too little fluid to keep them dissolved. The commonest type is calcium-based (calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate); others include uric acid stones, struvite stones (linked to infection), and rarer cystine stones. Stones can sit quietly in the kidney for years, or travel down the ureter — the tube to the bladder — where they can partly or completely block urine flow.
**Why it happens:** Not drinking enough fluid is the biggest everyday factor, because concentrated urine crystallises more easily. Diet matters too: lots of salt, lots of animal protein, and (for some people) oxalate-rich foods raise risk. Other factors include a family history of stones, obesity, certain bowel and metabolic conditions, some medicines, and hot climates or heavy sweating without enough fluid replacement.
**Signs to watch for:** The classic sign is sudden, intense pain in the side or back below the ribs, often coming in waves and spreading toward the groin. There may also be nausea and vomiting, blood in the urine (pink, red, or brown), and a frequent urge to urinate. A stone plus fever or chills is an emergency — a blocked, infected kidney can become dangerous fast: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A urine test (often showing blood), blood tests including creatinine to check the kidneys are coping, and imaging. A CT scan maps stones precisely and is the usual test in an emergency; ultrasound is often used first, especially in pregnancy or to avoid radiation. A stone that passes may be caught and analysed to identify its type.
**How it's treated:** Small stones often pass on their own with pain relief (including NSAID or stronger pain medicines) and good fluid intake; a medicine that relaxes the ureter is an approach doctors commonly consider to help a stone pass. Larger or stuck stones are the urologist's territory: options include shock-wave lithotripsy (sound waves break the stone up), ureteroscopy (a thin scope passed up to remove or laser the stone), and, for very large stones, a small operation through the back. An infected, blocked kidney is drained urgently with a tube or stent.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, yes. Drinking enough fluid to keep urine pale is the single most effective step. Eating less salt and moderate amounts of animal protein helps most people; people with calcium stones are usually advised to keep normal calcium in the diet rather than cut it out. If stones recur, doctors may analyse the stone and urine chemistry and suggest a specific diet or preventive medicine.

## Kidney infection (pyelonephritis)
**Summary:** A kidney infection happens when bacteria — usually from a bladder infection — climb up to one or both kidneys. Unlike a simple bladder infection, it makes you properly ill with fever and back pain, and untreated it can lead to sepsis and permanent kidney damage.
**Image:** images/kidney-infection.png
**What it is:** Most urinary infections start in the bladder, where they cause burning urination and frequent trips to the toilet but rarely make someone feel seriously unwell. If the bacteria travel up the ureters to the kidneys, the infection becomes pyelonephritis: the kidney tissue itself is infected and inflamed. It usually affects one kidney, though both can be involved.
**Why it happens:** The usual cause is bacteria from the bowel (most often E. coli) entering the urinary tract. Infections climb more easily when urine flow is blocked or slowed — by a kidney stone, pregnancy, an enlarged prostate, or a urinary catheter — and when the immune system is weakened, for example by diabetes. Women get urinary infections more often because the urethra is shorter.
**Signs to watch for:** Fever and chills, pain in the side or back below the ribs (often one-sided), feeling generally ill, nausea and vomiting, plus the bladder symptoms — burning when urinating, needing to go often, cloudy or bloody urine. High fever with severe illness, confusion, or inability to keep fluids down needs urgent care: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A urine test looking for infection and blood, and a urine culture to identify the bacteria and which antibiotics work. Blood tests check inflammation and kidney function (creatinine/eGFR), and a blood culture if sepsis is suspected. Ultrasound or a CT scan is used if there's a stone, blockage, pregnancy, or the infection isn't settling as expected.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics are the mainstay — taken by mouth for milder cases, or given through a drip in hospital if you're vomiting, very unwell, pregnant, or not improving. Rest and fluids help, and pain and fever are treated with fever-reducing medicines. If a stone or blockage is feeding the infection, a urologist may need to drain the kidney urgently. Most people recover fully with prompt treatment.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treating bladder infections promptly, drinking enough fluid, and not holding urine for long periods all help. Managing blockages (such as treating a stone or prostate enlargement) lowers the risk of recurrence. People with repeated infections should discuss a prevention plan with their doctor rather than keeping leftover antibiotics at home.

## Acute kidney injury
**Summary:** Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a sudden fall in kidney function over hours or days — the kidneys abruptly filter far less than they should. It often happens during another serious illness, and the kidneys frequently recover if the cause is found and treated quickly.
**Image:** images/acute-kidney-injury.svg
**What it is:** AKI isn't one disease but a sudden failure of filtering with several possible triggers. Waste products and fluid build up quickly, blood pressure and salt balance go off track, and other organs can suffer. It ranges from a mild, test-only abnormality to complete shutdown requiring temporary dialysis. Many cases happen in hospital, in people already ill for another reason.
**Why it happens:** The commonest trigger is reduced blood flow to the kidneys — from severe dehydration (vomiting, diarrhoea, poor fluid intake), major blood loss, heart failure, or sepsis (overwhelming infection). Direct kidney injury can come from some medicines, including NSAID painkillers and certain antibiotics, especially in people who are already dehydrated or have CKD. A third mechanism is sudden blockage of urine flow, for example from a stone or a greatly enlarged prostate.
**Signs to watch for:** Passing much less urine than usual (or none) is the clearest sign, along with swelling of the legs or face, breathlessness from fluid build-up, nausea, and unusual tiredness or confusion. But AKI can also be completely silent at first and show up only on blood tests — one reason doctors monitor kidney function closely during serious illness. Little or no urine with swelling or breathlessness is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Blood creatinine and eGFR, checked urgently and compared with previous results — a sudden rise in creatinine is the hallmark. Urine tests, blood pressure and fluid-balance assessment, and blood tests for salts like potassium help judge severity. An ultrasound rules out a blocked urinary tract.
**How it's treated:** Treatment is about the cause: replacing fluids carefully if dehydration is the problem (and the opposite — removing fluid — if the body is overloaded), stopping or adjusting medicines that strain the kidneys, treating sepsis with antibiotics, and relieving any blockage, which may need a urologist. Salt imbalances are corrected, and in severe cases temporary dialysis supports the body while the kidneys recover. A nephrologist usually supervises hospital care.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, partly. Staying hydrated during illness with vomiting, diarrhoea, or fever helps, as does being cautious with NSAID painkillers when you're dehydrated or already have kidney problems — ask a pharmacist or doctor first. If you take blood-pressure or diabetes medicines, ask your doctor whether to pause them temporarily during a severe dehydrating illness; many doctors advise this. Treating infections and blood loss promptly also protects the kidneys.
