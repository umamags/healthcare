## Uterine fibroids
**Summary:** Fibroids are benign (non-cancerous) growths of muscle and fibrous tissue in or on the wall of the womb. They are very common, and many cause no symptoms at all and never need treatment — they are often found by chance on a scan done for another reason.
**Image:** images/fibroids.png
**What it is:** Fibroids grow from the muscle layer of the womb. They can be as small as a seed or as large as a grapefruit, and there may be one or several. Depending on where they sit — inside the cavity, within the wall, or on the outer surface — they can press on the bladder or bowel, distort the womb's shape, or make periods heavy. They are not cancer, and they almost never turn into cancer.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't known, but fibroids grow under the influence of the hormones oestrogen and progesterone, which is why they appear during the reproductive years and usually shrink after the menopause. They tend to run in families, and they are more common in some ethnic groups. Having children seems to lower the risk slightly.
**Signs to watch for:** Many women have none. When symptoms occur they include heavy or prolonged periods, pelvic pressure or a feeling of fullness, needing to urinate often, constipation, lower back pain, pain during sex, and a visibly swollen lower belly. Heavy bleeding over time can cause iron-deficiency anaemia, with tiredness, breathlessness, and pale skin. Bleeding that soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for 2 or more hours is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic examination can suggest an enlarged or irregular womb. A pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound is the usual confirming test and shows the size and position of fibroids; MRI is sometimes used to map them in detail before a procedure.
**How it's treated:** Fibroids that cause no problems usually just need monitoring — "watch and wait." Medicines can ease heavy bleeding (such as tranexamic acid or anti-inflammatory painkillers) or shrink fibroids temporarily (hormonal medicines). Procedures include uterine artery embolisation, which blocks the fibroids' blood supply, and focused ultrasound treatment. Surgery ranges from removing just the fibroids (myomectomy) to removing the womb (hysterectomy), which is the only permanent cure. The choice depends on symptoms, fibroid size and position, and whether future pregnancy is wanted.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no proven way to prevent fibroids. Keeping a healthy weight may help slightly, since risk is a little higher with excess weight. The practical step is not prevention but awareness: heavy periods are worth investigating rather than enduring.

## Endometriosis
**Summary:** Endometriosis is a long-term condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows in places outside the womb, most often on the ovaries, bowel, and the lining of the pelvis. It can cause severe period pain, ongoing pelvic pain, and difficulty getting pregnant — and it is often diagnosed late, after years of symptoms.
**Image:** images/endometriosis.jpg
**What it is:** The misplaced tissue behaves like womb lining: it thickens and bleeds with each cycle, but the blood has nowhere to escape. This triggers inflammation, scarring, and sometimes cysts on the ovaries (sometimes called "chocolate cysts" — those are covered in the ovaries file; this entry is the full picture of the condition). Organs can stick together with scar tissue (adhesions). It is not an infection, it is not contagious, and it is not caused by anything the person did.
**Why it happens:** The cause isn't fully understood. One leading idea is that some menstrual blood flows backwards through the fallopian tubes into the pelvis, carrying lining cells that implant and grow. Genetics plays a part — it often runs in families — and immune and hormonal factors are likely involved.
**Signs to watch for:** Period pain that is much worse than "normal" cramps and interferes with daily life, pain during or after sex, pain when emptying the bladder or bowel (especially around periods), heavy periods, ongoing pelvic or lower back pain, fatigue, and difficulty conceiving. Symptoms vary a lot and don't reliably match how much disease is present — small amounts can hurt a great deal. Honest acknowledgement matters here: because period pain is often dismissed, diagnosis is frequently delayed by years, and pushing for referral is reasonable.
**How doctors confirm it:** The doctor will ask about the pattern of pain and may do a pelvic examination and an ultrasound, which can spot ovarian cysts and some deep disease. The only way to be completely certain has traditionally been a laparoscopy — keyhole surgery with a camera — which also allows tissue to be sampled; MRI can help map deep disease. Increasingly, treatment is started on a strong clinical suspicion without waiting for surgery.
**How it's treated:** There is no outright cure, but symptoms can usually be well controlled. Anti-inflammatory painkillers help pain. Hormonal treatments — the combined pill, progestogens, or medicines that temporarily switch off the cycle — suppress the misplaced tissue. Keyhole surgery can remove or destroy visible disease and divide adhesions, which can also help fertility. Treatment is individual and often combines approaches over time.
**Can it be prevented?:** No known prevention exists. Earlier recognition is the realistic goal: severe period pain is not something to simply accept, and getting assessed sooner can limit years of uncontrolled symptoms.

## Adenomyosis
**Summary:** Adenomyosis is a condition in which the lining of the womb grows into the muscle wall of the womb itself. It can make the womb enlarged and tender and cause heavy, painful periods — and it is easily confused with fibroids or endometriosis, which it often occurs alongside.
**Image:** images/adenomyosis.jpg
**What it is:** Normally the womb lining (endometrium) stays on the inner surface. In adenomyosis, glands from the lining are found buried within the muscle layer (myometrium). The muscle around them thickens, and the whole womb may become bulky and soft. The buried tissue still responds to hormones each month, which is thought to drive the pain and bleeding. It is benign — not cancer.
**Why it happens:** The cause is not certain. It is more common in women in their thirties and forties who have had children, and previous surgery to the womb (such as a caesarean section or curettage) may increase the risk, possibly by disturbing the boundary between lining and muscle. It frequently coexists with fibroids and endometriosis, which is one reason it is hard to pin down.
**Signs to watch for:** Heavy periods, sometimes with clots; period pain that may worsen over the years and can feel like pressure or dragging; ongoing pelvic ache; pain during sex; and a sense of pelvic fullness from an enlarged womb. Long-standing heavy bleeding can lead to iron-deficiency anaemia. As with fibroids, bleeding that soaks a pad or tampon every hour for 2 or more hours needs emergency care — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** After a pelvic examination, a transvaginal ultrasound often shows typical features — a thickened, uneven muscle wall. MRI gives the clearest picture and is used when the diagnosis is uncertain or surgery is being planned. In the past adenomyosis was only confirmed after a hysterectomy, by examining the womb under a microscope; modern imaging has changed that.
**How it's treated:** Anti-inflammatory painkillers and medicines that reduce menstrual bleeding (such as tranexamic acid) help some women. Hormonal options include a hormonal intrauterine system (coil) placed in the womb, which often reduces bleeding and pain considerably, and other hormonal medicines. If symptoms are severe and family is complete, hysterectomy is the definitive treatment. Procedures that preserve the womb are more limited than for fibroids because the disease is spread through the muscle rather than in a removable lump.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no known way to prevent adenomyosis. As with other causes of heavy periods, the useful step is early assessment: heavy, worsening periods deserve investigation rather than being put down to age.

## Endometrial polyps
**Summary:** Endometrial polyps are small, soft growths of the womb lining that project into the cavity of the womb. They are usually benign, and their commonest clue is bleeding at unexpected times — between periods, after sex, or after the menopause.
**Image:** images/endometrial-polyps.png
**What it is:** A polyp is an overgrowth of the endometrial tissue, attached to the lining by a stalk or a broader base. There may be one or several, and they range from a few millimetres to a few centimetres. Most are harmless, but a small proportion — especially in women after the menopause — contain abnormal or pre-cancerous cells, which is why removed polyps are always checked in the laboratory.
**Why it happens:** Polyps grow in response to the hormone oestrogen. They are more common as women approach and pass the menopause, and risk is higher with excess weight (body fat produces oestrogen), high blood pressure, and certain breast cancer medicines that stimulate the womb lining. Many occur for no clear reason.
**Signs to watch for:** Irregular bleeding is the hallmark: bleeding between periods, periods that are heavier or less predictable, spotting after sex, and — most importantly — any bleeding after the menopause, which should always be reported promptly. Some polyps cause no symptoms and are found on a scan done for something else. Occasionally a polyp is linked to difficulty conceiving.
**How doctors confirm it:** A transvaginal ultrasound may show a thickening or a definite polyp, sometimes with a fluid-enhanced scan for a clearer view. The definitive test is hysteroscopy — a thin camera passed through the cervix to look directly inside the womb — which allows the polyp to be seen and removed in the same procedure. Removed tissue is sent for laboratory examination.
**How it's treated:** Small polyps in younger women sometimes disappear on their own and can be watched. Usually, however, polyps that cause bleeding are removed by hysteroscopic surgery — a day-case procedure done through the cervix with no cuts to the abdomen. Removal typically settles the abnormal bleeding, and the laboratory result guides whether anything further is needed.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no reliable prevention. Maintaining a healthy weight may modestly reduce risk. The key point is response rather than prevention: unexpected bleeding, and any bleeding after the menopause, should be checked promptly rather than watched at home.

## Heavy menstrual bleeding
**Summary:** Heavy menstrual bleeding means periods heavy enough to interfere with everyday life — flooding, large clots, or needing to change protection very often. It is a symptom pattern with many possible causes rather than a single disease, and it is always worth investigating rather than simply putting up with.
**Image:** images/heavy-menstrual-bleeding.jpg
**What it is:** In this entry, heavy bleeding is treated as the problem to be explained. Common causes within the womb include fibroids, endometrial polyps, and adenomyosis (all covered on this page). Other causes include hormone imbalance (cycles where no egg is released, thyroid problems), inherited or acquired clotting disorders, a copper intrauterine device, and — uncommonly — pre-cancerous change or cancer of the lining. Sometimes no structural cause is found.
**Why it happens:** Periods become heavy when the lining builds up too much, when the womb's muscle can't contract down effectively on the blood vessels — as happens when fibroids or adenomyosis distort it — or when the blood's clotting is impaired. Hormonal causes are common around puberty and in the years before the menopause, when cycles become irregular.
**Signs to watch for:** Needing to change a pad or tampon every hour or two, passing large clots, bleeding lasting more than about a week, flooding through clothes or bedding, and avoiding activities during periods. The consequence to watch for is iron-deficiency anaemia: tiredness, pallor, breathlessness, headaches, and reduced concentration. Any bleeding during pregnancy is not a period and needs urgent assessment. Bleeding that soaks through a pad or tampon every hour for 2 or more hours is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** The doctor will take a history of the bleeding pattern and may do a pelvic examination and blood tests (blood count and iron levels, sometimes thyroid and clotting tests). A pelvic or transvaginal ultrasound looks for fibroids, polyps, and adenomyosis. Depending on age and findings, hysteroscopy or an endometrial biopsy (a small sample of the lining) may be used to check the lining directly.
**How it's treated:** Treatment targets the cause, but bleeding itself can be reduced in the meantime: iron supplements for anaemia, medicines such as tranexamic acid or anti-inflammatory painkillers taken during periods, and hormonal options including the combined pill or a hormonal intrauterine system. If a polyp or fibroid is responsible, removing it usually helps. Surgery on the lining (endometrial ablation) or hysterectomy are options when other measures fail and pregnancy is no longer wanted.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely — it depends on the cause. Treating thyroid or clotting problems, and dealing with growths in the womb, addresses specific causes. The main message is that heavy periods are a legitimate medical problem with effective treatments, not something that has to be endured.

## Uterine prolapse
**Summary:** Uterine prolapse happens when the womb slips down from its normal position into, or even out of, the vagina because the supporting pelvic floor muscles and ligaments have weakened. It is common, not dangerous in itself, and there are effective non-surgical options as well as surgery.
**Image:** images/uterine-prolapse.svg
**What it is:** The womb is held in place by the pelvic floor and ligaments. When these supports stretch or weaken, the womb descends. In mild prolapse it drops slightly; in more advanced cases the cervix or the whole womb can protrude from the vaginal opening. Prolapse of the womb often occurs together with prolapse of the bladder or bowel into the vaginal walls — the broader picture of pelvic organ prolapse is covered in the vagina file.
**Why it happens:** Pregnancy and vaginal childbirth are the main causes, especially after large babies, difficult deliveries, or several children. The menopause weakens tissues further as oestrogen falls. Anything that repeatedly raises pressure in the abdomen adds strain: chronic cough, constipation and straining, heavy lifting, and excess weight.
**Signs to watch for:** A feeling of heaviness or dragging in the pelvis, a sensation of "something coming down," or a visible or palpable bulge at the vaginal opening. Symptoms are often worse after standing all day and better lying down. There may be difficulty emptying the bladder or bowel completely, needing to urinate often, leakage of urine, and discomfort during sex. Severe pelvic pain is not typical of prolapse and needs prompt assessment in its own right.
**How doctors confirm it:** Diagnosis is usually made on a pelvic examination, where the doctor assesses how far the womb has descended, sometimes asking the patient to bear down or cough. Scans are rarely needed. A specialist called a urogynaecologist often leads care when prolapse is more advanced.
**How it's treated:** Mild prolapse may need nothing beyond pelvic floor muscle training, which a physiotherapist can teach and supervise — it genuinely helps mild cases. A pessary, a removable device fitted in the vagina that supports the womb, is a good non-surgical option at any stage. Weight management and treating constipation and cough reduce the strain. If these aren't enough, surgery can repair the supports or, when appropriate, remove the womb and close the vaginal walls partially. Treatment is tailored — there is no obligation to have surgery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not completely, but risk can be lowered: doing pelvic floor exercises during and after pregnancy and keeping them up long-term, avoiding chronic straining by treating constipation, maintaining a healthy weight, and being cautious with repeated very heavy lifting.

## Endometrial (womb) cancer
**Summary:** Endometrial cancer is cancer of the lining of the womb. Its most important warning sign is bleeding after the menopause, which should always be checked promptly — when this cancer is found early, as it usually is because of that warning sign, treatment is often successful.
**Image:** images/endometrial-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** The cancer develops when cells of the endometrium (the womb lining) grow out of control. It is the commonest cancer of the female reproductive organs in many countries, and it typically occurs after the menopause. Most cases are of a type that grows relatively slowly and announces itself early through bleeding. This entry is an overview; see our cancer section for the broader picture of diagnosis and treatment.
**Why it happens:** The main driver is prolonged exposure of the lining to oestrogen without the balancing effect of progesterone. Risk is higher with excess weight (fat tissue produces oestrogen), never having been pregnant, diabetes, taking oestrogen-only hormone therapy, certain breast cancer medicines, and an inherited condition called Lynch syndrome that raises the risk of womb and bowel cancers. Age is a factor — most cases occur after the menopause.
**Signs to watch for:** Bleeding after the menopause is the red flag — even a single episode of spotting should be reported, and most such bleeding turns out not to be cancer, but it must always be checked. In younger women, persistent bleeding between periods or a clear change in bleeding pattern is worth assessing. Later signs can include pelvic pain or pressure, pain during sex, and unexplained weight loss. Severe pelvic pain needs urgent care: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** The first test is usually a transvaginal ultrasound to measure the thickness of the lining. Confirmation requires an endometrial biopsy — a small sample of the lining, taken in clinic or during a hysteroscopy (camera inspection of the womb) — examined under a microscope. If cancer is confirmed, MRI or CT scans show whether it has spread, which determines the treatment plan. Note that a Pap test checks the cervix, not the womb lining, so a normal Pap result does not rule this out.
**How it's treated:** Surgery is the main treatment — usually removal of the womb, fallopian tubes, and ovaries (hysterectomy), often done by keyhole surgery. Some patients also need radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or hormonal treatment depending on the type and stage of the cancer. Care is led by a gynaecologic oncologist (a cancer surgeon specialising in women's cancers) within a specialist team.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely, but risk can be reduced: maintaining a healthy weight is the single most useful step, and combined hormonal contraception and the hormonal coil appear to lower risk. There is no screening test for women at average risk — which makes responding to the warning sign, bleeding after the menopause, all the more important.
