Ailments of the throat — expanded from the throat card.

## Sore throat (pharyngitis)
**Summary:** A sore throat is pain, scratchiness, or irritation at the back of the throat, and most cases are caused by viruses — the same ones behind the common cold. Antibiotics don't help viral infections; most sore throats settle on their own within about a week with simple self-care.
**Image:** images/sore-throat.jpg
**What it is:** Pharyngitis means inflammation of the pharynx, the muscular tube at the back of the throat. It is one of the most common reasons people visit a doctor, and in the large majority of cases a virus is responsible — often alongside a runny nose, cough, and hoarseness. A smaller share are bacterial, most importantly strep throat, which has its own entry on this page.
**Why it happens:** Cold and flu viruses are the usual cause, spread through coughs, sneezes, and close contact. Other triggers include dry air, breathing through the mouth, air pollution or smoke, shouting, and acid reflux irritating the throat lining. Because viruses cause most cases, a sore throat often arrives with other cold symptoms.
**Signs to watch for:** A raw, scratchy feeling, pain when swallowing, redness at the back of the throat, mild fever, and swollen glands in the neck. Seek help sooner for a sore throat lasting more than a week, one that keeps coming back, or pain so severe you can't swallow saliva or open your mouth fully. Trouble breathing or swallowing is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually a brief history and a look at the throat with a light, plus feeling the neck for swollen glands. If a bacterial cause is suspected — for example high fever with pus on the tonsils but no cough — a rapid strep test or throat swab helps confirm it before any antibiotics are given.
**How it's treated:** For viral sore throats, what actually helps is rest, drinking plenty of fluids, warm drinks, gargling with salt water, and pain-relief medicines such as paracetamol or anti-inflammatory medicines used as directed. Lozenges and throat sprays can soothe. Honey can help adults and older children (never infants under one year). Antibiotics are reserved for confirmed bacterial infections.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Washing hands often, not sharing drinks or utensils, covering coughs and sneezes, and keeping distance from people with colds all reduce the chance of catching the viruses that cause most sore throats. Avoiding smoke and treating reflux also protect the throat lining.

## Tonsillitis
**Summary:** Tonsillitis is inflammation of the tonsils — the two pads of immune tissue at the back of the throat — making them swollen, red, and painful. It is especially common in children and teenagers, and can be caused by viruses or by bacteria, including the strep bacterium.
**Image:** images/tonsillitis.jpg
**What it is:** The tonsils are part of the immune system, trapping germs entering through the mouth and nose. When they become infected and inflamed themselves, that is tonsillitis. It differs from strep throat: tonsillitis describes *where* the infection is (the tonsils), and viruses cause many cases, whereas strep throat is one specific bacterial infection. Tonsillitis can occur once, or keep returning as recurring episodes.
**Why it happens:** The same viruses that cause colds and sore throats are frequent culprits, and bacteria — including group A streptococcus — cause others. Children and teens are most often affected because their tonsils are most active and they are exposed to many infections at school. Close contact spreads the germs involved.
**Signs to watch for:** A sore throat that is often severe, red and swollen tonsils sometimes with white patches or pus, fever, swollen tender glands in the neck, bad breath, and pain when swallowing. Young children may drool or refuse to eat. A rare complication is a peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) — a pocket of pus beside one tonsil causing severe one-sided pain and difficulty opening the mouth; that needs urgent care.
**How doctors confirm it:** A throat exam shows the swollen, inflamed tonsils, and the doctor feels the neck glands. Because appearance alone can't reliably separate viral from bacterial tonsillitis, a rapid strep test or throat swab is often used. Blood tests are sometimes done if glandular fever (mononucleosis) is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Viral tonsillitis is managed with rest, fluids, and pain relief while it settles. Antibiotics are used when a bacterial cause is confirmed or strongly suspected. An abscess usually needs drainage plus antibiotics. When episodes keep recurring and are severe, an ENT specialist may discuss tonsillectomy — surgical removal of the tonsils — weighing the frequency and severity of episodes against the risks of surgery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not completely, since the germs involved are widespread. Hand washing, not sharing drinks or toothbrushes, and staying home while unwell reduce spread. For people with frequent bacterial episodes, prompt treatment of each infection — and, in selected cases, tonsillectomy — can reduce how often it returns.

## Strep throat
**Summary:** Strep throat is a bacterial sore throat caused by group A streptococcus. Unlike most sore throats, it does benefit from antibiotics — not just to shorten the illness, but to prevent uncommon but serious complications such as rheumatic fever.
**Image:** images/strep-throat.jpg
**What it is:** Strep throat is an infection of the throat and often the tonsils by one specific bacterium, *Streptococcus pyogenes* (group A strep). It typically causes sudden, severe throat pain and fever, but characteristically *no* cough or runny nose — that pattern helps distinguish it from viral infections. It is most common in school-age children and spreads through close contact.
**Why it happens:** The bacterium passes between people in saliva and nasal secretions — through coughing, sneezing, shared drinks, or close contact in homes, schools, and crowded settings. Anyone can catch it, but it is most frequent in children and teenagers and in people in close contact with them.
**Signs to watch for:** Sudden severe sore throat, pain when swallowing, fever, red and swollen tonsils sometimes with white patches, tiny red spots on the roof of the mouth, and tender swollen glands at the front of the neck. Headache, stomach ache, and nausea are common in children. A sandpaper-like rash (scarlet fever) can accompany it.
**How doctors confirm it:** A rapid strep test on a throat swab gives an answer within minutes; sometimes a swab is sent for culture, which takes longer but catches cases the rapid test misses. Testing matters because doctors aim to give antibiotics for confirmed strep, not for viral sore throats.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics are the standard treatment, and completing the full course matters even once you feel better. Treatment shortens symptoms modestly, reduces spread to others, and — most importantly — greatly lowers the risk of complications such as rheumatic fever (which can damage the heart) and a type of kidney inflammation that can follow strep. Pain relief, fluids, and rest ease symptoms meanwhile.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no vaccine. Good hand hygiene, covering coughs, not sharing drinks or utensils, and keeping a person with strep home until they have been on antibiotics for at least a day or so — as local guidance advises — reduce spread. Treating confirmed cases fully also protects close contacts.

## Laryngitis
**Summary:** Laryngitis is inflammation of the larynx (voice box) and vocal cords, causing hoarseness, a weak or husky voice, or complete voice loss. It is usually short-lived — from a virus or from overusing the voice — and settles with rest and hydration.
**Image:** images/laryngitis.png
**What it is:** The vocal cords are two bands of tissue in the larynx that vibrate to produce sound. When they become swollen and inflamed, they can't vibrate cleanly, so the voice sounds rough, breathy, or disappears altogether. Acute laryngitis usually lasts a few days to about two weeks; hoarseness that drags on longer is classed as chronic and needs a proper look at the vocal cords.
**Why it happens:** Viral infections (often with a cold) are the commonest cause. Straining the voice — shouting, singing loudly, speaking for long periods, or talking over noise — also inflames the cords. Other irritants include smoke, dust, dry air, and acid reflux reaching the larynx. Chronic cases are often linked to smoking, ongoing voice misuse, or reflux.
**Signs to watch for:** Hoarseness or croakiness, a weak or fading voice, a tickly cough, a feeling of rawness in the throat, and needing to clear your throat often. Hoarseness lasting more than 2–3 weeks should always be checked by a doctor. Difficulty breathing or noisy breathing, especially in a child, is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often from the story and the sound of the voice alone, sometimes with a throat exam. If hoarseness persists beyond 2–3 weeks, or there is no obvious cause, an ENT specialist examines the vocal cords directly with laryngoscopy — a thin camera passed through the nose or mouth — to see the cords and rule out other problems.
**How it's treated:** The core treatment is voice rest: using your voice as little as possible, speaking softly when you must, and avoiding shouting — and also avoiding whispering, which strains the vocal cords more than gentle speech. Breathing humid air, drinking fluids, and avoiding smoke help. A speech therapist may advise on voice care. Antibiotics don't help viral laryngitis; when reflux or another cause is behind it, that cause is treated.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Rest your voice when you have a cold or hoarseness, avoid shouting or speaking over loud noise for long periods, stay hydrated, don't smoke, and manage reflux. People who use their voice professionally benefit from learning healthy voice technique with a speech therapist.

## Acid reflux irritating the throat (laryngopharyngeal reflux)
**Summary:** Stomach acid can travel up beyond the food pipe and splash onto the throat and voice box, causing chronic throat-clearing, hoarseness, and a "lump in the throat" feeling. This is called laryngopharyngeal reflux, and it often happens *without* heartburn — hence its nickname, "silent reflux."
**Image:** images/throat-reflux.jpg
**What it is:** In reflux, the valve at the top of the stomach doesn't fully stop acidic stomach contents from rising. When reflux reaches the larynx and pharynx — tissues far more delicate than the food pipe — even small amounts of acid and digestive enzymes can irritate them. Many people with this throat-focused pattern never feel classic heartburn, so the link to reflux is easily missed.
**Why it happens:** A weakened or more relaxed lower oesophageal valve, excess weight, large or late-evening meals, lying down soon after eating, alcohol, caffeine, spicy or fatty foods, smoking, and tight clothing around the waist can all contribute. Pregnancy and a hiatus hernia increase the likelihood too.
**Signs to watch for:** Persistent throat-clearing, hoarseness (often worse in the morning), a sensation of a lump in the throat (globus) that doesn't affect swallowing food, excess throat mucus, a chronic cough, and a sore or burning throat — frequently without heartburn or indigestion. Because these symptoms overlap with other conditions, persistent cases deserve proper assessment rather than assumptions.
**How doctors confirm it:** A doctor or ENT specialist takes a symptom history and may examine the voice box with laryngoscopy, looking for redness and swelling patterns typical of reflux irritation. Sometimes a trial of reflux treatment is used to see whether symptoms improve. In unclear or stubborn cases, tests measuring acid in the food pipe over 24 hours may be arranged, and other causes of hoarseness are excluded.
**How it's treated:** Lifestyle measures come first and are often the mainstay: eating smaller meals, not eating within a few hours of bedtime, losing excess weight, raising the head of the bed, and cutting back on alcohol, caffeine, and trigger foods. Doctors may also consider acid-reducing medicines, such as medicines that reduce acid production or that neutralise acid. Voice therapy from a speech therapist can help break throat-clearing habits that add strain.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, yes — the same lifestyle measures used in treatment (meal timing, weight, avoiding triggers, not smoking) prevent most irritation. Because the condition tends to recur, keeping those habits up matters more than a short course of treatment.

## Swallowing difficulty (dysphagia)
**Summary:** Dysphagia means difficulty swallowing — food sticking, coughing or choking when eating or drinking, or feeling that a swallow won't go down. It is a symptom rather than a disease in itself, it always deserves prompt assessment, and its causes range from reflux to stroke to, less commonly, cancer.
**Image:** images/dysphagia.png
**What it is:** A normal swallow is a fast, coordinated sequence involving the mouth, throat, and food pipe (oesophagus), with a flap protecting the airway. Dysphagia can arise at the throat stage (food sticks high up, or goes "down the wrong way") or lower down in the food pipe (food sticks behind the breastbone).
**Why it happens:** Causes in the throat include stroke and other neurological conditions that weaken the swallowing muscles, severe reflux, infections, and — less commonly — growths. Causes in the food pipe include narrowing from long-standing reflux, rings or webs of tissue, muscle disorders, and cancers. Some medicines, a very dry mouth, and poor teeth can add to the problem, especially in older adults.
**Signs to watch for:** Coughing or choking during meals, food sticking, needing several swallows for one mouthful, a wet or gurgly voice after swallowing, bringing food back up, avoiding certain foods, and losing weight. A useful clue: trouble mainly with *liquids* points more toward a coordination or nerve problem, while trouble mainly with *solids* suggests a narrowing. Choking that blocks the airway is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A doctor takes a careful history of what sticks, where, and how it started, then examines the mouth, throat, and neck. Tests may include laryngoscopy to view the throat, a formal swallowing assessment (sometimes with a speech therapist, watching you swallow foods of different textures on X-ray or camera), and imaging or a camera test of the food pipe when a lower cause is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Treatment targets the cause: treating reflux, dilating a narrowed food pipe, treating infections, or addressing a growth, for example. Alongside this, a speech therapist often helps with safer swallowing techniques, posture, and food textures that reduce the risk of food entering the airway (aspiration) and causing chest infections. Dietitians may help maintain nutrition while swallowing is impaired.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only partly — it depends on the cause. Treating reflux properly, not smoking, limiting heavy alcohol use, and good mouth and dental care all lower some risks. Because prompt assessment gives the best outcomes whatever the cause, the practical message is not to live with worsening swallowing difficulty — get it checked.

## Persistent hoarseness & neck lumps
**Summary:** Hoarseness lasting more than 2–3 weeks, or a new lump in the neck, is the throat's main warning sign — usually the cause turns out to be something benign, but these symptoms occasionally signal throat or voice box cancer, so they always need a proper check. This entry explains what doctors look for and why timely checking matters.
**Image:** images/hoarseness-neck-lump.svg
**What it is:** This is a warning-sign entry rather than a single disease. Hoarseness that doesn't settle within 2–3 weeks means something is affecting the vocal cords beyond a passing cold; a neck lump is a swelling — often a lymph gland — that may reflect infection, a thyroid problem, or occasionally a cancer of the throat, voice box, mouth, or thyroid. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Common benign causes include lingering laryngitis, voice overuse, benign vocal cord growths such as nodules or polyps, reflux irritation, and thyroid problems. Neck lumps are most often lymph glands swollen by infection. The less common but important cause is head and neck cancer — including laryngeal (voice box) cancer — whose main risk factors are smoking and heavy alcohol use; some throat cancers are linked to HPV infection.
**Signs to watch for:** Hoarseness beyond 2–3 weeks; a neck lump that persists beyond a few weeks, is growing, or feels hard and fixed; difficulty or pain on swallowing; an ongoing sore throat or ear pain on one side; unexplained weight loss. Any of these means booking a doctor's appointment promptly rather than waiting to see. In children, a different emergency is epiglottitis — drooling, fever, and difficulty swallowing: don't try to look down the throat; call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** The doctor examines the throat and feels the neck, then usually refers to an ENT specialist. The key test is laryngoscopy — viewing the vocal cords directly with a thin camera. If something suspicious is seen, or a lump needs explaining, scans (ultrasound, CT, or MRI) and sometimes a biopsy or needle sample from the lump give the answer.
**How it's treated:** Treatment depends entirely on what is found: voice therapy and reflux treatment for benign cord problems, surgery for some benign growths, treatment of infection or thyroid conditions for many neck lumps. If cancer is confirmed, a specialist team plans care — surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or combinations — with far better outcomes when it is caught early, which is exactly why the 2–3 week rule exists.
**Can it be prevented?:** The serious causes are partly preventable: not smoking and limiting alcohol substantially reduce the risk of throat and voice box cancers, and HPV vaccination protects against HPV-related throat cancers. Nothing prevents every cause, so the safety net is awareness — persistent hoarseness or a new neck lump should never just be waited out.
