## Cervicitis
**Summary:** Cervicitis is inflammation of the cervix — the short passage at the neck of the womb. It's commonly caused by infection, including sexually transmitted infections, but irritation can do it too. Many cases cause no symptoms at all and are only spotted during an examination or screening.
**Image:** images/cervicitis.jpg
**What it is:** The cervix becomes swollen, red, and tender, and may bleed easily when touched. Cervicitis is a description of inflammation rather than a single disease — finding and treating the underlying cause is what matters. Left untreated, some infections can spread upwards into the womb and tubes (pelvic inflammatory disease), which can affect fertility.
**Why it happens:** The commonest causes are sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea; other germs, including herpes and the normal vaginal bacteria getting out of balance, can be involved too. Not all cervicitis is infectious: irritation from products such as spermicides, latex, or douching, or a reaction after a procedure, can inflame the cervix as well.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none. When symptoms occur they can include unusual vaginal discharge (sometimes yellowish or pus-like), bleeding after sex or between periods, pain during sex, and a need to pass urine more often or discomfort doing so. Pelvic pain or fever suggests the infection may have spread higher — that needs prompt medical care, and heavy bleeding or severe pelvic pain with fever means you should call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic examination, where the cervix looks inflamed and may bleed when a swab touches it. Swabs from the cervix and vagina are tested for infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea, and urine tests are sometimes used. Screening tests (Pap test and HPV test) may also be done.
**How it's treated:** Treatment targets the cause. Infections are treated with antibiotics or antiviral medicines as appropriate, and if a sexually transmitted infection is found, sexual partners also need testing and treatment — otherwise the infection simply passes back and forth. Irritation-related cervicitis settles once the irritant is avoided. Follow-up checks confirm the inflammation has cleared.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Safer sex — using condoms and limiting the chance of sexually transmitted infections — lowers the risk, and regular sexual-health checks catch infections early. Avoiding douching and harsh or fragranced vaginal products helps prevent the irritation type.

## Cervical polyps
**Summary:** Cervical polyps are small, soft growths that develop on the cervix. They are almost always harmless (benign), very common, and often found by chance during a routine examination or screening appointment. Removal is quick and simple, and the tissue is sent to a laboratory just to be sure.
**Image:** images/cervical-polyps.svg
**What it is:** A polyp is a small finger-like or rounded growth of tissue, usually reddish and soft, growing either on the surface of the cervix or just inside its opening. Most are only a few millimetres across. Polyps are not cancer, and only very rarely does a polyp contain abnormal cells — which is exactly why removed polyps are always checked in a laboratory.
**Why it happens:** Nobody knows the full reason. Polyps seem to grow in response to the hormone oestrogen, local inflammation, or increased blood flow to the cervix. They are most common in women who have had children and in the years around the menopause, but they can occur at any adult age.
**Signs to watch for:** Most polyps cause no symptoms at all and are found incidentally. When they do cause problems, it's usually light bleeding — after sex, between periods, or after the menopause — or a heavier or unusual discharge. Any bleeding after the menopause should always be checked promptly by a doctor, whether or not a polyp is the cause.
**How doctors confirm it:** A polyp is usually seen directly during a pelvic examination, when a speculum holds the vagina open and the cervix comes into view — for example at a screening visit. Occasionally an ultrasound is used if the picture isn't clear.
**How it's treated:** Small polyps on a thin stalk can often be removed during the same examination, by gently twisting them off with a small instrument; this is quick and usually painless or only briefly uncomfortable. Larger polyps may need removal at a clinic or hospital appointment, sometimes with a camera check of the womb (hysteroscopy). The removed tissue goes to the laboratory for checking, and polyps occasionally grow back, in which case they can be removed again.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not really — there's no known way to stop polyps forming, and nothing about lifestyle is proven to cause them. Attending examinations and screening when invited is how they're found and dealt with early.

## HPV infection
**Summary:** HPV (human papillomavirus) is an extremely common virus passed on through intimate skin-to-skin contact, including sexual contact. Most sexually active people catch it at some point. In the great majority of cases the immune system clears it silently within a couple of years, with no symptoms and no lasting harm.
**Image:** images/hpv-infection.jpg
**What it is:** HPV is not one virus but a family of over a hundred related types. They fall into two broad groups: types that cause warts (including genital warts), which are troublesome but not dangerous, and "high-risk" types that can, in a small minority of infections that persist for many years, drive the cell changes that lead to cervical cancer. A wart-causing infection does not turn into a cancer-linked one — they are different types.
**Why it happens:** The virus spreads through close skin contact in the genital area; condoms reduce but don't completely remove the risk because the virus can live on skin a condom doesn't cover. Catching HPV is a normal consequence of having sexual partners at some point in life — it says nothing about anyone's behaviour or cleanliness, and it can be acquired years before it's detected.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none at all — that's the important point. Most infections are invisible and clear without the person ever knowing. Wart-causing types may produce small soft growths on the genital skin. High-risk types produce no warts and no symptoms; their only footprint is the cell changes picked up by cervical screening.
**How doctors confirm it:** The HPV test, done on a sample of cells brushed from the cervix in the same way as a Pap test, detects the high-risk types. Many screening programmes now test for HPV first (primary HPV screening) and look at the cells under the microscope only if the virus is found. A positive result means the virus is present — not that cancer or even cell changes are present.
**How it's treated:** There is no treatment that clears the virus itself, and none is needed in most cases — the immune system does the job. Genital warts can be treated with creams, freezing, or minor procedures if they're bothersome. For high-risk HPV, the "treatment" is monitoring: screening at the recommended intervals so that any cell changes are caught and dealt with early (see the next two entries).
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, largely. HPV vaccination — offered to young people in many countries, usually before sexual activity begins — protects against the wart-causing types and the high-risk types responsible for most cervical cancers. Vaccination does not remove the need for screening, though: it doesn't cover every high-risk type, and many adults were never vaccinated. Safer sex reduces risk further, and not smoking helps the body clear the virus.

## Precancerous cell changes (CIN)
**Summary:** Sometimes screening finds abnormal cells on the cervix. These changes — called CIN, short for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia — are *not* cancer. They are early warning changes that, if left alone, might turn into cancer over many years. Finding and treating them is screening working exactly as intended.
**Image:** images/cervical-precancer.jpg
**What it is:** "Intraepithelial" means the abnormal cells are still confined to the surface layer of the cervix — they have not invaded deeper tissue, which is what would make them cancer. Doctors grade the changes by how much of the surface layer is affected: CIN 1 is mild (only the lower part of the layer), CIN 2 is moderate, and CIN 3 is severe (most or all of the layer). The higher the grade, the more likely treatment is advised; low-grade changes often return to normal on their own.
**Why it happens:** Persistent infection with a high-risk type of HPV (see the entry above) is the cause. Most HPV infections clear, but when one lingers for years it can gradually alter the cells of the cervix. Smoking makes clearance less likely, which is one reason smoking raises cervical cancer risk.
**Signs to watch for:** None. CIN causes no symptoms whatsoever — no pain, no discharge, no bleeding. It can only be found by screening. This is why attending screening matters even when you feel completely well: the entire point is to find these changes during the long silent window before they could become dangerous.
**How doctors confirm it:** An abnormal screening result (a positive HPV test and/or abnormal cells on a Pap test) leads to colposcopy — an examination where a specialist looks at the cervix under magnification, usually with a weak vinegar-like solution that makes abnormal areas show up white. Small samples (biopsies) are taken from any suspicious area, and the laboratory confirms the grade of CIN.
**How it's treated:** CIN 1 is often simply watched, with repeat screening, because it frequently settles by itself. CIN 2 and CIN 3 are usually treated by removing or destroying the small area of abnormal cells. The commonest method is loop excision (known as LEEP or LLETZ), where a thin wire loop removes the area under local anaesthetic in a clinic visit; freezing (cryotherapy) or heat treatments are alternatives in some settings. Follow-up testing afterwards confirms the area has cleared.
**Can it be prevented?:** To a great extent, yes — this is the preventable step. HPV vaccination prevents the infections that cause most CIN, and regular screening finds and treats CIN before it can progress. Not smoking helps the immune system clear HPV.

## Cervical cancer
**Summary:** Cervical cancer develops when abnormal cells on the cervix break through the surface layer and invade deeper tissue. It is one of the most preventable of all cancers: vaccination prevents most of the infections behind it, screening finds the precancer stage, and treating that precancer stops cancer from starting. The World Health Organization has a global strategy aimed at eliminating it as a public health problem.
**Image:** images/cervical-cancer.svg
**What it is:** Almost all cervical cancers are caused by long-persistent high-risk HPV infection, progressing through the CIN stage described above — a journey that usually takes many years, which is why screening has so many chances to interrupt it. The commonest type starts in the flat surface cells of the outer cervix; a less common type starts in the glandular cells inside the cervical canal. For the broader picture of how cancer is diagnosed and treated, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** The necessary background cause is a high-risk HPV infection that the immune system fails to clear. Factors that raise the risk include never having screening, smoking, a weakened immune system (for example untreated HIV or medicines that suppress immunity after a transplant), and long-term use of contraceptive pills, which slightly increases risk while they're used.
**Signs to watch for:** Early cervical cancer often causes no symptoms — again, screening is the safety net. When symptoms do appear, they are usually: bleeding after sex, bleeding between periods or after the menopause, unusual or watery/bloody discharge, and pelvic pain or pain during sex. These symptoms are far more often caused by harmless conditions such as polyps, but they should always be checked promptly. Heavy bleeding, or severe pelvic pain with fever, is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Examination and colposcopy with a biopsy confirm the diagnosis. If cancer is found, scans (such as MRI and CT) and blood tests establish the stage — how large the cancer is and whether it has spread — and a specialist team (including a gynaecologic oncologist) plans treatment. Checking the kidneys and lymph nodes is part of staging.
**How it's treated:** Treatment depends on the stage. Small, early cancers may be treated with surgery alone — sometimes a cone-shaped removal of part of the cervix, sometimes removal of the womb (hysterectomy). Larger or more advanced cancers are commonly treated with a combination of radiotherapy and chemotherapy given together; chemotherapy and targeted or immunotherapy medicines may be used if the cancer has spread. Care involves a specialist team, and support for fertility questions, side effects, and recovery is part of modern treatment.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes — more than almost any other cancer. The three layers of protection are HPV vaccination, regular cervical screening, and prompt treatment of any precancerous changes found. Avoiding smoking adds further protection. Few cancers offer so many chances to stop them before they start.
