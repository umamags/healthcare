## Plantar fasciitis
**Summary:** Plantar fasciitis is irritation of the thick band of tissue (the plantar fascia) that runs under the foot from heel to toes. It causes sharp heel pain that is typically worst with the first steps in the morning, and in most people it settles over time with simple measures.
**Image:** images/plantar-fasciitis.jpg
**What it is:** The plantar fascia helps support the arch and absorbs shock with every step. When it is repeatedly strained, tiny areas of damage and irritation build up, usually where the band attaches to the heel bone. It is one of the most common causes of heel pain in adults.
**Why it happens:** Risk rises with long hours standing or walking on hard floors, sudden increases in running or walking distance, tight calf muscles, flat feet or very high arches, and worn-out or unsupportive shoes. Excess body weight adds load through the band with every step.
**Signs to watch for:** A sharp or stabbing pain under the heel, worst with the first steps after rest — getting out of bed, or standing after sitting — that eases as you warm up but may ache again after a long day. The point under the heel is usually tender to press. Pain that is severe after an injury, or comes with numbness, needs checking.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the story and a physical exam — pressing the heel and arch and checking your gait and flexibility. Ultrasound can show a thickened fascia. X-ray is sometimes done to rule out other causes; it often shows a small heel "spur," but spurs are common in people with no pain at all and are usually not the cause.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include rest from aggravating activity, calf and plantar fascia stretching exercises, supportive footwear or insoles, ice, and anti-inflammatory pain relief for short periods. Most cases improve over months with these measures. Persistent cases may be offered physiotherapy, night splints, or injections, and surgery is a last resort.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — building up activity gradually, keeping calf muscles flexible, replacing worn shoes, and wearing supportive footwear for long standing shifts all reduce the strain.

## Bunions
**Summary:** A bunion is a bony bump that forms at the base of the big toe as the toe joint gradually drifts out of line. It tends to develop slowly over years, and while shoes can aggravate it, inherited foot shape is usually the main driver.
**Image:** images/bunions.jpg
**What it is:** In a bunion (doctors call it hallux valgus), the big toe leans toward the smaller toes and the joint at its base sticks outward, forming the familiar bump. The bump can become red, swollen, and painful where it rubs against footwear, and a fluid-filled sac (bursa) over it may flare up.
**Why it happens:** Genes and foot shape are the biggest factors — bunions often run in families and are commoner in people with flat feet or loose joints. Narrow, pointed, or high-heeled shoes squeeze the toes and can make an existing bunion worse and more painful, but footwear alone rarely starts the drift in a foot that wasn't prone to it. Inflammatory joint conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis can also contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** A visible bump at the big-toe joint, soreness or redness over it, pain when walking or in certain shoes, and the big toe starting to overlap or crowd the second toe. Thickened skin under the ball of the foot and pain in the smaller toes can develop as weight shifts.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam is usually enough — the doctor checks the joint's position, movement, and your gait. An X-ray taken while standing shows the exact angle of the drift and whether arthritis is present, which helps if surgery is being considered.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider are wider, roomier shoes, bunion pads and toe spacers to reduce rubbing, ice and anti-inflammatory pain relief during flare-ups, and insoles if flat feet contribute. These measures manage symptoms but cannot straighten the joint — only surgery (an operation to realign the bones and joint, done by an orthopedic foot & ankle surgeon or podiatric surgeon) can do that, and it is generally offered for pain and walking difficulty, not for appearance.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully, because foot shape is inherited — but choosing shoes with a wide toe box and avoiding chronically squeezing the toes together may slow worsening.

## Athlete's foot
**Summary:** Athlete's foot is a common fungal infection of the skin of the feet, causing itching, scaling, and cracking — especially between the toes. It spreads easily in warm, damp places like changing rooms and pool decks. (It is the foot-specific side of the general fungal skin infections covered on our skin page.)
**Image:** images/athletes-foot.jpg
**What it is:** Fungi that normally cause no trouble flourish in the warm, moist environment inside shoes. On the feet they most often produce itchy, white, soggy skin between the toes, or dry, flaky scaling across the sole. The same fungi can also spread to the toenails or the groin.
**Why it happens:** Walking barefoot on communal wet floors, sharing towels, and wearing the same sweaty shoes day after day all raise the risk — hence the name, from athletes' changing rooms. Tight, non-breathable footwear and heavy foot sweating make the skin a better home for the fungi.
**Signs to watch for:** Itching, stinging, or burning between the toes or on the sole, peeling or cracked skin, and sometimes blisters. If the redness spreads up the foot, the foot swells, or it hurts to walk, bacteria may have joined in — that needs medical care. People with diabetes should get foot sores or infections checked promptly rather than waiting.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually on appearance alone during a physical exam. If the diagnosis is uncertain or treatment hasn't worked, the doctor may gently scrape a little skin for examination or culture to confirm a fungus.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider are antifungal creams, sprays, or powders applied for the full course — often one to four weeks, and continuing a little after the skin looks clear, because stopping early lets it return. Keeping feet dry, changing socks when damp, and treating or rotating shoes helps the medicine work. Widespread or stubborn infections, or those involving the nails, may need antifungal tablets.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes — dry carefully between the toes, wear sandals on communal wet floors, don't share towels, alternate shoes so each pair dries out, and change socks after sweating.

## Ingrown toenails
**Summary:** An ingrown toenail happens when the edge or corner of a nail — usually the big toenail — grows into the soft skin beside it, causing pain, redness, and sometimes infection. How you trim your nails is the most common cause, and the most fixable one.
**Image:** images/ingrown-toenails.png
**What it is:** When the nail edge presses or pierces the skin fold, the skin responds with soreness and swelling. Bacteria can enter through the broken skin, producing pus and increasing pain. Some people are prone because their nails are naturally curved or their nail folds are bulky.
**Why it happens:** Cutting nails in a curve, trimming them very short, or tearing them off leaves a sharp corner that digs in as the nail grows. Tight or pointed shoes pressing the toes together, stubbing the toe, and fungal nails that thicken also contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** Tenderness and redness along one side of the nail, pain when the toe is pressed or in shoes, and swelling. Yellow pus, spreading redness, or throbbing pain signal infection and should be seen. Anyone with diabetes or poor circulation should treat an ingrown nail as a professional job from the start.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam is enough — the doctor looks at the nail edge, checks for infection, and asks about diabetes and circulation. X-ray is rarely needed, only if a deep infection is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Early, mild cases may settle with warm-water soaks and anti-inflammatory pain relief while the nail grows past the skin. Doctors commonly consider antibiotics if there is infection. For repeated or severe cases, a podiatrist or doctor can remove the offending nail edge under local anaesthetic, sometimes treating the nail root so that edge doesn't regrow. People with diabetes or poor circulation should not attempt "bathroom surgery" at home — the risk of a poorly healing wound is too high; see a professional instead.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly — trim nails straight across (not curved), keep them at a moderate length, file sharp corners gently, and wear shoes that give the toes room.

## Ankle sprains
**Summary:** An ankle sprain is a stretch or tear of the ligaments that steady the ankle, usually from rolling or twisting it. Most sprains heal with first aid and graded movement, but repeated sprains can leave the ankle unstable if each one isn't rehabilitated.
**Image:** images/ankle-sprains.jpg
**What it is:** Ligaments are tough bands that hold the ankle bones in alignment while letting it move. When the foot rolls inward (the commonest pattern), the ligaments on the outer ankle are stretched beyond their range and can partly or completely tear. Severity ranges from a mild stretch to a full rupture with marked swelling.
**Why it happens:** A misstep on uneven ground, landing awkwardly from a jump, or a sudden change of direction in sport. A previous sprain that left the ankle's ligaments and balance reactions weakened is the strongest predictor of another one. Poorly fitting shoes and fatigue add to the risk.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain on the outside of the ankle, swelling that develops within hours, bruising, and difficulty putting weight on the foot. A "pop" at the moment of injury, inability to bear weight, or tenderness over the ankle bones raises the chance of a fracture — sudden inability to bear weight after a twist or fall is an emergency: call your local emergency number. A foot that turns pale, cold, or numb after injury also needs urgent care.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam and gait/weight-bearing check, pressing the ligaments and bones. An X-ray is commonly considered when the person can't bear weight or has bony tenderness (the features doctors use to decide), and ultrasound or MRI may be used if a complete tear or lingering problem is suspected.
**How it's treated:** First aid in the first day or two is commonly advised as rest from painful activity, ice, compression with a bandage, and elevation above heart level (often shortened to RICE). Anti-inflammatory pain relief helps early on. Protected movement and physiotherapy — especially balance and strengthening exercises — are then key to restoring stability; a brace or tape may be used for sport. Surgery is uncommon, reserved for complete ruptures or ankles that stay unstable.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — balance and ankle-strengthening exercises, taping or a brace for previously injured ankles, proper sports footwear, and care on uneven ground all lower the risk.

## Diabetes-related foot problems
**Summary:** In diabetes, years of high blood sugar can damage the nerves in the feet and narrow their blood vessels, so injuries may go unfelt and wounds heal slowly. Small problems — a blister, a cut — can then become ulcers and serious infections, which is why daily foot checks are a core part of diabetes care.
**Image:** images/diabetic-foot.jpg
**What it is:** Two changes combine. Nerve damage (neuropathy) dulls or removes protective feeling, so a stone in the shoe or a rubbing seam may go unnoticed; it can also cause tingling, burning, or numbness. Poor circulation reduces the blood supply needed to fight infection and heal. Together they allow minor skin breaks to deepen into ulcers, most often under the ball of the foot or on the toes.
**Why it happens:** Long-standing high blood sugar is the underlying cause, with risk rising the longer diabetes has been present and the less controlled it is. Smoking further narrows blood vessels. Foot deformities (such as bunions or claw toes), ill-fitting shoes, and walking barefoot create the pressure points and injuries that start ulcers.
**Signs to watch for:** Loss of feeling, tingling or burning pain, dry cracked skin, and any break in the skin that isn't healing. Redness that is spreading, swelling, warmth, a bad smell, or fever with a foot wound means infection may be advancing — in a person with diabetes, a foot injury with spreading redness, swelling, or warmth is an emergency: call your local emergency number. Black or discoloured tissue needs urgent care too.
**How doctors confirm it:** Regular screening exams: a monofilament sensation test (a thin fibre pressed on the sole to check feeling), pulse and circulation checks, and a look at skin, nails, and foot shape. Blood tests gauge diabetes control; X-ray or MRI is used if a deep or bone infection is suspected.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include careful wound care and dressings, offloading (keeping weight off the ulcer with special footwear, boots, or casts), antibiotics for infection, and procedures to improve circulation where needed. Blood-sugar, blood-pressure, and cholesterol control slow further nerve and vessel damage. Care is usually shared between your diabetes team, a podiatrist, and sometimes an orthopedic foot & ankle or vascular surgeon. Treated early, most ulcers heal and amputation can usually be avoided — early care is what makes the difference.
**Can it be prevented?:** To a great extent, yes — check both feet daily (use a mirror for the soles), never walk barefoot even indoors, wear well-fitting shoes and clean socks, moisturise dry skin but not between the toes, have nails trimmed professionally if feeling or sight is reduced, and keep blood sugar controlled. Any sore that isn't healing within a few days deserves prompt professional attention.
