## Testicular torsion
**Summary:** The spermatic cord — the cord the testicle hangs from, carrying its blood supply — twists and cuts off the blood flow. This is a true emergency: without surgery within hours, the testicle can be permanently damaged or lost.
**Image:** images/testicular-torsion.jpg
**What it is:** Inside the scrotum, each testicle is suspended by the spermatic cord, which contains its artery, veins, and the sperm duct. In torsion, the testicle rotates on this cord, twisting it like a wrung cloth. The veins are blocked first, then the artery, so the testicle swells and is starved of oxygen. It most often affects teenagers and young men, and it can happen during sport, after a minor knock, or for no obvious reason at all — many cases start during sleep, waking the person with pain.
**Why it happens:** Some boys and men are born with a testicle that isn't firmly anchored inside the scrotum, so it can swing and rotate freely (sometimes called a "bell-clapper" arrangement). That loose attachment is the underlying cause; the twist itself can be triggered by movement, cold, or nothing identifiable. Because the same loose arrangement is often present on both sides, the other testicle is usually fixed during the same operation to protect it.
**Signs to watch for:** Sudden, severe pain in one testicle is the hallmark — this is not a pain that builds gently over days. The scrotum on that side swells, the testicle may sit higher than usual or lie at an odd angle, and there is often nausea or vomiting and lower abdominal pain. Do not wait to see if it settles: sudden severe testicular pain is torsion until proven otherwise. Call your local emergency number or go to emergency care immediately — hours matter.
**How doctors confirm it:** A doctor examines the scrotum and, if torsion is suspected, the priority is getting to the operating theatre quickly rather than delaying for tests. A scrotal Doppler ultrasound, which shows blood flow to the testicle, is often used when the diagnosis is uncertain and it can be done immediately. Blood flow that is absent or reduced supports torsion.
**How it's treated:** Emergency surgery (usually under general anaesthetic) through a small cut in the scrotum: the surgeon untwists the cord, checks whether the testicle recovers, and stitches it in place so it cannot twist again. The other testicle is normally fixed at the same time. If surgery happens within the first few hours, the testicle is usually saved. If it has been starved of blood too long, it may have to be removed — which is why speed matters so much. A man with one healthy testicle usually keeps normal fertility and hormone levels.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not really — the loose attachment is present from birth and usually unknown until a twist happens. The real protection is speed: know that sudden severe testicular pain is always an emergency, never something to sleep on or feel embarrassed about.

## Epididymitis and orchitis
**Summary:** Inflammation of the epididymis (the coiled tube behind the testicle where sperm mature) or of the testicle itself, usually caused by infection. Pain typically builds over a few days — the key contrast with the sudden start of torsion.
**Image:** images/epididymitis-orchitis.jpg
**What it is:** Epididymitis is inflammation of the epididymis; orchitis is inflammation of the testicle itself. The two often occur together, as infection spreads from one to the other. The affected side becomes painful, swollen, and tender, sometimes with a fever. (The epididymitis entry in our sperm-ducts page covers the same condition from the duct's point of view; this entry focuses on the testicle.)
**Why it happens:** The usual cause depends on age. In younger, sexually active men, sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea are common causes. In older men, urinary tract infections and prostate enlargement are more typical, with bowel-type bacteria tracking back along the sperm duct. Less often, a virus is responsible — mumps is the classic cause of orchitis in boys and men who aren't immune.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain and swelling that come on gradually over one to several days, often with a heavy, aching feeling in the scrotum. There may be pain or burning when passing urine, an urgent need to go, discharge from the penis, or fever. Important caution: if pain starts suddenly and severely, treat it as torsion first (see above) and seek emergency care — the two conditions can be confused, and torsion cannot wait.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical examination, urine tests, and swabs or urine tests for sexually transmitted infections. A scrotal ultrasound is often used to check the blood flow and rule out torsion or an abscess. Sexual partners may need testing and treatment too, so the infection isn't passed back and forth.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics matched to the likely cause, usually a course of one to two weeks or longer, plus rest, supporting the scrotum (supportive underwear or a folded towel while lying down), ice packs, and anti-inflammatory painkillers. Symptoms typically ease within a few days of starting antibiotics, though the swelling can take weeks to disappear fully. Always finish the course, and avoid sex until cleared if an STI is involved.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Practising safer sex reduces the STI-related cases, and prompt treatment of urinary infections helps. Mumps vaccination prevents mumps orchitis. Seeking care early for scrotal pain also prevents complications such as abscesses or lasting damage.

## Varicocele
**Summary:** A cluster of enlarged veins in the scrotum, above the testicle — often described as feeling like a "bag of worms." It's usually on the left side, is often harmless, and is covered in more detail in our scrotum page.
**Image:** images/varicocele.png
**What it is:** The veins that drain blood away from the testicle (the pampiniform plexus) become widened and twisted, similar to varicose veins in the leg. Varicoceles are common, usually develop around puberty, and most cause no problems at all. This is a shorter version of the entry in our scrotum page, which covers varicocele in full.
**Why it happens:** The valves inside the veins fail to stop blood flowing backwards, so blood pools and the veins stretch. The left side is affected far more often because of how the left testicular vein drains into a larger vein at an angle that makes backflow easier.
**Signs to watch for:** Often nothing — many are found only on examination. When there are symptoms, expect a soft swelling above the testicle that feels like a bag of worms, more obvious when standing and often gone when lying down, sometimes with a dull ache or heaviness after standing for long periods. In teenagers, the testicle on that side may grow more slowly.
**How doctors confirm it:** Physical examination (standing, and while bearing down) is usually enough; a scrotal ultrasound confirms it and measures the testicles. Because varicoceles are linked to reduced sperm quality in some men, a semen analysis may be offered if fertility is a concern.
**How it's treated:** Many need no treatment at all. Treatment — surgery to tie off the affected veins, or a radiology procedure (embolisation) that blocks them from inside — is usually considered when there is ongoing pain, a noticeable difference in testicle size in a teenager, or fertility difficulties. Supportive underwear and simple painkillers often settle mild aching.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — it's a matter of vein anatomy, not anything the person has done. Regular awareness of what your scrotum normally feels like helps you notice any change.

## Hydrocele
**Summary:** A build-up of fluid in the sac around a testicle, causing painless swelling of the scrotum. Very common in baby boys and usually harmless; in adults it is checked to make sure the testicle underneath is healthy. See also the fuller entry in our scrotum page.
**Image:** images/hydrocele.jpg
**What it is:** Each testicle sits inside a thin sac that normally contains a small amount of lubricating fluid. A hydrocele is when extra fluid collects in that sac and the scrotum swells. In babies it appears when the channel the testicle descended through hasn't fully closed; in adults it can follow inflammation or a minor injury, or appear with no clear cause.
**Why it happens:** Either the sac makes more fluid than it absorbs, or — in infants — fluid from the abdomen drains down a still-open passage. In adults, infection or injury to the scrotum can trigger it, but often no cause is found.
**Signs to watch for:** A smooth, painless swelling on one side of the scrotum that may slowly grow; the scrotum can feel heavy if the hydrocele is large. By itself it shouldn't be painful — pain, rapid change, or a lump that feels part of the testicle itself should be checked promptly, since those suggest something else.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical examination is the first step, and shining a light through the swelling (which glows if it's clear fluid) can support the diagnosis. A scrotal ultrasound is commonly done in adults to view the testicle itself and confirm it is normal, since the fluid can hide it from the fingers.
**How it's treated:** In babies, hydroceles usually disappear on their own in the first year or two, so doctors simply watch. In adults, a small hydrocele that isn't troubling can also be left alone. If it is large or uncomfortable, options include surgery to remove or fold the sac, or draining the fluid with a needle (which tends to refill). Any underlying cause, such as infection, is treated too.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — it isn't caused by behaviour. What matters is having any new scrotal swelling examined once, so a doctor can confirm it's fluid and nothing more.

## Testicular cancer
**Summary:** Cancer that starts in a testicle. It's the most common cancer in younger men, usually appears as a painless lump, and — said plainly — it is one of the most curable cancers, especially when caught early.
**Image:** images/testicular-cancer.png
**What it is:** Testicular cancer usually begins in the germ cells, the cells that make sperm. There are two broad types (seminoma and non-seminoma) that behave slightly differently, but both are treated along similar lines. Unlike many cancers, it mainly affects younger men — teenagers through to men in their thirties and forties. Because the testicle can be felt through the scrotum, changes are often found early, by the man himself.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't known. Known risk factors include having had an undescended testicle in childhood (even if corrected), a brother or father who had testicular cancer, and having had it in the other testicle before. It is not caused by injury, sport, tight clothing, or anything the person did.
**Signs to watch for:** Most often a painless lump, a firm area, or one testicle becoming larger, heavier, or a different shape. Some men notice a dull ache, heaviness, or a dragging feeling in the scrotum or lower abdomen. Occasionally there is pain, and rarely breast tenderness or growth, caused by hormones some tumours produce. Any new lump or change should be seen by a doctor promptly — most lumps turn out not to be cancer, but only tests can tell.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical examination and a scrotal ultrasound are the key first steps. Blood tests measure tumour markers — substances such as AFP, hCG, and LDH that some testicular tumours release. If cancer is suspected, the testicle is removed through a small cut in the groin and examined in the laboratory; this confirms the type. CT scans may then check whether it has spread, most often to lymph nodes in the abdomen or the lungs.
**How it's treated:** Removal of the affected testicle (radical orchidectomy) is both the way the diagnosis is confirmed and the first treatment; for early cancer confined to the testicle, it may be the only treatment needed, with careful monitoring (surveillance) afterwards. If the cancer has spread, chemotherapy is highly effective, and some men also have radiotherapy or further surgery to remove lymph nodes. Even when testicular cancer has spread, cure rates are high. One testicle is enough for normal hormone levels and fertility in most cases, and sperm banking may be discussed before chemotherapy. For the wider picture of cancer care, see our cancer section.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no known way to prevent it, but there is a powerful alternative: early detection. Know what your testicles normally feel like, and have any lump, swelling, or change checked promptly rather than waiting for it to go away.

## Low testosterone
**Summary:** When the testes make less testosterone than the body needs, symptoms can include tiredness, low sex drive, and low mood — but these are vague and overlap with many other conditions, so blood tests, not symptoms alone, make the diagnosis.
**Image:** images/low-testosterone.png
**What it is:** Testosterone is the main male hormone, made in the testes under signals from the pituitary gland in the brain. Levels naturally vary through the day (highest in the morning) and drift down slowly with age. Low testosterone — sometimes called hypogonadism or testosterone deficiency — means a level low enough, confirmed on testing, to cause symptoms.
**Why it happens:** Causes fall into two groups. Problems in the testes themselves: injury, infection such as mumps orchitis, chemotherapy or radiotherapy, undescended testicle, or age-related decline. Problems with the signals from the brain: pituitary disorders, certain medicines (including opioids and steroids), and significant obesity or chronic illness can all lower the signal. Often doctors find a mix, especially in older men with other health problems.
**Signs to watch for:** Reduced sex drive, fewer or weaker erections, tiredness, low mood, reduced muscle and increased body fat, and sometimes hot flushes, poor concentration, or reduced body hair. The catch is that every one of these is common in other conditions too — stress, poor sleep, depression, diabetes, thyroid problems — so symptoms alone never prove low testosterone.
**How doctors confirm it:** A blood test for testosterone, taken in the morning when levels are highest — and because a single low result is common and often temporary, guidelines call for two separate morning tests before the diagnosis is made. Further blood tests (such as LH and prolactin) help find the cause, and a low result sometimes reverses when an underlying problem, like weight or a medicine, is addressed.
**How it's treated:** First, the cause: treating an underlying condition or adjusting medicines sometimes restores normal levels, and lifestyle measures (weight loss, sleep, exercise) genuinely help. If levels remain low and symptoms persist, testosterone replacement — gels, injections, or patches — is a prescription-only treatment decided with a specialist. Honest framing matters: it is a treatment for a confirmed deficiency, not an anti-ageing tonic. It needs ongoing monitoring, it suppresses sperm production so it is unsuitable for men trying to father a child, and it isn't started on symptoms alone.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Keeping a healthy weight, treating sleep and metabolic problems, and reviewing medicines with your doctor all support normal levels. Some causes, such as injury or pituitary disease, can't be prevented — but they can be found and treated.

## Undescended testicle
**Summary:** A testicle that hasn't moved down into the scrotum before birth. It's checked for at baby health checks, usually descends on its own in the first months, and is fixed with a small operation in infancy if it doesn't — because leaving it raises fertility and cancer risks later in life.
**Image:** images/undescended-testicle.jpg
**What it is:** During development, the testes form inside the abdomen and normally travel down into the scrotum before birth. An undescended testicle (the medical word is cryptorchidism) is one that stops somewhere along that path — in the groin or, less often, still in the abdomen. It's most common in babies born early or small.
**Why it happens:** The descent is driven by hormones and anatomy, and sometimes the process simply doesn't complete. It's nobody's fault and nothing the parents did or didn't do. In some boys a testicle that was down can later ride up again ("ascending" testicle), which is why position is checked at routine child health visits, not only at birth.
**Signs to watch for:** An empty-feeling scrotum on one side (rarely both), noticed at the newborn check or by parents at bath time. It's painless and the baby is otherwise well. A different, look-alike situation is a "retractile" testicle that can be gently guided down and stays — that one is normal and usually needs no operation, which is why a doctor's examination matters.
**How doctors confirm it:** Careful physical examination does most of the work; examination is repeated over the first months to see whether the testicle comes down by itself. If a testicle can't be felt at all, an ultrasound or a keyhole look inside the abdomen (laparoscopy) may be used to find it.
**How it's treated:** Many undescended testicles descend on their own in the first few months of life. If one hasn't descended by around six months, doctors usually recommend a small operation called orchidopexy — the surgeon frees the testicle and fixes it in the scrotum — generally done in infancy, before the second birthday. Placing it in the scrotum keeps it at the cooler temperature sperm production needs and puts it where it can be examined throughout life.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — but its long-term effects largely can be, by operating in infancy rather than waiting. An undescended testicle left in place has a higher risk of fertility problems and of testicular cancer later, even after surgery the risk stays somewhat higher than average, so men who had this condition should know what their testicles normally feel like and have any change checked promptly (see the testicular cancer entry above).
