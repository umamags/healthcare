## Allergic rhinitis (hay fever)
**Summary:** The immune system overreacts to harmless airborne substances such as pollen, dust mites, or animal dander, causing sneezing, a runny or blocked nose, and itchy eyes. It's not an infection and isn't contagious, but unmanaged it often drives sinus problems and poor sleep.
**Image:** images/allergic-rhinitis.png
**What it is:** The lining of the nose becomes inflamed when it meets a trigger the immune system mistakes for a threat. It may be seasonal (pollen in spring or summer — the classic "hay fever"), year-round (dust mites, mould, animal dander), or both. Allergic rhinitis is closely linked with asthma and eczema, and the three often run in the same families.
**Why it happens:** Inhaled allergens trigger the release of histamine and other chemicals in the nasal lining. Common triggers include tree, grass, and weed pollens; house dust mites in bedding and carpets; dander from cats and dogs; and mould spores. Having a parent or sibling with allergies, asthma, or eczema raises the risk.
**Signs to watch for:** Sneezing fits, a clear runny nose, itching of the nose, throat, and eyes, watery eyes, and congestion that follows a pattern — worse in a particular season, around animals, or after dust exposure. Unlike a cold, there's usually no fever, and symptoms last as long as exposure continues rather than clearing in a week.
**How doctors confirm it:** The pattern of symptoms and triggers is often enough. An allergist/immunologist can confirm it with allergy testing — skin-prick tests or a blood test for specific allergens. A nasal exam (sometimes with a thin endoscope) checks the lining and rules out polyps or a deviated septum adding to the blockage.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider start with avoiding known triggers (see below), then saline rinses, antihistamine tablets or sprays, and nasal steroid sprays — the mainstay for congestion — used regularly rather than only on bad days. For severe or poorly controlled cases, an allergist may offer immunotherapy ("allergy shots" or under-the-tongue tablets, also called desensitisation), which gradually retrains the immune response over a few years.
**Can it be prevented?:** The allergy itself can't usually be prevented, but attacks often can be reduced: keep windows closed and shower/change after being outdoors on high-pollen days, use dust-mite-proof bedding covers and wash bedding hot, keep pets out of the bedroom, fix damp and mould, and don't smoke or allow smoking indoors. Starting nasal sprays before your pollen season begins often works better than waiting for symptoms.

## Sinusitis
**Summary:** The sinuses — air-filled spaces in the skull that drain through the nose — become inflamed, causing facial pressure, congestion, and thick discharge. Most acute cases are viral and settle on their own; sinusitis lasting 12 weeks or more is called chronic and needs a different approach.
**Image:** images/sinusitis.png
**What it is:** The sinus linings swell and mucus can't drain, so pressure and congestion build up. *Acute* sinusitis usually follows a cold and lasts days to a few weeks. *Chronic* sinusitis is ongoing inflammation lasting 12 weeks or more, sometimes with nasal polyps, and tends to flare and settle rather than fully clear. This entry covers both.
**Why it happens:** Most acute sinusitis is viral — the same viruses that cause colds — and resolves without antibiotics. Bacterial infection is suspected when symptoms last beyond roughly 10 days without improving, are severe from the start (high fever, marked facial pain), or get better then suddenly worsen ("double worsening"). Chronic sinusitis is driven by persistent inflammation rather than a lingering infection alone; allergies, nasal polyps, a deviated septum, and smoking all contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** Blocked nose, thick yellow-green discharge, facial pressure or pain (cheeks, forehead, around the eyes), reduced sense of smell, headache, and sometimes toothache in the upper jaw or a cough that's worse at night. Rarely, infection spreads beyond the sinuses — swelling around the eye, vision changes, a swollen forehead, severe headache, or confusion are red flags: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the symptom pattern and a nasal exam, sometimes with a thin endoscope to see the sinus openings and discharge. A CT scan of the sinuses is used for chronic cases, complications, or surgical planning — not for routine colds. Allergy testing may be done when allergies seem to be driving recurrent episodes.
**How it's treated:** For viral and mild cases: rest, fluids, saline rinses or sprays, pain relief, and sometimes a short course of decongestant — antibiotics don't help viruses and are reserved for cases that look bacterial by the criteria above. Chronic sinusitis is usually managed longer-term with regular saline irrigation and nasal steroid sprays; antibiotics or a short course of steroid tablets are sometimes used for flares, and an ENT specialist may consider surgery to widen drainage pathways when medical treatment isn't enough.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treating allergic rhinitis properly, saline rinsing during colds, not smoking, and humidifying very dry indoor air all reduce episodes. Blowing your nose gently — one nostril at a time — avoids forcing mucus into the sinuses. Flu vaccination reduces one common trigger of winter episodes.

## Deviated septum
**Summary:** The septum — the wall of cartilage and bone between the two nostrils — sits off-centre instead of straight. Most people have some degree of deviation and never notice it; treatment is only needed when it blocks airflow or contributes to other problems.
**Image:** images/deviated-septum.jpg
**What it is:** A perfectly straight septum is actually uncommon. Deviation ranges from a slight bend with no effect to a marked shift that narrows one side of the nose, making that side feel permanently blocked. It is a structural feature, not an infection or growth, and it doesn't come and go like congestion from a cold.
**Why it happens:** Many deviations are present from birth or develop as the nose grows during childhood and adolescence. Others follow an injury — a sports knock, fall, or accident — sometimes one that seemed minor at the time. Ageing can also make an existing bend more noticeable as tissues change.
**Signs to watch for:** One-sided blockage that never fully clears, noisy breathing or mouth-breathing, snoring, a tendency to sleep on one side, and sometimes recurrent sinusitis or nosebleeds on the narrower, drier side. Many people have none of these and only learn of the deviation during an exam for something else.
**How doctors confirm it:** A nasal exam is usually enough; an ENT specialist may use a thin endoscope to see the full length of the septum and check for polyps or other causes of blockage. A CT scan of the sinuses is sometimes done if surgery is being planned or chronic sinusitis coexists.
**How it's treated:** A deviation causing no symptoms needs no treatment. When blockage is the problem, doctors may first try treating anything adding to it — nasal steroid sprays or allergy treatment for swollen lining — since the bend itself doesn't respond to medicines. When symptoms persist and matter, an ENT surgeon can straighten the septum in an operation called septoplasty; it's done for function (breathing, sinus drainage), and on its own it isn't a cosmetic nose-shape procedure.
**Can it be prevented?:** Deviation from growth can't be prevented. Injury-related cases can be reduced with seatbelts and appropriate protective gear or helmets in contact sports and cycling. If a nose injury leaves you with new one-sided blockage, getting it checked early is worthwhile.

## Nosebleeds
**Summary:** Bleeding from the nose is common and usually minor — most bleeds come from small vessels at the front of the nose and stop with simple first aid. Bleeds from deeper in the nose (posterior bleeds) are heavier, harder to stop, and more common in older adults.
**Image:** images/nosebleeds.jpg
**What it is:** The nasal lining is rich in tiny blood vessels close to the surface, especially at the front of the septum, so it doesn't take much to start bleeding. Most nosebleeds (anterior) come from this front area and drain out of one nostril. Posterior bleeds start deeper, tend to run down the throat as well, and usually need medical treatment to stop.
**Why it happens:** Dry air and heated indoor air, nose-picking or vigorous blowing, colds and allergic rhinitis that leave the lining inflamed, and minor knocks are the usual triggers. Blood-thinning medicines (anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs), frequent NSAID use, alcohol, and high blood pressure can make bleeds heavier or more frequent. Recurrent bleeds on one side only deserve checking for a local cause.
**Signs to watch for:** Blood from one or both nostrils, or a taste of blood from it running down the throat. First aid: sit up, lean slightly **forward** (not back), and pinch the soft part of the nose — below the bony bridge — firmly for 15–20 minutes without letting go to check, breathing through your mouth. Seek emergency care — call your local emergency number — if bleeding doesn't stop after about 20 minutes of proper pressure, follows a head injury, is heavy, makes you feel faint or breathless, or you're on blood thinners and it won't settle.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by examining the nose to find the bleeding point, sometimes with an endoscope for deeper bleeds. Doctors ask about medicines, blood pressure, and how often bleeds happen; blood pressure is checked and blood tests are sometimes done if bleeds are frequent, heavy, or you're on anticoagulants. Scans are rarely needed.
**How it's treated:** First aid (above) stops most bleeds. In a clinic or emergency department, doctors may apply a medicated spray or solution to constrict vessels, cauterise a visible bleeding point, or pack the nose to apply pressure from inside. Frequent bleeds are managed by treating the cause — nasal moisturisers or saline gel for dryness, reviewing blood-thinning medicines with the prescribing doctor, and controlling rhinitis and blood pressure.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, yes: keep the nasal lining moist with saline spray or a thin layer of petroleum jelly in dry conditions, humidify heated indoor air, blow gently, and avoid picking — keeping children's nails short helps. If you use a nasal steroid spray, aim it slightly outward rather than at the septum, as directed.

## Nasal polyps
**Summary:** Nasal polyps are soft, painless, grape-like swellings of the nasal and sinus lining. They aren't cancerous, but as they grow they block the nose, blunt the sense of smell, and are closely tied to chronic sinusitis and asthma.
**Image:** images/nasal-polyps.png
**What it is:** Polyps form when the lining of the nose and sinuses stays inflamed for a long time and swells into smooth, translucent, teardrop-shaped growths hanging into the airway. Small ones may cause nothing; larger or multiple polyps block airflow and sinus drainage. They most often grow on both sides — a one-sided growth is checked more carefully to rule out other causes.
**Why it happens:** Long-standing inflammation is the driver, so polyps are strongly linked with chronic sinusitis and allergic or non-allergic rhinitis. They're also more common in people with asthma, and in some people with sensitivity to aspirin and similar painkillers, where asthma, polyps, and that sensitivity occur together. They aren't caused by infection alone and aren't contagious.
**Signs to watch for:** A blocked, stuffy nose that doesn't clear, constant runny nose or mucus dripping down the throat, markedly reduced or lost sense of smell (and therefore taste), facial pressure, and snoring. Because they grow slowly and are painless, people often blame "permanent congestion" on allergies for years before polyps are found.
**How doctors confirm it:** A nasal exam with a thin endoscope usually shows the characteristic pale, smooth swellings directly. A CT scan of the sinuses maps their extent and the state of the sinuses, especially before surgery. Allergy testing may be added, since treating underlying rhinitis is part of long-term control.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider start with nasal steroid sprays or drops, sometimes with saline irrigation — steroids often shrink polyps and restore some smell. A short course of steroid tablets may be used for large polyps. In selected asthma-and-polyp cases, specialists may consider newer injectable biologic medicines. When medicines aren't enough, an ENT surgeon can remove polyps and open the sinuses (endoscopic sinus surgery) — but polyps can regrow over time, so follow-up steroid sprays and rhinitis control continue after surgery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely, but recurrence and growth can often be slowed by controlling the underlying inflammation: regular nasal steroid spray as directed, saline rinses, good management of rhinitis and asthma, and not smoking. Keeping follow-up appointments matters, since early regrowth is easier to manage with sprays than with repeat surgery.

## Loss of smell
**Summary:** Smell can fade (hyposmia) or disappear (anosmia), and because smell does much of the work of flavour, food tastes bland too. Causes range from a blocked nose to viral infections and ageing — and because persistent loss occasionally signals other conditions, it deserves checking if it doesn't return.
**Image:** images/loss-of-smell.svg
**What it is:** Smell works when odour molecules reach the smell receptors high inside the nose and the signal travels to the brain. Loss can be *conductive* — swollen lining or polyps physically block odours from reaching the receptors — or *sensorineural*, where the receptors or nerve pathways themselves are damaged. The distinction matters because conductive loss often improves when the blockage is treated.
**Why it happens:** The commonest causes are nasal congestion from colds, allergic rhinitis, chronic sinusitis, and nasal polyps. Viral infections — including COVID-19 — can damage smell receptors directly, sometimes suddenly and without much congestion. Other causes include head injury, ageing (smell gradually declines with age), long-term exposure to smoke or strong chemicals, and, less commonly, early neurological diseases such as Parkinson's or Alzheimer's, where smell loss can appear years before other symptoms.
**Signs to watch for:** Food tasting flat, needing more salt or spice, not noticing gas, smoke, or spoiled food, and loss of appetite. Treat it as a safety issue as well as a symptom: without smell you lose an early-warning system. Sudden loss after a head injury, loss on one side only, or loss accompanied by severe headache, vision changes, or weakness needs prompt medical assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** History and a nasal exam, often with an endoscope, to look for congestion, polyps, or chronic sinusitis. Formal smell testing (identifying standard odours) can measure the loss. Allergy testing may help if rhinitis is suspected; a CT scan of the sinuses or, occasionally, an MRI is used when the cause isn't clear or a neurological cause is possible. An ENT specialist usually leads the workup, sometimes with an allergist.
**How it's treated:** Treatment follows the cause: clearing congestion and inflammation with saline rinses and nasal steroid sprays, treating rhinitis or sinusitis, or removing polyps may restore conductive loss. For smell lost after a viral infection, doctors often suggest smell training — deliberately sniffing a set of strong scents twice daily for several months — which has evidence behind it and no real downside. Loss from ageing or nerve damage is harder to reverse, and doctors will be honest about that.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only partly: manage rhinitis and sinus disease rather than ignoring them, don't smoke, and protect against head injury with helmets and seatbelts. If smell does go, act on the safety side — make sure smoke and gas detectors work, be strict about food expiry dates, and label household chemicals — and see a doctor if it persists more than a few weeks after a cold has cleared.
