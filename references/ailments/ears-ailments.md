## Hearing loss (age-related and noise-induced)
**Summary:** Hearing loss means sounds — especially speech — become harder to hear or follow. The two commonest patterns are a gradual decline with age and permanent damage from loud noise. Gradual loss is managed, not an emergency, but sudden hearing loss is: it needs same-day care.
**Image:** images/hearing-loss.jpg
**What it is:** Hearing loss happens when something in the chain from outer ear to inner ear stops working well. In age-related loss, the cochlea's tiny hair cells wear down over time, usually starting with high-pitched sounds and making conversations in noisy places hard to follow. In noise-induced loss, loud sound damages those same hair cells. Hair cells don't regrow, so both types are permanent — but they affect life far less when treated.
**Why it happens:** Ageing is the main driver of gradual loss, and family history plays a part. Noise damage comes from loud workplaces, concerts, power tools, firearms, and personal audio played too loud for too long — the risk depends on both volume and duration. Other causes include impacted earwax, middle-ear problems, some viral infections, certain medicines, and Ménière's disease (see vertigo below).
**Signs to watch for:** Asking people to repeat themselves, turning the TV up, struggling on the phone or in background noise, and tinnitus often accompany gradual loss. **Sudden hearing loss in one or both ears — over hours or a few days — is an emergency: call your local emergency number or seek same-day care, because treatment works best when started within days.** Untreated loss is also linked to social isolation and faster cognitive decline, which is why acting on gradual loss matters too.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy first, to rule out wax or infection. An audiologist then does audiometry — you respond to tones at different pitches and volumes — which maps the pattern and severity of the loss. Tympanometry checks eardrum movement and middle-ear function, helping separate middle-ear (conductive) from inner-ear (sensorineural) loss. Sudden loss may prompt urgent ENT assessment and sometimes an MRI.
**How it's treated:** Hearing aids are the mainstay for permanent loss — an audiologist selects and fits them. Assistive devices such as amplified phones help too. Cochlear implants are an option for severe to profound loss when aids aren't enough. Conductive causes (wax, fluid, infection) are treated directly. Sudden sensorineural loss is usually treated urgently with corticosteroid medicines, which is why speed matters.
**Can it be prevented?:** Noise-induced loss, yes: wear earplugs or earmuffs at concerts, around machinery, and with firearms, and keep personal audio at a moderate volume. Age-related loss can't be fully prevented, but protecting your hearing when young, treating infections promptly, and having regular hearing checks limit its impact.

## Middle-ear infection (otitis media)
**Summary:** Otitis media is an infection of the space behind the eardrum, most often in young children. It typically causes ear pain and fever following a cold, and most episodes settle — but repeated or persistent cases can affect hearing and speech.
**Image:** images/otitis-media.jpg
**What it is:** The middle ear is a small air-filled space containing the three tiny hearing bones. In children, the tube connecting this space to the back of the nose (the Eustachian tube) is shorter and more horizontal, so viruses and bacteria from a cold reach the ear more easily. Infection causes fluid and sometimes pus to build up behind the eardrum, which bulges and hurts. Fluid can linger after the infection clears — sometimes called glue ear — and dull hearing for weeks.
**Why it happens:** Usually a cold or other upper-respiratory infection that spreads to the middle ear. It's commonest in babies and young children, and risk is higher with exposure to other children (nursery, siblings), bottle-feeding lying flat, dummy (pacifier) use, and household tobacco smoke. Children with allergies or enlarged adenoids are also more prone.
**Signs to watch for:** Ear pain (babies may tug at the ear, cry more, and sleep poorly), fever, irritability, reduced appetite, and temporarily muffled hearing. Occasionally the eardrum bursts, releasing fluid and briefly relieving pain — the ear then discharges. Repeated infections, persistent fluid with hearing difficulty, or delayed speech in a young child are reasons to see a doctor soon. Discharge with swelling or tenderness behind the ear needs prompt care.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy — looking at the eardrum with a lighted instrument — shows redness, bulging, or fluid behind it. Tympanometry measures how the eardrum moves and confirms fluid or pressure problems. Audiometry is used when hearing loss from lingering fluid is a concern, especially before decisions about surgery.
**How it's treated:** Many episodes are watched first, with pain relief (such as paracetamol or ibuprofen), because many settle on their own; oral antibiotic medicines are used when infection is severe, in very young children, or not improving. For children with repeated infections or persistent glue ear affecting hearing or speech, an ENT specialist may consider ear tubes (grommets) — tiny tubes placed in the eardrum that let fluid drain and air in, usually falling out on their own within months.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Keeping vaccinations up to date (including flu and pneumococcal vaccines) reduces risk, as do breastfeeding in infancy where possible, avoiding tobacco-smoke exposure, and treating colds supportively. Prevention isn't guaranteed — many children simply outgrow the tendency as their Eustachian tubes mature.

## Swimmer's ear (otitis externa)
**Summary:** Swimmer's ear is an infection or inflammation of the outer ear canal — the passage leading to the eardrum. It's a different problem from a middle-ear infection: it sits in a different part of the ear and is usually treated with drops rather than medicines by mouth.
**Image:** images/swimmers-ear.jpg
**What it is:** The ear canal is protected by a thin layer of skin and wax. When water stays trapped in the canal — after swimming, bathing, or in humid weather — the skin softens and bacteria (sometimes fungi) can infect it. The canal becomes red, swollen, and tender. Because the infection is in the outer canal, the eardrum and middle ear behind it are usually normal, which is exactly what sets it apart from otitis media.
**Why it happens:** Moisture is the classic trigger — hence the name — but anything that breaks the canal's skin barrier invites it: scratching with cotton buds or fingernails, earplugs or hearing aids that irritate the canal, and skin conditions such as eczema or psoriasis. People who swim regularly and people with narrow ear canals get it more often.
**Signs to watch for:** Itching in the canal at first, then ear pain that is often worse when the ear is pulled or when chewing. There may be redness, swelling, discharge, and muffled hearing if the canal swells shut. Severe pain, spreading redness, fever, or swimmer's ear in someone with diabetes or a weakened immune system needs prompt medical care, as the infection can occasionally spread.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy shows a red, swollen canal, often with debris or discharge; the eardrum, if visible, usually looks normal — confirming the problem is outer, not middle, ear. If infection keeps returning or doesn't respond, a swab of the discharge may be sent to identify the germ. Tympanometry and audiometry are rarely needed unless another problem is suspected.
**How it's treated:** The mainstay is ear drops placed directly into the canal — typically antibiotic drops, sometimes combined with a steroid to reduce swelling, or antifungal drops if a fungus is the cause. A doctor may first clean out debris so drops can reach the skin, and a small wick is sometimes placed in a swollen canal. Keeping the ear dry during treatment is essential. Oral antibiotics are reserved for infection that spreads beyond the canal or in people at higher risk.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Dry your ears gently after swimming or bathing (tip the head, towel the outer ear), avoid pushing cotton buds or objects into the canal, and consider earplugs or a swimming cap if you're prone to it. Don't try to "clean out" healthy wax — it protects the canal.

## Tinnitus
**Summary:** Tinnitus is hearing a sound — ringing, buzzing, or hissing — that isn't coming from outside. It is a symptom, not a disease, and it most often goes together with hearing loss. Usually harmless but annoying, it sometimes points to something that needs checking.
**Image:** images/tinnitus.svg
**What it is:** Tinnitus happens when the hearing system generates or misinterprets signals somewhere between the ear and the brain. For most people it accompanies damage to the cochlea's hair cells: the brain turns up its internal "gain" to compensate for lost input, and that extra activity is heard as sound. It may be constant or intermittent, in one or both ears, and is often most noticeable in quiet surroundings or at night.
**Why it happens:** The commonest association is hearing loss — age-related or noise-induced. Other contributors include earwax blockage, middle-ear problems, Ménière's disease, some medicines (including high-dose aspirin and certain antibiotics), head or neck injury, and jaw-joint (TMJ) problems. Often no single cause is found. Stress and tiredness don't cause tinnitus but commonly make it seem louder.
**Signs to watch for:** Tinnitus on its own, in both ears, with gradual hearing loss is usually managed routinely. **Tinnitus in only one ear, tinnitus that pulses in time with your heartbeat, or tinnitus with hearing loss on one side, vertigo, or facial weakness should be checked promptly** — these patterns occasionally signal a growth or blood-vessel problem. Tinnitus that badly affects sleep, concentration, or mood deserves help in its own right.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy to check for wax or middle-ear problems, then audiometry — because measuring the hearing loss usually explains the tinnitus and guides treatment. Tympanometry may check middle-ear function. If the tinnitus is one-sided or pulsing, doctors may arrange an MRI or other imaging to look for an underlying cause.
**How it's treated:** There's no cure in most cases, but management genuinely helps. Treating the underlying hearing loss — especially fitting hearing aids — is often the single most effective step. Sound therapy (background sound, fans, or sound generators, especially at night), counselling, and tinnitus-focused therapy approaches reduce how much it intrudes. Doctors also review medicines and treat any cause found, such as wax or infection.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — the same steps that protect hearing protect against tinnitus: ear protection around loud noise and moderate volume for personal audio. If you already have it, avoiding total silence, managing stress, and treating hearing loss keep it from dominating.

## Vertigo & Ménière's disease
**Summary:** Vertigo is the sensation that you or the room is spinning. It's a symptom with several causes — most brief and treatable — and Ménière's disease is one specific inner-ear condition that causes repeated vertigo attacks together with hearing symptoms.
**Image:** images/vertigo.jpg
**What it is:** Vertigo comes from a mismatch in the balance system, usually in the inner ear's vestibular organs, which sense head movement using fluid and tiny crystals. In the commonest cause, BPPV, loose crystals trigger brief spinning when you move your head in certain ways. Viral inflammation of the balance nerve or inner ear (vestibular neuritis, labyrinthitis) causes longer episodes. Ménière's disease is different: fluid pressure builds up in the inner ear, producing attacks that combine vertigo with hearing symptoms in one ear. It tends to be long-term, with symptoms fluctuating over time.
**Why it happens:** BPPV often follows a head knock or inner-ear infection, or simply ageing of the balance organs. Vestibular neuritis usually follows a viral illness. Ménière's disease has no single proven cause — fluid regulation in the inner ear goes wrong, and it may run in families; salt intake, stress, and tiredness are commonly reported attack triggers. Migraine can also cause vertigo (vestibular migraine).
**Signs to watch for:** Spinning lasting seconds with head movement suggests BPPV; hours-long attacks with nausea suggest neuritis or Ménière's. Ménière's attacks typically add a feeling of fullness in one ear, fluctuating hearing loss, and tinnitus in that ear. **Vertigo with weakness, speech difficulty, facial droop, double vision, or inability to walk is an emergency — think stroke and call your local emergency number.** Fainting or falls with attacks also need prompt care.
**How doctors confirm it:** A balance assessment — positional and movement tests — is central: for BPPV, a specific head-positioning test reproduces the spinning and eye movements a doctor can see. Otoscopy and audiometry check the ear and hearing (Ménière's typically shows a characteristic pattern of loss in one ear), and tympanometry may rule out middle-ear problems. Blood tests or MRI are used when a central (brain) cause or a growth is suspected.
**How it's treated:** BPPV is usually fixed with a repositioning manoeuvre — a guided series of head movements (such as the Epley manoeuvre) that settles the crystals back, often within one or two sessions. Vestibular rehabilitation exercises help longer-lasting imbalance. Medicines that ease vertigo and nausea are used short-term for attacks. For Ménière's, doctors commonly consider dietary measures (such as lower, evenly spread salt intake), medicines taken to reduce attack frequency, and — for persistent cases — procedures on the inner ear. An ENT specialist guides long-term care; an audiologist monitors hearing.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully. BPPV and Ménière's can't be reliably prevented, though managing reported triggers (regular sleep, stress, salt) may reduce Ménière's attacks for some people. What matters most is getting the cause identified — most vertigo is treatable once diagnosed — and treating any hearing loss that comes with it.

## Earwax blockage
**Summary:** Earwax is normal and protective; it only becomes a problem when it builds up and blocks the canal, dulling hearing. The safe fix is softening drops or professional removal — never probing with cotton buds or trying ear candling.
**Image:** images/earwax-blockage.jpg
**What it is:** Glands in the ear canal make wax (cerumen) to trap dust and protect the skin. The canal normally cleans itself: wax slowly migrates outwards as you chew and talk. A blockage (impaction) happens when wax accumulates faster than it clears, or is pushed deep and compacted. The packed wax acts like an earplug, causing conductive hearing loss on that side until it's removed.
**Why it happens:** Some people simply make more wax, or have narrow or curvy canals. Hearing aids, earplugs, and earbuds can block the natural exit. The commonest avoidable cause is cleaning with cotton buds, hairpins, or similar objects — these push wax deeper and pack it down. **Ear candling is not a safe alternative: it doesn't remove wax effectively and can burn the ear or drip wax into the canal.**
**Signs to watch for:** Gradual muffled hearing in one ear, a feeling of fullness or blockage, earache, itching, tinnitus, and sometimes dizziness or cough (the canal shares a nerve with the throat). Hearing that suddenly worsens after swimming or a shower often means water has swollen existing wax. Pain with discharge or fever suggests infection rather than simple wax — see a doctor.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy directly shows wax filling the canal and confirms nothing else is going on. If hearing doesn't recover after removal, audiometry checks for an underlying loss, and tympanometry can assess the eardrum and middle ear once the canal is clear.
**How it's treated:** Softening ear drops (such as olive oil or pharmacy wax-softening drops) used for several days often let the wax clear on its own. If not, a clinician removes it — by gentle irrigation with warm water, or by microsuction or fine instruments under direct vision, which is the approach ENT specialists commonly use. Removal is quick and hearing usually returns immediately.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly by leaving your ears alone: never push cotton buds or objects into the canal — clean only the outer ear with a cloth. If you're prone to build-up, regular softening drops may help. Hearing-aid and earplug users should have their ears checked periodically, since devices trap wax.

## Perforated eardrum
**Summary:** A perforated eardrum is a hole or tear in the thin membrane separating the ear canal from the middle ear. Most perforations heal on their own within weeks — the key while healing is keeping water out of the ear.
**Image:** images/perforated-eardrum.png
**What it is:** The eardrum vibrates with sound and also seals the middle ear against water and germs from outside. A tear lets air and fluid through, so hearing on that side is often temporarily reduced, and the middle ear is exposed to infection. Perforations vary from pinhead-sized to large; small ones typically close by themselves as the membrane regrows.
**Why it happens:** The commonest cause is a middle-ear infection — pressure from pus behind the eardrum builds until it bursts. Other causes include injury (a slap or blow to the ear, a cotton bud pushed too deep, a blast or explosion), sudden pressure changes during flying or scuba diving (barotrauma), and, rarely, a skull injury. Objects inserted into the ear are an avoidable cause worth stressing: the eardrum is only a few centimetres inside.
**Signs to watch for:** Sudden ear pain followed by discharge — and often relief of pain — when an infected eardrum bursts. There may be hearing loss on that side, tinnitus, or brief vertigo. Blood or clear fluid after a head injury, hearing loss with facial weakness, or severe dizziness after an ear injury needs emergency care: call your local emergency number. A perforation that keeps discharging should be reviewed by an ENT specialist.
**How doctors confirm it:** Otoscopy directly shows the hole or tear in the eardrum. Audiometry measures how much hearing is affected and whether the loss is conductive (from the perforation itself) or involves the inner ear. Tympanometry is usually avoided until the drum heals, since it uses pressure changes. Persistent cases may get imaging to check the middle-ear structures.
**How it's treated:** Most perforations heal by themselves in a few weeks with no specific treatment. The essential care is keeping the ear dry — no swimming, and care when showering or washing hair — so the middle ear isn't infected through the hole while it closes. Antibiotic drops or oral antibiotics are used if infection is present. A perforation that hasn't healed after a few months, or one causing repeated infections, may be repaired surgically by an ENT specialist (a patch or graft procedure, called myringoplasty or tympanoplasty).
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treat middle-ear infections promptly, never insert objects into the canal, protect your ears from blasts and blows, and manage pressure changes when flying or diving (swallowing, yawning, or equalising gently — and avoid diving with a cold). Healed eardrums are slightly more delicate, so ongoing care helps.
