## Seminal vesiculitis
**Summary:** Seminal vesiculitis is inflammation of one or both seminal vesicles, the glands that make most of the fluid in semen. It almost always occurs together with inflammation of the prostate, and its best-known sign is blood appearing in the semen.
**Image:** images/seminal-vesiculitis.jpg
**What it is:** Because the seminal vesicles sit directly behind the prostate and share ducts with it, infection and inflammation rarely affect one without the other — doctors often treat vesiculitis and prostatitis as a single overlapping problem (see the prostatitis entry in the prostate file). The inflammation may settle in weeks or linger and recur.
**Why it happens:** The most common cause is a bacterial infection reaching the glands from the urinary tract or prostate; sexually transmitted infections can be involved. Non-bacterial inflammation also occurs, sometimes linked to congestion or irritation, and diabetes raises the risk of infections in this area generally.
**Signs to watch for:** A deep ache in the pelvis, perineum (the area between the scrotum and anus), or lower belly; pain during or just after ejaculation; and blood in the semen. Blood in the semen (haematospermia) is the symptom that most often brings men to a doctor, and it is worth understanding on its own: in younger men it is usually harmless, has no serious cause, and clears by itself within weeks. It deserves proper checking when it keeps happening, doesn't settle, or occurs in men over 40. Fever, feeling generally unwell, or pain when passing urine may accompany an infection. High fever with pelvic pain and feeling very unwell needs emergency care — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A prostate exam, urine tests and cultures, and a semen culture to look for infection. Transrectal ultrasound (a small probe in the back passage) shows the glands directly and can reveal swelling or debris; pelvic MRI is used if the picture is unclear or symptoms persist.
**How it's treated:** When bacteria are found or strongly suspected, a course of antibiotics is the main treatment, sometimes continued for several weeks because these glands are slow to clear infection. Pain relievers, warm baths, and avoiding ejaculation-related strain while things settle can help symptoms. If no infection is found, treatment focuses on easing inflammation and pain while the condition is monitored.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — treating urinary and prostate infections promptly rather than ignoring them, practising safer sex to reduce infection risk, and managing diabetes all lower the chances.

## Seminal vesicle cysts
**Summary:** A cyst is a fluid-filled sac in or near a seminal vesicle. Most are small, completely silent, and never need any treatment at all.
**Image:** images/seminal-vesicle-cysts.jpg
**What it is:** Cysts may be present from birth or develop later in life, sometimes after inflammation or a partial blockage of the gland's duct. They are usually discovered entirely by accident, on an ultrasound or MRI done for another reason. Only when a cyst grows large does it tend to make itself felt.
**Why it happens:** Some cysts are developmental — a small outpouching that was always there. Acquired cysts tend to follow infection, inflammation, or scarring that traps fluid inside the gland. Cysts present from birth are sometimes found alongside other variations in how the urinary system developed on the same side.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none whatsoever. A large cyst can cause a deep pelvic ache, discomfort when sitting, a feeling of fullness in the perineum, painful ejaculation, or urinary symptoms such as a weaker stream — simply by taking up space in a crowded area.
**How doctors confirm it:** Transrectal ultrasound usually shows the cyst clearly; pelvic MRI gives the most detailed picture of its size and what it sits beside. If a cyst is found along with fertility problems, semen analysis may be done to check whether it is affecting semen volume or sperm.
**How it's treated:** A cyst that causes no symptoms is simply left alone — no treatment, and often no follow-up beyond what the specialist advises. If a cyst is large or clearly causing symptoms, options include draining it with a needle under ultrasound guidance or removing it with surgery, sometimes keyhole surgery (sometimes called minimally invasive or robotic-assisted surgery, depending on the centre).
**Can it be prevented?:** No — cysts present from birth simply occur, and acquired ones can't reliably be avoided beyond treating pelvic infections promptly.

## Seminal vesicle stones
**Summary:** Small stones (calculi) can occasionally form inside the seminal vesicles. They are rare, often found by chance on scans, and only need treatment if they are actually causing symptoms.
**Image:** images/seminal-vesicle-stones.jpg
**What it is:** These stones form when salts and debris build up in fluid that isn't draining well from the gland, gradually hardening into small, gritty deposits. They may sit quietly for years. When they do cause trouble, it's usually by irritating the gland or partly blocking its duct. Note that blood in the semen can also come from stones rubbing inside the gland — as covered in the vesiculitis entry above, it is usually harmless in younger men but persistent bleeding, or any bleeding after 40, should be checked.
**Why it happens:** Slow or incomplete drainage of the gland is the usual background — after inflammation, with a narrow or partly blocked duct, or alongside an enlarged prostate. The exact reason some men form stones and most don't isn't fully understood.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none — the stone is an incidental scan finding. When symptoms occur they are typically painful ejaculation, blood in the semen, or a recurring deep pelvic or perineal ache. Repeated infections in the gland can also be a clue.
**How doctors confirm it:** Transrectal ultrasound or pelvic CT/MRI shows stones as bright, dense spots inside the gland. Because the symptoms overlap with prostatitis and other causes of blood in the semen, doctors may also do urine and semen cultures and a semen analysis to build the full picture.
**How it's treated:** If the stone causes no symptoms, nothing needs to be done. If it is clearly the source of pain, bleeding, or repeated infection, a urologist can sometimes remove it through a fine telescope passed into the urinary tract, or drain and clear the gland; treating any accompanying infection comes first.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not specifically — there is no diet or habit known to prevent these stones. Treating gland infections promptly and keeping follow-up for recurring symptoms is the practical approach.

## Absent seminal vesicles (from birth)
**Summary:** Some men are born without one or both seminal vesicles. It causes no symptoms in daily life and is usually discovered only during fertility testing, because it produces very low-volume semen.
**Image:** images/absent-seminal-vesicles.png
**What it is:** The seminal vesicles and the sperm ducts (vas deferens) develop together before birth, so when the vesicles are absent, the sperm ducts are usually absent on the same side too. The combination is discussed further in the sperm-ducts file (see the entry on blocked or absent sperm ducts). The man feels entirely normal — sex drive, erections, and ejaculation all happen; there is just very little fluid, and it contains no sperm.
**Why it happens:** In many cases the cause is a change in a gene that is also linked to cystic fibrosis. The man himself is typically healthy — he carries one changed copy and usually has no lung or digestive symptoms — but the change can stop the sperm ducts and seminal vesicles from forming. This is why genetic testing matters: if his partner also carries a change in the same gene, a child could inherit cystic fibrosis itself, so carrier testing for the partner is an important part of planning.
**Signs to watch for:** There are none in everyday life. The finding appears during an infertility workup: semen volume is very low, the semen lacks fructose (the sugar the vesicles normally supply — a standard lab clue), and no sperm are present even though the testes make them normally.
**How doctors confirm it:** Semen analysis (volume, fructose, and sperm count) raises the suspicion; a physical examination may find that the sperm ducts can't be felt. Transrectal ultrasound or pelvic MRI confirms the vesicles are absent, and blood tests for the gene change are usually arranged for both partners as part of genetic counselling.
**How it's treated:** The glands themselves can't be created, but fatherhood is often still possible: sperm are being made normally in the testes and can be collected directly from the testicle or its attached duct with a minor procedure, then used with IVF (in which an egg is fertilised in the laboratory, commonly by injecting a single sperm, in generic terms). A fertility specialist guides the steps, including the genetic counselling described above.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — this is a developmental difference present from birth, not caused by anything the person or his parents did or didn't do.

## Seminal vesicle cancer
**Summary:** Cancer starting in the seminal vesicles is extremely rare. Far more often, a cancer from a neighbouring organ — prostate, bladder, or rectum — grows into them.
**Image:** images/seminal-vesicle-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** True primary cancer of the seminal vesicle is one of the rarest cancers urologists ever see. When cancer is found in these glands, it has usually spread from next door. For the broader picture of how cancers behave and are treated, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** There's no established cause for the rare primary form. Involvement by spread reflects the growth of an adjacent cancer rather than anything the vesicles themselves did wrong.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none of its own — it may cause pelvic pain, blood in the semen or urine, or urinary difficulty, and is frequently discovered during the workup of one of the neighbouring cancers.
**How doctors confirm it:** Pelvic MRI or CT points to it; a tissue sample (biopsy) confirms it and, crucially, establishes whether it's a primary tumour or spread from elsewhere, which changes the whole treatment plan.
**How it's treated:** Treatment is led by a cancer team and depends on the origin: spread from another organ is treated as part of that cancer, while the rare primary tumour may be removed surgically, sometimes with radiation or chemotherapy.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not specifically. The practical protection is prompt attention to persistent pelvic symptoms and blood in the semen, so anything present is found early.
