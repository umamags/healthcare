## Benign breast lumps (cysts and fibroadenomas)
**Summary:** Most breast lumps are NOT cancer. The two commonest harmless types are cysts — fluid-filled sacs that come and go with the menstrual cycle — and fibroadenomas, smooth solid lumps most often found in younger women. Any new lump still needs proper checking, because no one can tell a harmless lump from a serious one by feel alone.
**Image:** images/benign-breast-lumps.jpg
**What it is:** A *cyst* is a small sac filled with fluid, a bit like a tiny water balloon in the breast tissue. Cysts are very common in women in their 30s and 40s, often appear in the run-up to a period, and may vanish afterwards — a breast can hold several at once. A *fibroadenoma* is a solid lump made of gland and fibrous tissue. It typically feels smooth, firm, and rubbery, and slips around under the fingers when pressed, which is why it's sometimes nicknamed a "breast mouse." Neither type turns into cancer.
**Why it happens:** Both are driven by hormones and the way breast tissue responds to them. Cysts form when tiny milk glands fill with fluid instead of draining, which is why they fluctuate with the cycle and become less common after the menopause. Fibroadenomas are an overgrowth of a normal breast lobule; why one woman develops them and another doesn't isn't fully understood, but they are a normal variant rather than a disease.
**Signs to watch for:** A cyst often feels round and smooth and may be tender, especially before a period. A fibroadenoma is usually painless. What matters more than the feel is change: a lump that is new, growing, hard and fixed in place, or still present after a period should be checked promptly — along with any skin dimpling, a nipple newly turning inward, or a lump in the armpit.
**How doctors confirm it:** Through what's called triple assessment: a clinical examination, imaging (ultrasound is usually best in younger women; a mammogram is often added), and — if the lump is still uncertain — a needle sample. That combination is how any lump is properly checked; a cyst can also be confirmed when it drains completely with a fine needle. Never self-diagnose a lump as "just a cyst."
**How it's treated:** Many benign lumps need no treatment at all once properly identified, just awareness of your own normal. A painful cyst can be drained with a fine needle, which usually makes it collapse. A fibroadenoma may simply be monitored, or removed with a small operation if it is large, growing, or causing worry.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not really — cysts and fibroadenomas arise from normal hormone-driven changes, not from anything you did or didn't do. What protects you is knowing how your breasts normally look and feel, and getting any new or persistent lump checked rather than waiting to see.

## Breast pain (mastalgia)
**Summary:** Breast pain is extremely common and, on its own, is rarely a sign of cancer — most breast cancers are painless. Pain that follows the menstrual cycle is the usual pattern, but one-sided, persistent pain, or pain with a lump or skin change, should be checked.
**Image:** images/breast-pain.svg
**What it is:** Doctors divide breast pain into two patterns. *Cyclical* pain is linked to the menstrual cycle: both breasts feel heavy, tender, sore, or slightly lumpy in the days before a period, and ease once it starts. *Non-cyclical* pain doesn't follow the cycle — it may affect one breast or one spot, and feel sharp, burning, or aching. Pain can also come from outside the breast itself, such as a strained chest-wall muscle, and simply be felt in the breast area.
**Why it happens:** Cyclical pain is caused by normal hormonal changes acting on breast tissue across the cycle, and it tends to settle after the menopause unless hormone therapy is used. Non-cyclical pain may come from a cyst, an injury, inflammation, larger breasts pulling on the supporting ligaments, or chest-wall problems. Often no single cause is found, which is frustrating but common.
**Signs to watch for:** Ordinary cyclical tenderness in both breasts usually needs no more than reassurance. Get pain checked if it is in one breast only, stays in one spot, persists day after day without easing, began after the menopause, or comes with any of the card's warning signs — a new lump or thickening, skin dimpling or orange-peel texture, redness, or nipple discharge. To be honest and clear: pain alone, with nothing else found, is seldom a cancer symptom — but new, unexplained, persistent pain deserves one proper assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually with a history and a clinical breast exam; keeping a simple pain diary for a couple of cycles helps show whether the pain follows your period. If the exam finds a lump or the pain is focal and persistent, imaging (ultrasound or mammogram) is used to look at that area. If everything is normal, further tests are rarely needed.
**How it's treated:** For most women, understanding the cause and being reassured is the main treatment. A well-fitting, supportive bra — including a softer bra at night during bad spells — genuinely helps. Simple pain-relief medicines and anti-inflammatory gels are the usual first steps; warm or cold compresses suit some people. Persistent, severe pain is worth a follow-up visit, as a few prescription options exist.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely, since cyclical pain is part of normal hormone activity. Good breast support during exercise, a properly fitted bra, and keeping a healthy weight may reduce discomfort. If pain started after beginning a hormone-containing medicine, mention it to your doctor rather than stopping it on your own.

## Mastitis
**Summary:** Mastitis is a painful inflammation of breast tissue, usually with infection, that most often strikes while breastfeeding. A hot, red, painful area of one breast with fever or flu-like aching needs same-day care.
**Image:** images/mastitis.jpg
**What it is:** Mastitis means the breast tissue has become inflamed and, usually, infected with bacteria. It typically affects one breast: a wedge-shaped area becomes red, hot, swollen, and sore, and you may feel genuinely unwell with fever, chills, and aching, as if flu is coming on. It is most common in the first weeks of breastfeeding, when cracked nipples and milk not draining well let bacteria in and multiply.
**Why it happens:** Two things combine: milk sitting in part of the breast (a blocked duct, missed feeds, a baby not latching well, an overly tight bra) and bacteria entering through a cracked or sore nipple. The stagnant milk feeds the bacteria, and the tissue reacts with inflammation. Mastitis can also occur in women who are not breastfeeding — there it always needs checking, because the cause isn't obvious and other problems must be ruled out.
**Signs to watch for:** A red, hot, tender patch on one breast; pain when feeding; fever, shivering, and flu-like aching; and milk flow reducing on that side. Per the card: a hot, painful, red breast with fever or flu-like aching needs treatment today — get same-day care, and call your local emergency number if you can't be seen quickly or feel very unwell. A soft, squishy, increasingly painful spot may mean an abscess (a pocket of pus) is forming.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the examination and your story — the appearance is characteristic. Blood tests may be used if you are very unwell. If things don't improve within a couple of days on treatment, or an abscess is suspected, an ultrasound scan can look for a pocket of pus and guide drainage.
**How it's treated:** Keep the milk flowing — in most cases you should keep feeding or expressing from the affected side, because emptying the breast is part of the cure, and the milk remains safe for the baby. Rest, fluids, and pain relief help; warm compresses before feeds and cold ones after can ease discomfort. Doctors commonly prescribe antibiotics when infection is likely or symptoms don't settle quickly. An abscess needs the pus drained, usually with a needle guided by ultrasound, occasionally with a small operation.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Good latch and feeding on demand so breasts drain fully, not skipping feeds suddenly, varying feeding positions, treating cracked nipples and blocked ducts early, and avoiding tight bras all lower the risk. Getting help from a lactation consultant early — at the first sore, blocked patch — often stops mastitis before it starts.

## Nipple discharge
**Summary:** Fluid leaking from the nipple is usually harmless — especially milky discharge, discharge that only appears when the nipple is squeezed, or discharge from both breasts. The pattern that always needs checking is discharge that comes on its own, from one breast, and is clear or bloody.
**Image:** images/nipple-discharge.jpg
**What it is:** Nipple discharge means any fluid that comes from the nipple other than breast milk during breastfeeding. It ranges from a few drops noticed as a stain on a bra to fluid that flows by itself. Most discharge has a benign cause inside the ducts. Looking at *how* it behaves — one side or both, squeezed or spontaneous, milky, coloured, clear, or blood-stained — tells the doctor a great deal before any test is done.
**Why it happens:** Common benign causes include normal hormonal effects (milky discharge, sometimes months after breastfeeding ends), *duct ectasia* — ducts widening and thickening with age, which can produce sticky green, brown, or black discharge — and *intraductal papilloma*, a small wart-like growth inside a duct that often causes clear or bloody discharge from a single duct. Certain medicines and thyroid or hormone problems can cause milky discharge from both sides. Rarely, discharge — particularly spontaneous, one-sided, and bloody — is caused by cancer in a duct, which is why that pattern is always investigated.
**Signs to watch for:** Lower concern: milky or multi-coloured discharge, from both breasts, only when squeezed, with no lump. Always get checked: discharge that happens without squeezing, comes from one breast or one spot, is clear and watery or contains blood, keeps happening, or is accompanied by a lump, skin change, or a nipple newly turning inward. In a man, any nipple discharge should be checked.
**How doctors confirm it:** The doctor will examine the breasts and may test which duct the fluid comes from. Imaging — mammogram and ultrasound, sometimes breast MRI — looks for a cause inside the ducts. A sample of the fluid or a core-needle biopsy of anything found can confirm the diagnosis; sometimes removing the affected duct is both the test and the treatment.
**How it's treated:** Treatment depends on the cause, and often none is needed beyond reassurance. Milky discharge from a hormone cause is managed by addressing that cause. A papilloma or a persistently discharging duct may be removed with a small operation, which also provides tissue to confirm it is benign. If cancer is found, care moves to the breast cancer pathway described below.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not really — most causes are age- and hormone-related changes in the ducts. One practical tip: avoid repeatedly squeezing the nipple to check whether discharge is still there, because squeezing itself stimulates more fluid and muddies the picture. Simply notice the pattern and report it.

## Breast cancer
**Summary:** Breast cancer starts when cells in the breast grow out of control, usually in the ducts or lobules. Found early — often by screening mammograms before anything can be felt — it is highly treatable. A new lump, skin dimpling, or a nipple newly turning inward should always be checked promptly.
**Image:** images/breast-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Most breast cancers begin in the milk ducts or the lobules that make milk. Some stay where they started (in situ), while others invade surrounding tissue and can spread, most often first to the lymph nodes in the armpit. Breast cancer also occurs in men, though far less commonly. One distinct type, *inflammatory breast cancer*, doesn't form a lump — the breast quickly becomes red, warm, swollen, and heavy, which can look deceptively like mastitis.
**Why it happens:** No single cause. Risk rises with age, with certain inherited gene changes passed through families, and with lifetime hormone exposure — early periods, late menopause, and some hormone therapies. Lifestyle plays a part: keeping a healthy weight after menopause, staying active, and limiting alcohol all lower risk, per the card. Most women diagnosed, though, have no strong family history and no obvious cause — which is why screening matters for everyone.
**Signs to watch for:** The card's list: a new lump or thickening; skin dimpling or an orange-peel texture; a nipple newly turning inward; nipple discharge, especially if bloody; redness or swelling of one breast; a lump in the armpit. Breast cancer is often painless, so don't wait for pain. And if a red, swollen breast diagnosed as mastitis doesn't settle quickly on antibiotics, go back — inflammatory breast cancer mimics infection and must be excluded.
**How doctors confirm it:** Through triple assessment: clinical exam, imaging (mammogram and ultrasound; MRI in some cases), and a *core-needle biopsy* — a small sample of tissue taken with a needle, which is the test that actually confirms cancer and shows its type. If cancer is confirmed, further scans may check whether it has spread, and the tissue is tested for hormone receptors and other markers that guide treatment.
**How it's treated:** Usually a combination, planned by a team of breast surgeon, oncologist, and radiologist: surgery (removing the lump, or the whole breast when needed), radiation therapy, hormone therapy for cancers that respond to hormones, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies aimed at specific features of the cancer. The order and mix depend on the type, size, and stage. For the broader picture of cancer care, see our cancer section.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not completely, but risk can be reduced: keep a healthy weight, stay physically active, limit alcohol, and attend breast screening offered by your local programme — screening mammograms find cancers before they can be felt, when treatment works best. Knowing your own normal and reporting changes promptly is the other half of protection.
