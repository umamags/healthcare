## Carpal tunnel syndrome
**Summary:** The main nerve to the hand gets squeezed as it passes through a narrow tunnel at the wrist, causing numbness, tingling, and weakness — often worst at night. Splints and injections help many people, but long-standing pressure can permanently damage the nerve, so persistent symptoms shouldn't be ignored.
**Image:** images/carpal-tunnel.jpg
**What it is:** The carpal tunnel is a snug passage at the wrist, and the median nerve — which gives feeling to the thumb, index, middle, and half of the ring finger, and powers the thumb muscles — runs through it. When the tissues inside swell or thicken, the nerve is compressed and its signals falter.
**Why it happens:** Anything that narrows the tunnel or swells its contents: wrist anatomy (some people are born with a tighter tunnel), pregnancy (fluid retention — this form often settles after delivery), diabetes, an underactive thyroid, arthritis, wrist fractures, and sustained gripping or bent-wrist postures. Typing alone is rarely the whole story; many cases have several contributing factors.
**Signs to watch for:** Numbness, tingling, or "pins and needles" in the thumb, index, and middle fingers — classically waking you at night, often relieved by shaking the hand. Later, clumsiness and dropping objects as grip weakens. Warning signs of advancing damage: numbness that never goes away and visible wasting of the thumb mound.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam with provocative tests — tapping over the nerve or holding the wrist bent to reproduce the tingling. A nerve conduction study, which measures how well signals travel through the median nerve, is the key confirmatory test. Ultrasound or MRI sometimes help identify a cause.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include a night wrist splint holding the wrist straight, activity changes, and a corticosteroid injection into the tunnel for temporary relief. If numbness is constant or the thumb muscle is wasting, a hand surgeon may recommend carpal tunnel release — a small operation dividing the tunnel's roof to free the nerve. Hand therapy supports recovery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not always — anatomy and conditions like diabetes aren't changeable. But keeping wrists straight rather than bent during long tasks, taking breaks from repetitive gripping, and managing diabetes and thyroid problems can lower the risk or slow progression.

## Arthritis of the hand
**Summary:** Joint wear and inflammation make hand joints stiff, achy, and sometimes swollen — most typically the thumb base and the finger joints. It's very common with age; treatment eases pain and keeps the hand working, even though worn cartilage doesn't regrow.
**Image:** images/hand-arthritis.jpg
**What it is:** This entry focuses on osteoarthritis — gradual wear of the smooth cartilage that caps the bones — especially at the base of the thumb, where pinching and gripping load the joint heavily. Finger joints often develop bony enlargements (nodes) near the tips or middle joints. Inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid arthritis, attacks joints differently and is covered in the bone page's arthritis entries.
**Why it happens:** Age and cumulative use are the main drivers. Previous injury to a joint, family history, and jobs or hobbies with forceful, repetitive thumb and finger use raise the risk. Inflammatory patterns — several joints hot, swollen, and stiff for over an hour in the morning — suggest a different cause and a different pathway of care.
**Signs to watch for:** Aching at the thumb base when opening jars, turning keys, or pinching; stiffness after rest that eases with gentle movement; grating or crunching sensations; gradual changes in joint shape. Rapid swelling, redness, or many joints flaring together deserves prompt assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam of range of motion, grip, and pinch strength, and an X-ray showing narrowed joint space and bony spurs. Blood tests help when an inflammatory arthritis is suspected, and a rheumatologist may be involved in those cases.
**How it's treated:** Approaches commonly considered include joint-protection techniques and hand therapy, splints that support the thumb base during activity, heat or cold, and pain-relieving medicines — anti-inflammatory drugs and painkillers, sometimes as gels applied to the skin. A corticosteroid injection into a painful joint can settle a flare. For severe thumb-base arthritis, a hand surgeon may discuss joint reconstruction or fusion once simpler measures no longer suffice.
**Can it be prevented?:** The wear itself can't be fully prevented, but its impact can be reduced: avoid overloading painful joints, use larger-grip tools and jar openers, keep the hand muscles strong, and maintain a healthy weight, since metabolic health influences joint inflammation.

## Trigger finger
**Summary:** A finger catches, clicks, or locks in a bent position, then snaps straight — sometimes painfully. It happens when a finger tendon can't glide smoothly through its tunnel, and a steroid injection or a small release procedure usually fixes it.
**Image:** images/trigger-finger.jpg
**What it is:** Each finger tendon runs through a series of snug rings called pulleys that hold it against the bone. If the tendon develops a small thickening or the pulley narrows, the tendon catches as it tries to slide through — like a knot jamming in a ring. The finger may lock bent and need the other hand to straighten it.
**Why it happens:** Repetitive or forceful gripping can irritate the tendon and its sheath. It's considerably more common in people with diabetes and in rheumatoid arthritis, and it often appears alongside carpal tunnel syndrome. Many cases, though, arise with no clear single cause, most often in the ring and middle fingers and the thumb.
**Signs to watch for:** Stiffness and clicking first thing in the morning, a tender lump in the palm at the base of the finger, catching or locking when you bend the finger, and a snap or pop when it releases. A finger fixed in a bent position and unable to straighten needs prompt review.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by physical exam alone — feeling and hearing the tendon catch as you open and close your hand, and finding the tender spot over the pulley. Ultrasound can show the thickened tendon gliding (or failing to) in real time when the diagnosis is uncertain.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include rest from aggravating gripping, a splint that rests the finger straight, and anti-inflammatory medicines for discomfort. A corticosteroid injection into the tendon sheath settles most cases. If triggering persists or returns, a hand surgeon can perform a brief release procedure — dividing the tight first pulley so the tendon glides freely — usually under local anaesthetic, followed by early movement and sometimes hand therapy.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only partly. Varying repetitive gripping tasks, using padded gloves or larger handles, and good diabetes control may reduce irritation, but trigger finger often develops without any avoidable trigger of its own.

## Tendon injuries
**Summary:** The tendons that bend and straighten the fingers can be cut, frayed, or snapped by injury or sudden force. A finger that won't bend or straighten after a cut needs same-day assessment — repaired tendons heal best when treated promptly, and rehabilitation is half the cure.
**Image:** images/tendon-injuries.png
**What it is:** Flexor tendons on the palm side pull the fingers closed; extensor tendons on the back straighten them. A deep cut can partly or completely divide a tendon, and a sudden load can tear one without any cut — a "jersey finger" when a gripping finger is forced straight, or a "mallet finger" when the tendon pulling the fingertip straight gives way.
**Why it happens:** Knife and glass cuts, machinery and bite wounds, sports injuries, and crush injuries are common causes. Tendons already frayed by arthritis or previous injury tear more easily. Because tendons glide inside tight sheaths with a limited blood supply, even a clean repair needs protection to heal without sticking.
**Signs to watch for:** A finger that won't bend (flexor injury) or won't straighten (extensor injury), a fingertip that droops, weakness after a cut or jam, and numbness suggesting a nerve was injured too. Seek urgent care — call your local emergency number for deep cuts with bleeding that won't stop, and in all cases arrange same-day assessment if movement is lost.
**How doctors confirm it:** A careful physical exam testing each tendon's movement individually, plus checking sensation and blood flow. Ultrasound can show the tendon ends and whether the cut is partial or complete; an X-ray rules out an associated fracture. MRI is reserved for complex cases.
**How it's treated:** Partial cuts sometimes heal with splinting alone; complete flexor tendon cuts usually need a hand surgeon to stitch the tendon ends together, ideally within days. Afterwards, specialist hand therapy is essential — closely supervised early-movement splints and exercises protect the repair while preventing it from sticking to its sheath, and this rehabilitation largely determines the final result.
**Can it be prevented?:** Many injuries can't be predicted, but cut-resistant gloves for kitchen, garden, and workshop tasks, safe knife habits, and removing rings before sport or machinery work all reduce the risk.

## Fractures and sprains of the hand and fingers
**Summary:** Falls, jams, and blows break hand and finger bones or tear the ligaments that stabilise the joints. Many heal with simple splinting — but a twisted (rotated) finger or certain wrist fractures can be missed, so "it's just a sprain" is a trap worth avoiding.
**Image:** images/hand-fractures-sprains.jpg
**What it is:** The hand's 27 bones range from the fingertip bones to the long metacarpals — the "boxer's fracture" through the neck of the little-finger metacarpal is a classic. Sprains are ligament injuries around a joint, from a mildly stretched ligament to a full tear with an unstable joint. Dislocations, where a joint is forced out of position, sit between the two.
**Why it happens:** Falls onto an outstretched hand, ball sports jamming a finger end-on, punching or striking objects, and workplace or crush injuries. The scaphoid bone in the wrist is a notorious special case: its fracture can hide on first X-rays, and tenderness in the "anatomical snuffbox" (the hollow at the thumb-side of the wrist) after a fall should never be dismissed.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain, swelling, bruising, and reduced movement. Red flags: any visible deformity, a finger that crosses over its neighbour when you make a fist (rotational deformity — this does not correct itself), numbness, or a pale or blue fingertip. A deformed finger after injury needs urgent care: call your local emergency number or attend emergency care the same day.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam checking alignment, rotation, stability, and grip and strength, followed by an X-ray — the key test for fractures. Ultrasound or MRI helps for ligament injuries and hidden fractures such as the scaphoid, which may need repeat imaging or a precautionary splint even when the first X-ray looks normal.
**How it's treated:** Approaches commonly considered include buddy-strapping a stable finger injury to its neighbour, splints or casts, and "relative motion" splinting with early movement guided by a hand therapist. Displaced, rotated, or unstable fractures may need a hand surgeon to realign the bone (sometimes under local anaesthetic) and fix it with fine wires, pins, or small plates. Anti-inflammatory medicines help pain and swelling. Rehabilitation restores movement and grip once healing allows.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — wrist guards for skating and snowboarding, proper ball-handling technique, not punching hard objects, and protective gloves for manual work all lower the risk. After any injury, completing the recommended splinting and exercises prevents the stiffness that often outlasts the fracture itself.
