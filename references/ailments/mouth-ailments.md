## Mouth ulcers (canker sores)
**Summary:** Canker sores are small, painful sores that appear inside the mouth — on the cheeks, lips, tongue, or gums. They are very common, are not contagious, and usually heal on their own within one to two weeks.
**Image:** images/mouth-ulcers.jpg
**What it is:** A canker sore (also called an aphthous ulcer) is a shallow, round or oval sore with a white or yellowish centre and a red edge. It sits on the soft tissue inside the mouth, never on the outer lips. This is the key difference from a cold sore: cold sores are blisters on or around the lips caused by a virus, while canker sores are inside the mouth and cannot be passed to anyone else. Most are small and heal within about ten days; larger ones are less common and can take several weeks.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't fully understood. Minor injury is a common trigger — biting your cheek, aggressive brushing, or hard and crunchy foods. Other triggers include stress, lack of sleep, and hormonal changes. Recurring ulcers sometimes signal something more: low iron, folate, or vitamin B12, or occasionally an underlying condition such as coeliac disease or inflammatory bowel disease.
**Signs to watch for:** A painful sore inside the mouth that stings when you eat, drink, or brush — citrus, spicy, and acidic foods often hurt most. One important rule: an ulcer that has not healed after 2 weeks needs to be checked by a dentist or doctor, whatever it looks like. See a professional sooner if ulcers are very large, keep coming back, or come with fever, weight loss, or diarrhoea.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by a simple oral exam — canker sores have a typical appearance and history. If ulcers recur often, blood tests can check iron, folate, and B12 levels and look for wider causes. A sore that doesn't heal may need a biopsy to rule out other problems.
**How it's treated:** Most need no treatment beyond time. Approaches doctors commonly consider include protective gels or mouthwashes to ease pain while the sore heals, topical anti-inflammatory or anaesthetic preparations, and treating any underlying deficiency (such as iron or B12) if blood tests find one. Good, gentle oral hygiene helps the mouth stay comfortable.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not completely — some people are simply prone to them. You can lower the chances by avoiding foods that trigger your own sores, using a soft toothbrush, correcting any vitamin deficiency, and managing stress where you can.

## Cold sores
**Summary:** Cold sores are clusters of small, fluid-filled blisters that appear on or around the lips, caused by the herpes simplex virus. They are contagious, tend to come back in the same spot, and usually clear within one to two weeks.
**Image:** images/cold-sores.jpg
**What it is:** Cold sores are caused by herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) — the same virus family as genital herpes, though cold sores on the mouth most often involve type 1. After the first infection, the virus stays quietly in a nerve and can reactivate later, travelling back to the lip to cause another outbreak. Many people carry the virus without ever getting a visible sore. Unlike canker sores, which form inside the mouth, cold sores form on the outside — the lip edge and nearby skin.
**Why it happens:** The first infection usually happens in childhood, often through close contact such as a kiss from someone with a cold sore. Later outbreaks are triggered by things like sunlight, fever or illness, stress, tiredness, cold weather, or a knock to the lip. The virus spreads through direct contact with a sore, and can also spread through saliva and shared items like cups, cutlery, and lip balm — especially while a sore is present.
**Signs to watch for:** An outbreak usually starts with tingling, itching, or burning on the lip, then small blisters appear, break open to form a sore, and crust over before healing. Extra care is needed around newborns and people with weak immune systems: herpes can be serious for them, so avoid kissing babies or sharing items while you have a sore. See a doctor if sores are very frequent, spread to the eyes, or don't heal.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by the appearance and history alone — cold sores in their typical spot are recognisable. If there's doubt, a swab of the sore can be tested for the virus.
**How it's treated:** Many outbreaks settle on their own. Antiviral medicines — as creams or tablets — can shorten an outbreak, especially when started at the first tingle. Doctors may consider regular antiviral tablets for people with very frequent outbreaks. Keeping the sore clean, not picking at it, and easing pain with simple pain relief are the usual supportive steps.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely, since the virus stays in the body. You can reduce spread by not sharing cups, cutlery, towels, or lip products, avoiding kissing while a sore is present, and washing hands after touching the sore. Protecting your lips from sun with sunscreen lip balm helps if sunlight is one of your triggers.

## Oral thrush
**Summary:** Oral thrush is a yeast infection of the mouth that causes creamy white patches on the tongue and inner cheeks. It's common in babies, denture wearers, and people whose immune system or mouth bacteria have been disturbed.
**Image:** images/oral-thrush.jpg
**What it is:** Thrush is caused by a yeast called Candida, which normally lives in small amounts in the mouth without causing trouble. Problems start when conditions let it multiply: the balance of mouth bacteria changes, or the immune system is weakened. The result is white or cream-coloured patches that can be wiped off, leaving a red, sometimes sore area underneath. It is an infection of the mouth lining, not a sign of poor hygiene.
**Why it happens:** Common triggers include a course of antibiotics (which also kill helpful bacteria), steroid inhalers for asthma — rinsing your mouth with water after each use lowers the risk — and dentures that aren't cleaned or are worn overnight. Dry mouth, uncontrolled diabetes, smoking, and a weakened immune system (from illness or from medicines that suppress immunity) also raise the risk. Babies get it easily because their immune systems are still maturing.
**Signs to watch for:** White patches on the tongue, inner cheeks, roof of the mouth, or throat that don't brush away easily; redness and soreness underneath; a cottony feeling in the mouth; altered taste; and sometimes cracking at the corners of the mouth. Babies may feed poorly. If patches spread or swallowing becomes painful, see a doctor promptly.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by an oral exam — thrush has a characteristic look. If the diagnosis is uncertain or thrush keeps returning, a swab or scraping can be sent for culture to confirm the yeast, and blood tests may check for causes such as diabetes or immune problems.
**How it's treated:** Antifungal medicines are the main treatment — commonly as lozenges, gels, or liquids held in the mouth, or tablets if the infection is more widespread. Doctors also address the trigger: cleaning dentures properly and removing them at night, rinsing after steroid inhalers, and improving blood-sugar control in diabetes.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Rinse your mouth after using a steroid inhaler, clean dentures daily and take them out overnight, manage diabetes well, avoid smoking, and keep up good oral hygiene. If thrush keeps coming back, that's a reason to look for an underlying cause rather than just repeat treatment.

## Dry mouth
**Summary:** Dry mouth means the saliva glands aren't making enough saliva to keep the mouth comfortably moist. It's often a side effect of common medicines, and because saliva protects the teeth, a persistently dry mouth raises the risk of cavities and gum disease.
**Image:** images/dry-mouth.svg
**What it is:** Saliva does far more than keep the mouth wet: it washes away food debris, neutralises acids, fights germs, protects tooth enamel, and makes speaking, chewing, and swallowing comfortable. When saliva flow drops, the mouth feels dry and sticky, and that protection drops with it. Dry mouth (doctors call it xerostomia) is a symptom with many causes rather than a disease in itself, and it's especially common in older adults — largely because of the medicines many take.
**Why it happens:** Medicines are the commonest cause — many widely used ones list dry mouth as a side effect, including some blood-pressure medicines, antidepressants, antihistamines, and painkillers. If you suspect a medicine, don't stop it on your own; raise it with the prescriber, who may be able to adjust the dose or suggest an alternative. Other causes include dehydration, breathing through the mouth, smoking, radiotherapy to the head or neck, and gland conditions such as Sjögren's syndrome, where the immune system attacks the saliva glands.
**Signs to watch for:** A dry, sticky feeling; thick or stringy saliva; bad breath; difficulty chewing, swallowing, or speaking; a sore throat or hoarseness; and cracked lips. Because protection is reduced, watch for new cavities — especially along the gumline — and sore or bleeding gums. See a dentist or doctor if dryness is persistent or makes eating and speaking hard.
**How doctors confirm it:** A history and oral exam usually point to the cause — the mouth looks dry, and the tongue may stick to the examining spatula. Doctors review your medicines, and may order blood tests if an immune or gland condition is suspected. Dentists mainly watch the consequences: decay and gum health.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include treating or working around the cause, frequent sips of water, saliva substitutes, and sugar-free gum or lozenges to stimulate flow. Because decay risk is higher, extra dental protection matters — fluoride toothpaste or treatments, and regular dental check-ups. Alcohol-free mouthwashes are usually suggested, since alcohol dries the mouth further.
**Can it be prevented?:** Sometimes — drinking enough water, limiting alcohol and tobacco, and using a humidifier at night can help. Medicine-related dryness can't always be avoided, but it can almost always be managed; mention it to your doctor or pharmacist rather than putting up with it.

## Persistent bad breath (halitosis)
**Summary:** Persistent bad breath usually comes from the mouth itself — most often bacteria coating the tongue, gum disease, or a dry mouth — rather than from the stomach. It is common, understandable, and in most cases fixable once the cause is found.
**Image:** images/bad-breath.png
**What it is:** Bad breath (halitosis) is an unpleasant odour from the mouth that doesn't go away with a quick brush or mint. Almost everyone has it briefly — on waking, after strong foods like garlic and onion, or after coffee. Persistent halitosis is different: it lingers despite reasonable hygiene. It can be embarrassing to raise, but dentists and doctors treat it as an ordinary health question, because most causes are straightforward and treatable.
**Why it happens:** In most cases the source is in the mouth. Bacteria that gather on the back of the tongue break down food particles and shed cells, releasing smelly gases. Gum disease and tooth decay give those bacteria more places to thrive, and a dry mouth makes things worse because saliva normally washes bacteria away. Less common causes include throat and sinus problems, reflux, diabetes, and certain medicines. Despite the common belief, the stomach is rarely the source.
**Signs to watch for:** An odour that persists despite brushing and flossing; a coated tongue; bleeding or swollen gums (a sign of gum disease); or dry mouth. Because it's hard to judge your own breath, a trusted person's honest feedback — or simply asking your dentist — is the practical way to know. Bad breath that won't go away despite good hygiene is worth a dental visit.
**How doctors confirm it:** A dentist or doctor starts with an oral exam, checking the teeth, gums, and tongue, and asking about dryness, medicines, and habits. If the mouth looks healthy, they may check the throat and sinuses or look for wider causes with blood tests.
**How it's treated:** Treatment targets the cause. For most people that means professional dental cleaning, treatment of gum disease or decay, and daily cleaning of the tongue — a tongue scraper or gentle brushing reaches the bacteria at the back of the tongue. Managing dry mouth, drinking enough water, and avoiding tobacco also help. Mouthwashes can mask odour briefly but don't fix the underlying cause.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Brush twice a day, clean between your teeth, gently clean your tongue daily, keep up regular dental check-ups, drink enough water, and avoid tobacco. If bad breath persists despite all of that, get it checked rather than just covering it up.

## Oral cancer
**Summary:** Oral cancer is cancer that develops in the mouth — on the lips, tongue, cheeks, gums, or floor or roof of the mouth. Its earliest signs are often painless, so any sore or patch that doesn't heal within 2 weeks should be checked; finding it early greatly improves the chances of successful treatment.
**Image:** images/oral-cancer.png
**What it is:** Oral cancer usually begins in the flat cells lining the mouth. It may appear as an ulcer that won't heal, a lump, or a white patch (leukoplakia) or red patch (erythroplakia) on the mouth lining — red and mixed red-white patches are the most concerning. It is one of the head and neck cancers, and it is covered in more detail in our cancer section. Unlike most mouth problems on this page, it doesn't resolve on its own and needs specialist care.
**Why it happens:** The biggest risk factors are tobacco in all its forms — smoking and chewing tobacco — and heavy alcohol use; the two together raise risk much more than either alone. Chewing betel nut (areca nut), with or without tobacco, is an important cause in India and parts of Asia. Infection with certain types of HPV (human papillomavirus) is linked to cancers at the back of the mouth and throat, and long-term sun exposure raises the risk of lip cancer. Many cases, though, involve more than one factor building up over years.
**Signs to watch for:** A sore or ulcer not healed within 2 weeks; a white, red, or mixed patch; a lump or thickened area; persistent numbness; loose teeth or a denture that suddenly fits badly; and pain or difficulty when swallowing. Early oral cancer often causes no pain at all — the absence of pain is not reassurance. Any of these changes lasting more than 2 weeks should be checked promptly.
**How doctors confirm it:** An oral exam of the mouth and neck comes first. The key test is a biopsy — a small tissue sample from the suspicious area, examined under a microscope. If cancer is confirmed, imaging such as CT or MRI shows its extent, and blood tests assess general health before treatment.
**How it's treated:** Approaches specialists commonly consider include surgery to remove the cancer, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy or targeted medicines, alone or in combination depending on the size, site, and spread. Care is given by a team that may include an oral and maxillofacial surgeon, an ENT specialist, and cancer specialists. Rebuilding the mouth after surgery and support with speech and eating are part of modern care.
**Can it be prevented?:** A large share of the risk is avoidable: don't use tobacco, avoid betel nut, limit alcohol, use sun protection on the lips, and keep up regular dental check-ups — dentists look for early signs as part of a routine exam. HPV vaccination, offered in many countries, protects against the virus types linked to these cancers. Prevention plus early checking is the strongest combination.
