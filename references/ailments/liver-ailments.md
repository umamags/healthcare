## Fatty liver disease (MASLD)
**Summary:** Fat builds up inside liver cells, usually without any symptoms. It's closely linked to excess weight and metabolic health, and in many people it can be improved — but left unchecked, it can progress to inflammation and scarring.
**Image:** images/fatty-liver.jpg
**What it is:** In 2023 the medical community renamed this condition from NAFLD (non-alcoholic fatty liver disease) to MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease), so you'll see both names for a while. In its simplest form, extra fat is stored in liver cells but the liver still works normally. In some people it advances to an inflamed form (sometimes called MASH), which over years can cause scarring (fibrosis), cirrhosis, and occasionally liver cancer.
**Why it happens:** MASLD is driven by metabolic risk factors: excess body weight (especially around the waist), type 2 diabetes or pre-diabetes, high cholesterol and triglycerides, and high blood pressure. It's not caused by alcohol, although alcohol can add further injury on top. Genetics and age also play a part.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none — most people feel completely well and it's found on a routine blood test or an ultrasound done for another reason. Some people notice vague tiredness or a dull ache under the right ribs. Because it's silent, risk factors matter more than symptoms.
**How doctors confirm it:** Liver function blood tests, an abdominal ultrasound (which shows fat in the liver), and elastography/FibroScan to check for stiffness or scarring. Doctors also check blood sugar, cholesterol, and blood pressure, and rule out other causes such as hepatitis or alcohol. A biopsy is occasionally needed.
**How it's treated:** There's no single magic pill. The mainstay approaches doctors commonly consider are sustained weight loss (even a modest amount often helps), regular physical activity, and good control of diabetes, cholesterol, and blood pressure. Medicines are used for those associated conditions, and doctors are beginning to use specific medicines for the inflamed form in selected patients.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes — keeping a healthy weight, staying active, eating a balanced diet, limiting sugary drinks and alcohol, and managing diabetes and cholesterol all lower the risk.

## Hepatitis A, B, and C
**Summary:** Hepatitis means inflammation of the liver, and these three viruses are the most common causes worldwide. They differ in how they spread and how long they last: A is usually short-lived, while B and C can become long-term (chronic) and silently damage the liver over decades.
**Image:** images/hepatitis.jpg
**What it is:** *Hepatitis A* spreads through contaminated food or water (or close contact) and causes an acute illness that usually resolves on its own; it never becomes chronic. *Hepatitis B* spreads through blood and body fluids — including sexual contact and from mother to baby at birth — and while many adults clear it, some develop chronic infection. *Hepatitis C* spreads mainly through blood (shared needles, unscreened blood products, unsterile medical or tattoo equipment); most infections become chronic if untreated.
**Why it happens:** Infection with one of the three viruses. Risk is higher with travel to regions where hepatitis A is common and sanitation is poor (for A), unprotected sex or a partner with hepatitis B, sharing needles or razors, and medical or cosmetic procedures with unsterile equipment (for B and C).
**Signs to watch for:** Often none at all, especially in chronic B and C — many people only learn they have it from a blood test years later. When symptoms occur they can include tiredness, nausea, loss of appetite, abdominal discomfort, dark urine, pale stools, and jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes).
**How doctors confirm it:** Specific hepatitis blood tests identify which virus is present, whether the infection is new or longstanding, and (for B and C) how active it is. Liver function tests, ultrasound, and FibroScan show how much damage has been done.
**How it's treated:** Hepatitis A needs rest and supportive care while the body clears it. Chronic hepatitis B can usually be controlled — though not completely eliminated — with antiviral medicines taken long-term. Hepatitis C can now usually be cured with a course of antiviral tablets over a few months. Doctors also monitor the liver for scarring and cancer.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, to a large extent. Effective vaccines exist for hepatitis A and B (there is no vaccine for C). Safe food and water habits protect against A; not sharing needles, razors, or toothbrushes, safer sex, and properly screened blood protect against B and C.

## Alcohol-related liver disease
**Summary:** Drinking more alcohol than the liver can handle injures it over time, ranging from fatty liver to severe inflammation to permanent scarring. It's a medical condition, not a moral failing — and the earlier drinking stops, the more the liver can recover.
**Image:** images/alcohol-related-liver-disease.png
**What it is:** The disease develops in stages. Fatty liver (fat building up in liver cells) is the earliest and can reverse if drinking stops. Alcohol-related hepatitis is inflammation of the liver, which can occasionally become severe and life-threatening quite quickly. Continued heavy drinking leads to cirrhosis — permanent scarring.
**Why it happens:** Alcohol is broken down in the liver, and its breakdown products damage liver cells. Risk rises with the amount drunk and the number of years of heavy drinking. Women, people with obesity or MASLD, and people with hepatitis B or C tend to develop damage at lower levels of drinking. Nutrition is often poor alongside, which adds to the injury.
**Signs to watch for:** Early stages usually cause no symptoms at all. Later signs include tiredness, loss of appetite and weight, nausea, jaundice, swelling of the belly or ankles, and easy bruising. Severe alcohol-related hepatitis can cause fever, marked jaundice, and confusion — that needs emergency care: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A careful history of drinking, liver function blood tests, an abdominal ultrasound, and FibroScan to assess scarring. Doctors also check for other causes (such as hepatitis viruses or MASLD) because they often coexist. Sometimes a biopsy is used.
**How it's treated:** The single most important step is stopping alcohol completely. Because sudden withdrawal can be dangerous for heavy drinkers, doctors often recommend medical support or supervised detox rather than stopping alone. Treatment also includes nutritional support (including vitamin supplements), medicines for complications such as fluid build-up, treatment of any infection, and support services or counselling to help people stay alcohol-free. In liver failure, transplantation may be considered.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes — by keeping drinking within low-risk limits or not drinking at all, having alcohol-free days, and seeking help early if cutting down feels difficult. Support is widely available and asking for it is a health decision, not a weakness.

## Cirrhosis
**Summary:** Cirrhosis is permanent scarring of the liver after years of injury from any cause. It's the end stage that several other entries on this page — MASLD, hepatitis B and C, and alcohol-related disease — can lead to if the underlying injury continues.
**Image:** images/cirrhosis.png
**What it is:** Healthy liver tissue is gradually replaced by stiff scar tissue, so the liver shrinks, hardens, and struggles to do its jobs — filtering blood, making clotting proteins, and processing nutrients. Early cirrhosis ("compensated") may cause no problems at all. Later ("decompensated") cirrhosis brings complications: fluid in the belly (ascites), confusion from toxins reaching the brain (encephalopathy), bleeding from swollen veins in the gullet, and a higher risk of liver cancer.
**Why it happens:** Any long-term liver injury can end in cirrhosis: chronic hepatitis B or C, alcohol-related disease, advanced MASLD, and a few rarer causes such as autoimmune or bile-duct diseases. The scarring itself is the final common pathway — the cause still matters, because treating it can slow or partly stabilise the disease.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none early. Later: persistent fatigue, loss of appetite and muscle, jaundice, itchy skin, easy bruising, swelling of the belly or ankles, and confusion or sleep reversal. Vomiting blood, black tarry stools, or sudden confusion are emergencies — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Liver function blood tests, an ultrasound, and FibroScan (which measures stiffness) usually establish it; CT or MRI add detail. Doctors also screen for complications — for example, a camera test (endoscopy) to look for swollen gullet veins — and for liver cancer with regular ultrasound scans. A biopsy is sometimes needed.
**How it's treated:** The scar tissue can't be removed, but treating the cause (antivirals for hepatitis, stopping alcohol, weight loss for MASLD) can prevent further damage and sometimes improve function. Doctors manage complications with medicines that reduce fluid, medicines that lower toxin build-up and confusion, and procedures to prevent or stop bleeding from gullet veins. Vaccination, avoiding alcohol entirely, and careful medicine use protect the remaining liver. When cirrhosis advances to liver failure, transplantation is the option doctors consider.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes — cirrhosis is largely preventable by treating its causes early: hepatitis vaccination and treatment, keeping alcohol within safe limits, and managing weight and metabolic health. The key is acting before scarring becomes advanced.

## Liver cancer
**Summary:** Cancer that starts in the liver (most commonly hepatocellular carcinoma) usually develops in a liver already damaged by cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B. It often causes no symptoms early, which is why people with cirrhosis are offered regular surveillance scans.
**Image:** images/liver-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Liver cancer means abnormal liver cells growing out of control. This entry covers cancer that begins in the liver; cancer that spreads to the liver from elsewhere (such as the bowel) is more common still, but is managed differently and isn't covered here. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** The biggest risk factors are cirrhosis from any cause and longstanding hepatitis B infection (which can cause liver cancer even without cirrhosis). Hepatitis C, heavy alcohol use, and advanced MASLD raise the risk too, as does a toxin called aflatoxin that can contaminate poorly stored grains and nuts in some regions.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none in the early stages. Later signs can include weight loss, loss of appetite, upper abdominal pain or swelling, jaundice, and a worsening of existing cirrhosis symptoms. Because early signs are unreliable, surveillance matters more than symptom-watching for people at risk.
**How doctors confirm it:** Regular ultrasound scans for people with cirrhosis, a blood test for a marker called AFP, and CT or MRI scans — liver cancer often has a characteristic appearance on imaging, so a biopsy isn't always needed. Blood tests assess how well the remaining liver is working, which shapes treatment choices.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the tumour and the liver's remaining function: surgery to remove the tumour, procedures that destroy it with heat (ablation), treatments that block its blood supply (embolisation), targeted medicines and immunotherapy, and — for a small number of selected patients — liver transplantation. Care usually involves a team of liver, cancer, and imaging specialists.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Hepatitis B vaccination, effective treatment of hepatitis B and C, limiting alcohol, and managing weight and metabolic health all reduce the risk. For people who already have cirrhosis, keeping surveillance appointments gives the best chance of finding a tumour early, when it's most treatable.
