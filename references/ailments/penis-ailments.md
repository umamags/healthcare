## Erectile dysfunction
**Summary:** Trouble getting or keeping an erection firm enough for sex. It is very common, especially with age, and it is a health issue worth taking seriously in its own right: because erections depend on healthy blood vessels, erectile dysfunction is often an early warning sign of blood-vessel or heart problems, so getting it checked protects far more than your sex life.
**Image:** images/erectile-dysfunction.jpg
**What it is:** Occasional difficulty is normal and happens to most men at some point, particularly when tired, stressed, or after drinking. Erectile dysfunction is when the problem keeps happening over weeks or months. An erection needs good blood flow into the penis, healthy nerves, and the right hormone balance, so problems can arise from any of these — or from a mix of physical and psychological causes.
**Why it happens:** The commonest causes are conditions that narrow or damage blood vessels: diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, smoking, and excess weight. Low testosterone, some prescription medicines (including some blood pressure and antidepressant medicines), heavy alcohol use, and nerve damage from surgery or injury can also be responsible. Stress, anxiety, depression, and relationship difficulties are frequent contributors, either alone or on top of a physical cause.
**Signs to watch for:** Repeatedly being unable to get an erection, erections that are not firm enough, or erections that fade quickly. Warning signs that it is physical rather than purely stress-related include a gradual onset and loss of morning erections. One separate emergency: an erection that lasts more than about 4 hours and will not go down (priapism) is not a good sign — it can damage the penis and needs emergency care: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A conversation about your health, medicines, and the pattern of the problem, plus a physical exam. Blood tests check blood sugar, testosterone, and cholesterol, and blood pressure is measured — this is how an underlying heart or diabetes risk gets picked up early. A penile ultrasound is sometimes used to check blood flow.
**How it's treated:** Lifestyle comes first and often helps on its own: stopping smoking, losing weight, exercising, and cutting down alcohol. Treating diabetes, blood pressure, and cholesterol matters for both erections and the heart. The best-known medicines are a class called PDE5 inhibitors, taken as tablets before sex. Two firm safety rules: they must never be combined with nitrate medicines (used for chest pain), as the combination can drop blood pressure dangerously, and they should only ever be bought from legitimate pharmacies — counterfeit tablets sold online are common and can be harmful. Other options include vacuum devices, injections, and counselling where anxiety plays a part.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, partly — the same habits that protect the heart protect erections: not smoking, staying active, keeping weight, blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol under control, and drinking moderately or not at all.

## Peyronie's disease
**Summary:** Scar tissue inside the penis causes the erection to bend or curve, sometimes with pain. It develops gradually, often settles down on its own after a period of change, and many cases are mild enough to need no treatment at all.
**Image:** images/peyronies-disease.jpg
**What it is:** Inside the penis, a patch of inelastic scar tissue (called a plaque) forms in the wall of the erection chambers. Because scar tissue doesn't stretch, the erection bends towards the scar — upwards, sideways, or downwards. The penis may also look narrower or shorter. In the early, "active" phase the curve can change and erections may hurt; over some months it usually stabilises and the pain fades, leaving a fixed curve.
**Why it happens:** The likely trigger is injury to the penis during sex or other activity — often minor and unnoticed at the time — that heals with excess scar tissue in men who are prone to it. It is more common with age, in men with diabetes, and in men who also have a related scarring condition of the palm (Dupuytren's contracture). It is not an infection, it is not contagious, and it is not cancer.
**Signs to watch for:** A new bend or curve in the erection, a lump or hard area in the shaft, pain during erections (early on), or difficulty with intercourse because of the angle. Any new curve, bend, or lump is on the card's "see a doctor soon" list.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by examination — a urologist can feel the plaque — and sometimes by examining the penis while erect, which may involve a medicine injection in the clinic to produce an erection, or a penile ultrasound to map the plaque. Photos of the erection from different angles can help the doctor judge the curve.
**How it's treated:** If the curve is mild and sex is comfortable, no treatment is needed. Treatment is only considered once the disease has stabilised. Options a urologist may discuss include injections into the plaque to soften it, traction devices, and surgery for severe curves that prevent intercourse — surgery works well but can shorten the penis slightly and carries the usual surgical risks.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no proven way to prevent it. Being aware that new curvature is worth checking means it can be assessed early, while the range of options is widest.

## Balanitis
**Summary:** Inflammation of the glans (the head of the penis), often involving the foreskin too. It causes redness, soreness, and itching, is usually minor and easily treated, and only rarely signals anything serious.
**Image:** images/balanitis.jpg
**What it is:** Balanitis is a description, not a single disease — several different things can inflame the glans. It is commoner in uncircumcised men, because the warm, moist space under the foreskin suits both irritation and germs. Most cases clear quickly once the cause is identified and treated.
**Why it happens:** The commonest causes are irritation from soaps, shower gels, detergents, or a build-up of smegma from infrequent washing; thrush (a candida yeast infection, the same fungus behind athlete's foot and vaginal thrush); bacterial infection; and skin conditions such as eczema, psoriasis, or lichen sclerosus affecting the area. Men with diabetes are more prone to it, because sugar in the urine encourages yeast.
**Signs to watch for:** Redness, swelling, soreness or itching of the glans, sometimes with a rash, small sores, a white coating, or discharge under the foreskin, and discomfort when passing urine or during sex. If it keeps coming back, that pattern itself is a sign worth investigating.
**How doctors confirm it:** By examining the area. If the cause isn't obvious, the doctor may take a gentle swab to check for yeast or bacteria, and if balanitis recurs, a blood test for blood sugar to check for diabetes.
**How it's treated:** Gentle daily washing with water (or a soap substitute), drying carefully, and pulling the foreskin back into place afterwards. Creams are matched to the cause: antifungal cream for thrush, antibacterial treatment for bacterial infection, or a mild steroid cream for inflammation and skin conditions. Treating the underlying cause — such as diabetes — stops it recurring.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes: wash gently every day including under the foreskin, avoid perfumed products on the area, dry thoroughly, and keep blood sugar controlled if you have diabetes. Recurrent episodes deserve a diabetes check even if you feel well.

## Phimosis
**Summary:** A foreskin that is too tight to pull back over the glans. In young boys this is normal and usually resolves on its own; in adults it is usually due to scarring and is straightforwardly treatable.
**Image:** images/phimosis.jpg
**What it is:** In babies and young boys, the foreskin is naturally attached and tight, and it loosens gradually through childhood. This is normal development, not a problem, and it should never be forced back — forcing it can tear the skin and cause the very scarring that creates a problem later. True phimosis in a teenager or adult means the foreskin cannot retract, or retracts only with pain and tightness.
**Why it happens:** In adults the usual cause is scarring of the foreskin, often from a skin condition called lichen sclerosus, which produces pale, thickened, itchy patches — the same condition affects women, and it's covered on the vulva page. Repeated balanitis, repeated forced retraction in childhood, or infections can also scar the foreskin into a tight ring.
**Signs to watch for:** A foreskin that won't pull back, ballooning of the foreskin when passing urine, pain during erections or sex, repeated infections under the foreskin (balanitis), and pale, shiny, scarred-looking skin at the foreskin tip.
**How doctors confirm it:** By examination — the doctor checks how tight the foreskin is, whether scarring is present, and whether the appearance suggests lichen sclerosus. No special tests are usually needed.
**How it's treated:** First line is a steroid cream applied to the tight ring, combined with gentle stretching over several weeks — this works for many men and boys. If the foreskin remains too tight, or lichen sclerosus is causing ongoing scarring, circumcision (surgical removal of the foreskin) is the definitive treatment. Treating any lichen sclerosus matters beyond comfort, because long-standing scarring slightly raises the risk of penile cancer.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly: never force a child's foreskin back, treat balanitis and skin conditions promptly rather than letting scarring build up, and wash gently under the foreskin once it retracts naturally.

## Paraphimosis
**Summary:** The foreskin has been pulled back behind the glans and gets stuck there, forming a tight, swelling ring that cuts off blood flow. This is an emergency: it needs urgent treatment, and the sooner it is dealt with, the simpler that treatment is.
**Image:** images/paraphimosis.jpg
**What it is:** When a foreskin is left retracted — after washing, sex, a medical examination, or catheter care — fluid builds up in the trapped fold of skin, which swells and acts like a tightening band behind the glans. The glans itself becomes swollen and painful, and if the band isn't released, its blood supply is strangled. It is a classic, preventable problem in elderly men with urinary catheters, where the foreskin is pulled back for catheter care and forgotten.
**Why it happens:** Almost always because a retracted foreskin wasn't returned to its normal position covering the glans. The risk is higher when the foreskin is already somewhat tight (phimosis), after procedures involving a catheter, and in men who need help with personal care.
**Signs to watch for:** A swollen, tight ring of foreskin stuck behind a swollen, painful glans that cannot be pushed back into place. The glans may change colour if blood flow is badly restricted. This is on the card's emergency list: get emergency care now — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** By looking. No tests are needed and none should delay treatment — the appearance is unmistakable.
**How it's treated:** A doctor usually first reduces the swelling by gentle firm squeezing, sometimes with numbing gel or an injection, then slides the foreskin back over the glans manually. If that fails, a small cut to release the tight band, or emergency circumcision, frees it. Once the immediate problem is fixed, circumcision may be advised to stop it happening again.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes — almost always. The rule from the card: always pull the foreskin back into place after washing, after sex, and after any catheter care. Carers and family members helping an older man with a catheter should know this simple step, because it prevents the whole emergency.

## Sexually transmitted infections
**Summary:** Infections passed on through sexual contact that can affect the penis — causing sores, discharge, or warts, but often causing no symptoms at all. Testing is the only way to know, treatment is effective, and partners need treating too.
**Image:** images/penile-stis.jpg
**What it is:** Several different infections are grouped here. Sores on the penis can be caused by syphilis (typically a painless sore) or herpes (painful blisters and sores that come and go). A discharge from the tip, often with burning when passing urine, is typical of gonorrhoea or chlamydia. Warts — soft growths on the penis or around it — are caused by HPV, the human papillomavirus. The same infections in women are covered on the vagina page.
**Why it happens:** Sexual contact — vaginal, oral, or anal — with an infected partner, including contact where no symptoms are visible. Condoms greatly reduce the risk but don't cover all skin-to-skin spread, which is how herpes and HPV often pass on.
**Signs to watch for:** Any new sore, blister, ulcer, or wart on the penis; discharge from the tip; burning when passing urine; itching or rash. But the most important fact is that many sexually transmitted infections cause no symptoms at all — chlamydia in particular is often silent — so feeling fine proves nothing.
**How doctors confirm it:** With STI tests: a urine sample, a swab from the tip or from any sore, and blood tests for syphilis and HIV. Sexual health clinics do this routinely, usually free of embarrassment and fuss — testing is a normal part of looking after yourself.
**How it's treated:** Bacterial infections such as syphilis, gonorrhoea, and chlamydia are treated with antibiotics. Herpes can't be cured, but antiviral medicines shorten and soften attacks. Warts can be treated with creams, freezing, or minor procedures, though they can recur. Partners need testing and treatment too, and sex should wait until the clinic says it's safe — otherwise the infection simply passes back and forth.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, to a large extent: using condoms, limiting partners, and regular testing — especially with a new partner. An HPV vaccine exists and is offered to young people in many countries; it protects against the wart-causing strains and lowers the risk of related cancers.

## Penile cancer
**Summary:** A rare cancer of the penis, usually appearing as a sore, lump, or patch that doesn't heal. It is uncommon, but catching it early matters, because early treatment can often remove the cancer while preserving most or all of the penis.
**Image:** images/penile-cancer.svg
**What it is:** Cancer starting in the skin or tissues of the penis, most often on the glans or foreskin. A sore, lump, or patch that doesn't heal is the typical first sign, and it is easily mistaken for infection or a skin condition — which is why any change that doesn't settle deserves a proper look. See our cancer section for the general picture of how cancers are diagnosed and treated.
**Why it happens:** Risk is raised by HPV infection, phimosis (a foreskin that cannot retract, which allows chronic inflammation underneath), lichen sclerosus, smoking, and a weakened immune system. It is much less common in men circumcised in childhood.
**Signs to watch for:** A sore, lump, wart-like growth, or red or pale patch that doesn't heal; bleeding, discharge, or an odour under the foreskin; thickening or change in colour of the skin. These overlap with harmless conditions, but persistence is the key warning.
**How doctors confirm it:** By examination and a biopsy — removing a small sample of the abnormal tissue to examine under a microscope. Scans may be used to check whether it has spread.
**How it's treated:** The earlier it is found, the more tissue-sparing the options: creams or laser treatment for the earliest surface changes, surgery to remove the affected area for larger tumours, and treatment of groin lymph nodes if needed.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — HPV vaccination, not smoking, treating phimosis and lichen sclerosus rather than ignoring them, and washing gently under the foreskin all lower the risk.
