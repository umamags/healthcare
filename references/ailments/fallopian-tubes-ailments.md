## Blocked fallopian tubes
**Summary:** One or both tubes are partly or completely closed off, so the egg and sperm can't meet — a common cause of difficulty conceiving. It usually causes no symptoms at all, and many women only discover it during fertility tests.
**Image:** images/tubal-blockage.jpg
**What it is:** The fallopian tubes are very narrow, especially near the womb. If the inside of a tube is blocked, or scar tissue sticks the tube to nearby organs, the egg can't travel down and sperm can't swim up. If only one tube is blocked, pregnancy is still possible through the other. If both are blocked, natural conception is very unlikely without treatment.
**Why it happens:** The commonest cause is scarring left behind by a past infection — often pelvic inflammatory disease (see the entry below), sometimes an infection the woman never knew she had. Endometriosis (womb-lining tissue growing outside the womb) can also distort or block the tubes, as can scar tissue from earlier pelvic or abdominal surgery, a previous ectopic pregnancy, or a burst appendix.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none — blocked tubes don't affect periods, and most women feel completely well. The usual clue is simply not conceiving after trying for a year (or six months if over 35). Some women have ongoing pelvic pain, especially if endometriosis or past infection is part of the picture.
**How doctors confirm it:** The main test is an HSG — a dye X-ray in which dye is passed through the womb; if it doesn't spill out of the tube ends, the tube is blocked. A similar ultrasound test uses foam or saline instead of X-ray dye. Laparoscopy (keyhole surgery with dye) is the most detailed check and lets the surgeon see the tubes and any scar tissue directly.
**How it's treated:** It depends on where and how badly the tube is damaged. Keyhole surgery can sometimes remove scar tissue or open a mildly blocked tube, but it works best for limited damage and carries a risk of ectopic pregnancy afterwards. When damage is severe, IVF (fertility treatment where eggs are fertilised in the laboratory, bypassing the tubes completely) is usually the more realistic route. A fertility specialist helps weigh the two.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Preventing and promptly treating sexually transmitted infections protects the tubes from their commonest cause of scarring. Other causes, such as endometriosis, can't currently be prevented — so unexplained difficulty conceiving is always worth investigating rather than waiting and hoping.

## Hydrosalpinx
**Summary:** A blocked tube that has swollen up with fluid, like a small sausage-shaped water balloon. It usually causes no symptoms and is often spotted for the first time during fertility investigations or a pelvic scan.
**Image:** images/hydrosalpinx.png
**What it is:** "Hydro" means water and "salpinx" is the medical word for the tube. When the outer end of a tube (nearest the ovary) seals shut, fluid that the tube lining normally produces has nowhere to drain, so the tube gradually stretches and fills. One or both tubes can be affected. The tube itself no longer works — an egg can't pass through it — and the trapped fluid can also leak back into the womb.
**Why it happens:** It's the end result of earlier damage to the tube, most often a past pelvic infection (pelvic inflammatory disease) that scarred the tube's delicate finger-like ends closed. Endometriosis, previous pelvic surgery, and a previous ectopic pregnancy or tubal surgery can lead to the same thing.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none. Some women notice a watery vaginal discharge, or pelvic discomfort. Most commonly it's found when a couple is being investigated for infertility, or seen on a scan done for another reason.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic ultrasound often shows the swollen, fluid-filled tube as a distinctive sausage shape beside the ovary. An HSG dye test shows dye entering the tube but not spilling out. Laparoscopy (keyhole surgery) confirms it directly if there's any doubt.
**How it's treated:** A hydrosalpinx can't be drained or repaired in a way that reliably restores it — the inner lining is usually too damaged. If a woman isn't trying to conceive and has no symptoms, it can simply be left alone. Before IVF, doctors often advise removing the affected tube or clipping it off from the womb, because fluid draining from the tube into the womb can wash over the lining and make it harder for an embryo to implant there. Treating it first noticeably improves the chance of IVF working. Surgery is usually keyhole, and the healthy tube and ovary are left untouched.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only in the sense that its causes can — protecting against sexually transmitted infections and treating pelvic infections early reduces the chance of the tubal damage that leads to it.

## Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
**Summary:** An infection of the womb, fallopian tubes, and sometimes the ovaries, usually caused by a sexually transmitted infection that has spread upwards. It can be surprisingly quiet — mild or even symptom-free — yet still scar the tubes, which is why prompt treatment matters so much.
**Image:** images/pelvic-inflammatory-disease.png
**What it is:** PID is an infection of the upper female reproductive organs. Bacteria travel up from the vagina and cervix into the womb and out into the tubes. The inflammation there is what does the damage: as it heals, it can leave scar tissue that blocks the tubes, sticks organs together, or raises the risk of an ectopic pregnancy later. A severe bout can also form a pocket of pus (an abscess) around the tube and ovary.
**Why it happens:** The most common causes are the sexually transmitted infections chlamydia and gonorrhoea, which often cause no symptoms in the vagina and cervix — so the infection can climb upwards unnoticed. Other vaginal bacteria can occasionally cause PID too, for example after childbirth, a miscarriage, or a procedure through the cervix.
**Signs to watch for:** These range from nothing at all to obvious illness. Possible signs include lower abdominal or pelvic pain, unusual vaginal discharge, pain during sex, bleeding between periods or after sex, pain when passing urine, and fever. Severe pain, vomiting, or high fever needs urgent care: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A doctor examines the pelvis, takes swabs from the cervix to test for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and other infections, and usually does a pregnancy test (to rule out ectopic pregnancy, which can feel similar). Blood tests can show signs of infection. An ultrasound may be used to look for an abscess. Because PID is hard to prove and delay causes damage, doctors often start antibiotics on suspicion rather than waiting for every result.
**How it's treated:** A course of antibiotics — usually a combination covering the likely bacteria — started as early as possible. Mild cases are treated with tablets at home; severe cases, pregnancy, or an abscess may need hospital treatment with antibiotics through a drip, and occasionally surgery to drain an abscess. Sexual partners need testing and treatment too, and sex should wait until treatment is finished, or the infection simply passes back and forth.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Condoms with new partners greatly reduce the risk, and regular STI testing catches chlamydia and gonorrhoea while they're still silent and easy to cure. If you're diagnosed with an STI, completing treatment and making sure partners are treated protects your tubes for the future.

## Ectopic pregnancy
**Summary:** A pregnancy that implants outside the womb — almost always in a fallopian tube, which cannot stretch to hold a growing pregnancy. It's an emergency: as it grows the tube can burst, causing dangerous internal bleeding. It cannot be moved into the womb and sadly cannot survive.
**Image:** images/ectopic-pregnancy.svg
**What it is:** Normally a fertilised egg travels down the tube and implants in the womb lining. In an ectopic pregnancy the egg gets stuck and implants in the tube wall instead. The tube is narrow and thin-walled, so the pregnancy outgrows it within weeks. If it bursts the tube, blood pours into the abdomen — this is life-threatening and needs emergency surgery.
**Why it happens:** Anything that slows the egg's journey raises the risk: tubes scarred by earlier infection (PID), previous tubal surgery or a previous ectopic pregnancy, endometriosis, and conceiving with a coil in place or after tubal sterilisation. Smoking also increases the risk. Often, though, no clear cause is found.
**Signs to watch for:** A missed period or positive pregnancy test plus one-sided lower abdominal pain is the classic warning. There may be unusual vaginal bleeding (often dark), pain in the tip of the shoulder, pain when passing urine or stools, dizziness, or fainting. Any of these in early pregnancy is an emergency: call your local emergency number now — don't wait to see if it settles.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pregnancy test (hCG) in blood or urine, blood hCG levels checked over time (they rise abnormally slowly in most ectopics), and a pelvic ultrasound — usually through the vagina — to see whether the womb is empty and whether there's a pregnancy or free fluid near a tube. Sometimes repeat scans and blood tests over a few days are needed before the picture is certain.
**How it's treated:** It depends on size, symptoms, and blood results. Very early, stable ectopics can sometimes be treated with a medicine called methotrexate — an injection that stops the pregnancy growing, after which the body gradually absorbs it; this needs close follow-up blood tests. Otherwise, keyhole surgery removes the pregnancy, either taking the affected tube with it or opening the tube to remove the pregnancy while leaving the tube. A burst tube means emergency surgery and possibly a blood transfusion.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not directly — but protecting the tubes from infection lowers the risk. After one ectopic pregnancy, the chance of another is higher, so future pregnancies need an early scan (usually around 6–7 weeks) to confirm the pregnancy is in the womb. Seek care immediately for any pain or bleeding in a new pregnancy.

## Tubal cancer
**Summary:** Cancer starting in a fallopian tube is rare, and this is only a short entry. It's often found by accident during surgery for something else, and doctors now believe many cancers once labelled "ovarian" actually begin in the tube.
**Image:** images/tubal-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Most tubal cancers begin in the lining near the tube's outer, finger-like end. Many high-grade cancers once labelled "ovarian" are now thought to start as tiny growths in exactly this spot, then spread to the ovary. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Risk factors overlap with ovarian cancer: inherited gene changes (such as BRCA1 and BRCA2), increasing age, and a family history of ovarian or breast cancer.
**Signs to watch for:** Early on, usually none. Later there may be pelvic pain or pressure, a watery or blood-stained discharge, or abdominal swelling — vague symptoms with many commoner causes.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often it's discovered unexpectedly, when a tube removed during other surgery is examined under the microscope. Otherwise, ultrasound, CT scans, and blood tests lead to surgery, which confirms it.
**How it's treated:** Usually surgery to remove the womb, tubes, and ovaries, often followed by chemotherapy — the same approach as for ovarian cancer, by a specialist cancer team.
**Can it be prevented?:** There's no screening test. Women with a strong inherited risk sometimes discuss preventive removal of the tubes and ovaries once childbearing is complete.
