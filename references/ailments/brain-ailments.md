## Stroke
**Summary:** A stroke happens when the blood supply to part of the brain is suddenly cut off, starving brain cells of oxygen. It is a medical emergency — fast treatment can save brain tissue and reduce disability, which is why specialists say "time is brain."
**Image:** images/stroke.jpg
**What it is:** There are two main types. In an ischaemic stroke, an artery supplying the brain becomes blocked, usually by a blood clot. In a haemorrhagic stroke, a blood vessel bursts and bleeds into or around the brain. Both stop blood reaching brain tissue, and cells in the affected area begin to die within minutes. A related event, a transient ischaemic attack (TIA or "mini-stroke"), causes the same symptoms but settles within minutes to hours — it is a warning sign of a possible bigger stroke and still needs urgent assessment. Learn the **FAST** check: **F**ace — has the face drooped on one side? **A**rm — can the person raise both arms and keep them there? **S**peech — is speech slurred or hard to understand? **T**ime — if you see any of these, it's time to call the emergency number immediately.
**Why it happens:** The main risk factors are high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, high cholesterol, and an irregular heartbeat called atrial fibrillation, which lets clots form in the heart and travel to the brain. Risk rises with age, and unhealthy arteries (narrowed by fatty deposits) make blockages more likely. Bleeding strokes are often linked to long-standing high blood pressure weakening vessel walls.
**Signs to watch for:** Sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm, or leg — especially on one side; sudden confusion or trouble speaking or understanding; sudden vision problems; sudden severe headache with no known cause; sudden dizziness or loss of balance. Any of these means emergency care now — call your local emergency number, even if the symptoms fade.
**How doctors confirm it:** Speed is everything. In the emergency department, doctors do a neurological exam and an urgent CT scan to tell a blockage from a bleed (treatment differs completely), sometimes followed by MRI and scans of the neck and heart arteries. Blood tests and a heart tracing help find the cause.
**How it's treated:** For a blockage, approaches doctors commonly consider include clot-busting medicines given within a few hours and, for larger clots, a procedure to physically remove the clot (thrombectomy). Bleeding strokes may need blood-pressure control and sometimes surgery. Afterwards, antiplatelet or anticoagulant medicines, cholesterol-lowering medicines, and rehabilitation (physiotherapy, speech and occupational therapy) help recovery and prevent another stroke.
**Can it be prevented?:** Often, yes. Controlling blood pressure is the single biggest step; not smoking, managing diabetes and cholesterol, staying active, limiting alcohol, and treating atrial fibrillation all substantially lower risk.

## Dementia (including Alzheimer's disease)
**Summary:** Dementia is an umbrella term for conditions that progressively damage memory, thinking, and behaviour enough to interfere with daily life. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause, but it is not the only one — and dementia is not a normal part of ageing.
**Image:** images/dementia.jpg
**What it is:** "Dementia" describes a set of symptoms; several diseases can cause it. Alzheimer's disease — where abnormal proteins build up in the brain — accounts for the majority of cases. Others include vascular dementia (from reduced blood flow, often after strokes), dementia with Lewy bodies, and frontotemporal dementia, which tends to affect behaviour and language earlier in life. Normal ageing brings occasional forgetfulness (misplacing keys, taking longer to recall a name) but the person copes independently; in dementia, the decline is greater, progressive, and interferes with everyday tasks.
**Why it happens:** Age is the strongest risk factor, and family history plays a part. Many of the same factors that harm blood vessels raise dementia risk: high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, high cholesterol, and hearing loss. Head injuries and heavy alcohol use also contribute in some cases. For Alzheimer's specifically, why the protein build-up starts is not fully understood.
**Signs to watch for:** Memory loss that affects daily life (repeating questions, forgetting recent events), getting lost in familiar places, difficulty managing money or familiar tasks, language problems, and changes in mood, personality, or judgement. Symptoms come on gradually over years — a sudden confusion is more likely something else (such as an infection) and needs prompt medical review.
**How doctors confirm it:** There is no single test. Doctors take a detailed history — often including family who know the person well — plus memory and thinking tests, blood tests to rule out reversible causes (such as thyroid problems or vitamin B12 deficiency), and usually a CT or MRI scan. Specialist memory clinics may add more detailed assessments.
**How it's treated:** There is currently no cure for Alzheimer's or most other dementias, but medicines that support brain signalling chemicals (such as cholinesterase inhibitors, and memantine) can ease symptoms for a time in some people. Just as important are treating blood pressure and other vessel risks, hearing aids, staying physically, mentally, and socially active, and practical support, safety planning, and help for carers.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully, but a substantial share of risk is thought to be modifiable: keep blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar controlled, don't smoke, stay active, treat hearing loss, maintain social and mental activity, and limit alcohol.

## Parkinson's disease
**Summary:** Parkinson's is a progressive condition in which brain cells that produce dopamine — a chemical needed for smooth, controlled movement — gradually die off. It mainly causes tremor, stiffness, and slowness, and while there is no cure, treatments can ease symptoms for many years.
**Image:** images/parkinsons.png
**What it is:** Dopamine acts as a messenger in the movement-control circuits of the brain. As those cells are lost, movements become slower and less automatic. Parkinson's usually starts after age 50 and progresses slowly. It is not contagious and is not caused by anything a person did or didn't do. The same person often has non-movement symptoms too, because Parkinson's affects other brain systems.
**Why it happens:** The underlying cause is not fully known. A small minority of cases run in families due to specific gene changes; for most people it appears to be a mix of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors. Age is the biggest risk factor. It is not caused by stress, though stress can make symptoms more noticeable.
**Signs to watch for:** A tremor at rest (often starting in one hand), stiffness in the limbs, slowness of movement, smaller handwriting, reduced facial expression, and a shuffling walk with balance problems later on. Early non-movement clues can include loss of sense of smell, constipation, acting out dreams during sleep, and low mood. See a doctor promptly if tremor, stiffness, or slowness appears — several treatable conditions can look similar.
**How doctors confirm it:** Diagnosis is mainly clinical: a neurological exam and the characteristic pattern of symptoms, often confirmed by how well symptoms respond to dopamine-replacing medicine. Scans (MRI) are sometimes used to rule out other causes, and a specialised dopamine scan may help in uncertain cases. Blood tests and lumbar puncture are not usually needed.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider centre on restoring dopamine signalling: levodopa (the most effective medicine, converted to dopamine in the brain), medicines that mimic dopamine, and medicines that slow dopamine breakdown. Physiotherapy, exercise, speech therapy, and occupational therapy are core parts of care. For selected patients with troublesome fluctuations, deep brain stimulation surgery may be considered.
**Can it be prevented?:** No proven prevention exists. Regular exercise doesn't guarantee avoiding Parkinson's, but it clearly helps mobility, balance, and quality of life for people living with it — and staying active is good general advice for brain health.

## Epilepsy
**Summary:** Epilepsy is a tendency to have repeated seizures — brief episodes caused by abnormal bursts of electrical activity in the brain. With the right medicine, many people with epilepsy become seizure-free; knowing simple seizure first aid helps everyone around them.
**Image:** images/epilepsy.png
**What it is:** A single seizure doesn't necessarily mean epilepsy — seizures can be provoked by fever in young children, very low blood sugar, or alcohol withdrawal. Epilepsy means an ongoing tendency to unprovoked seizures. Seizures vary: generalised convulsions (whole-body stiffening and jerking with loss of consciousness), focal seizures (affecting one body part or awareness, sometimes with odd sensations or behaviour), and brief "absence" episodes of staring. What happens depends on which part of the brain is involved.
**Why it happens:** Sometimes a cause is found — a previous stroke, head injury, brain infection, tumour, or a genetic tendency — and often no cause is identified. Common triggers for seizures in people with epilepsy include missed medicine doses, lack of sleep, illness, flashing lights (in a small minority), and alcohol.
**Signs to watch for:** Any episode of unexplained collapse with jerking, a blank spell with unresponsiveness, repeated lip-smacking or fumbling movements, or waking with a bitten tongue, muscle aches, or confusion suggests a possible seizure and should be checked — a first seizure always deserves a prompt doctor visit. **Seizure first aid:** protect the person from injury (cushion the head, move objects away), time the seizure, put nothing in their mouth, and don't restrain them. As it ends, roll them onto their side. Call your local emergency number if the seizure lasts more than about 5 minutes, repeats, the person is injured, pregnant, has diabetes, doesn't recover normally, or it's their first seizure.
**How doctors confirm it:** A detailed account of the episodes — ideally from someone who saw one (a phone video can help) — plus a neurological exam. An EEG records the brain's electrical patterns and may show epileptic activity. MRI looks for a structural cause, and blood tests check for triggers or mimics.
**How it's treated:** The mainstay is daily anti-seizure medication, chosen by seizure type; getting the dose right often takes some adjustment. For people whose seizures persist despite medicines, doctors may consider epilepsy surgery, implanted stimulators, or specialised diets (mainly in children). Driving and safety rules apply and vary by place — doctors will advise.
**Can it be prevented?:** Epilepsy itself often can't be, but some causes can: preventing head injuries (helmets, seatbelts), good care around childbirth, treating brain infections promptly, and stroke prevention all reduce cases. For people with epilepsy, taking medicine reliably and avoiding known triggers prevents seizures.

## Migraine
**Summary:** Migraine is much more than "just a bad headache" — it's a neurological condition causing attacks of moderate-to-severe, often one-sided, throbbing pain, typically with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. Attacks can last hours to days and can be genuinely disabling.
**Image:** images/migraine.png
**What it is:** During an attack, brain activity and blood vessels change in a cascade that activates pain pathways. Some people get an "aura" beforehand — flashing lights, zigzag lines, blind spots, or tingling — that builds up over minutes and fades as the headache starts. Without aura is more common. Many people also feel unusually tired, irritable, or crave foods in the day before (the warning phase), and washed-out afterwards.
**Why it happens:** Migraine runs in families, so genes play a large role, and it's more common in women, likely due to hormonal influences. Attacks are set off by triggers that vary by person: poor sleep, skipped meals, dehydration, stress (or the let-down after it), certain foods and alcohol, bright light, and hormonal changes around periods. Triggers stack — several small ones together may tip into an attack.
**Signs to watch for:** Headache that is moderate to severe, often one-sided and throbbing, worse with movement, lasting 4–72 hours, with nausea and/or marked sensitivity to light and sound. Importantly, some headaches are **not** migraine and need urgent checks: a sudden "thunderclap" headache reaching maximum within seconds (the worst ever), headache after a head injury, headache with fever and a stiff neck, a new headache pattern after age 50, or headache with weakness, confusion, or vision loss. Any of these — call your local emergency number or seek emergency care.
**How doctors confirm it:** Diagnosis is based on the pattern of attacks from your history — there's no single blood test or scan for migraine. Doctors may arrange a CT or MRI if there are red flags or the pattern is unusual. Keeping a simple headache diary (when, how long, possible triggers, what helped) is genuinely useful.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider fall into two groups. Acute treatment, taken at attack onset: pain relievers, and migraine-specific medicines (triptans, and newer gepant-class medicines), plus anti-nausea medicines. Preventive treatment, taken daily or periodically when attacks are frequent: certain blood-pressure, anti-seizure, or antidepressant medicines (used for their migraine effect), and newer antibody treatments targeting a migraine-related chemical (CGRP). Resting in a dark, quiet room and treating early help most attacks.
**Can it be prevented?:** Attacks often can be reduced, even if the tendency can't be removed: regular sleep, meals, and exercise, staying hydrated, and identifying and managing your personal triggers. If you're using acute pain relievers most days, tell your doctor — overuse itself can cause more headaches.

## Brain tumours
**Summary:** A brain tumour is an abnormal growth of cells in or around the brain. Many brain tumours are benign (non-cancerous) and some are cancerous; even benign ones can cause serious problems because the skull leaves no room to expand. See our cancer section for the wider picture.
**Image:** images/brain-tumours.jpg
**What it is:** Primary tumours start in the brain or its coverings (meningiomas, from the coverings, are often benign; gliomas start in the brain's supporting cells). Secondary (metastatic) tumours have spread from a cancer elsewhere, such as the lung or breast, and are actually more common than primary ones. Symptoms come less from the tumour type than from pressure: on surrounding brain, on fluid pathways, or on specific areas controlling movement, speech, or vision.
**Why it happens:** For most brain tumours, no cause is found and nothing the person did caused it. Known risk factors are few: previous radiation to the head and some rare inherited conditions account for a small share. Despite popular worry, there's no solid evidence that mobile phones cause brain tumours. Age matters — some types are more common in children, others in older adults.
**Signs to watch for:** Headaches that are new, progressively worse, worse in the morning or when lying down or coughing; seizures in someone who's never had them; gradually worsening weakness, clumsiness, speech, or vision problems; personality or behaviour changes; nausea and vomiting from raised pressure. These symptoms are far more often caused by something else, but a new, persistent, or progressive pattern should always be checked promptly.
**How doctors confirm it:** A neurological exam followed by MRI (the key test — it shows the tumour's size, location, and effect on surrounding brain), sometimes CT first. A tissue sample (biopsy, or tissue taken during surgery) is usually needed to name the exact type, which guides treatment. Lumbar puncture is generally avoided until imaging shows it's safe, because of the pressure issue.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include surgery (to remove as much as safely possible and to get tissue for diagnosis), radiotherapy, and chemotherapy or targeted medicines, in combinations depending on type and location. Steroid medicines reduce swelling around the tumour and often relieve symptoms quickly; anti-seizure medicines are used if seizures occur. Rehabilitation and specialist nursing support recovery, and benign, slow-growing tumours may simply be monitored with regular scans.
**Can it be prevented?:** For the most part, no — there's no established lifestyle prevention for brain tumours. The practical step is not prevention but prompt attention: new persistent headaches, a first seizure, or gradually worsening neurological symptoms deserve timely assessment so treatment, when needed, starts early.

## Traumatic brain injury
**Summary:** Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is damage to the brain from a blow, jolt, or penetrating injury — ranging from concussion at the milder end to severe injury with bleeding or swelling. Most concussions recover fully over days to weeks, but repeat injury before recovery is a real risk.
**Image:** images/traumatic-brain-injury.jpg
**What it is:** Concussion is a temporary disturbance of brain function after an impact that shakes the brain inside the skull. Crucially, standard CT and MRI scans are often completely normal in concussion — the injury is functional, not structural, so a clear scan doesn't mean nothing happened. More severe TBI can involve bruising of the brain, bleeding inside the skull, and swelling, which can be life-threatening and needs emergency care.
**Why it happens:** Falls are the leading cause, especially in young children and older adults; road traffic collisions and sports impacts are the other major sources, with assaults and workplace injuries contributing too. Alcohol is involved in a significant share of injuries. Anything that reduces falls and impacts — or cushions them with helmets and seatbelts — reduces TBI.
**Signs to watch for:** After concussion: headache, dizziness, nausea, feeling "foggy" or slowed down, sensitivity to light and noise, irritability, sleep changes, and memory or concentration problems. Most improve steadily over days to a few weeks. **Danger signs after any head injury** — worsening headache, repeated vomiting, increasing drowsiness or confusion, weakness, slurred speech, unequal pupils, a seizure, clear fluid from the nose or ear, or behaviour changes in a child — mean emergency care now: call your local emergency number. Anyone who was knocked out should be assessed the same day.
**How doctors confirm it:** Assessment starts with the history (how it happened, any loss of consciousness or memory gap) and a neurological exam, including alertness scoring. CT is used when there's concern about bleeding — particularly with danger signs, vomiting, older age, or blood-thinning medicines. MRI may follow for persistent symptoms. For concussion, structured symptom checklists and balance/cognitive testing help track recovery.
**How it's treated:** Concussion care is mainly guided recovery: a day or two of relative rest (not strict bed or dark-room isolation), then gradually resuming normal activity and light exercise as tolerated, with pain relievers for headache and attention to sleep. Return to sport or risky work should be stepwise and only after symptoms settle — a second impact before recovery can cause much worse injury, so this caution matters, especially for young athletes. Moderate and severe TBI need hospital care: monitoring pressure and bleeding, surgery if required, and rehabilitation (physiotherapy, occupational, speech, and cognitive therapy).
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Helmets for cycling, skiing, and contact sports; seatbelts and child car seats; fall prevention at home (lighting, rails, reviewing medicines that cause dizziness in older adults); and not driving under the influence all prevent brain injuries. Prevention works far better than any treatment after the fact.
