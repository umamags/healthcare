Ailments of the skin — expanded from the skin card.

## Acne
**Summary:** Acne is a very common condition in which oil glands and hair follicles become blocked and inflamed, causing spots, blackheads, and sometimes deeper, painful lumps. It affects most teenagers and many adults, and it is not caused by dirty skin.
**Image:** images/acne.jpg
**What it is:** Each pore contains a tiny oil (sebaceous) gland. When oil and dead skin cells block the pore, a spot forms — a blackhead if the pore stays open, a whitehead if it closes, and an inflamed red spot or pus-filled spot if bacteria multiply and the wall of the pore becomes irritated. In more severe acne, deeper nodules and cysts develop under the skin, and these are the ones most likely to scar.
**Why it happens:** Hormones are the main driver — at puberty, and in many women around periods, pregnancy, or conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, hormone changes make glands produce more oil. Genetics plays a part: acne often runs in families. Certain medicines, heavy or oily cosmetics, and friction from helmets or tight straps can aggravate it. Contrary to the common myth, acne is not caused by poor hygiene, and scrubbing harder usually makes inflammation worse, not better.
**Signs to watch for:** Spots on the face, chest, shoulders, or back, ranging from blackheads to tender, deep lumps. Beyond the skin itself, acne commonly affects mood and self-confidence, and that impact deserves to be taken seriously — low mood alongside severe acne is a reason to raise both with your doctor. Picking or squeezing spots drives infection deeper and increases the risk of permanent scars and dark marks, so hands off, however tempting.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by a skin exam alone — the appearance and distribution of the spots are characteristic. Blood tests are not routine, but a doctor may check hormone levels in adult women with sudden, severe, or treatment-resistant acne, and a biopsy is rarely needed.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include vitamin-A-derived creams (topical retinoids), benzoyl peroxide creams, and topical or oral antibiotics for inflamed acne. For women, hormonal treatments are sometimes used. Severe or scarring acne may be treated with a closely monitored vitamin-A tablet (isotretinoin). Most treatments take several weeks to show results, and doctors often advise gentle cleansing and non-comedogenic moisturisers alongside.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely — hormones and genetics can't be changed. But gentle skin care, avoiding oily products on acne-prone areas, not picking, and starting treatment early in moderate or severe cases all reduce the risk of it worsening and scarring.

## Eczema (atopic dermatitis)
**Summary:** Eczema is a long-term condition in which the skin barrier doesn't hold moisture well, leaving skin dry, itchy, and prone to inflamed flare-ups. It often starts in childhood and commonly runs in families alongside asthma and hay fever.
**Image:** images/eczema.jpg
**What it is:** In healthy skin, the outer barrier keeps water in and irritants and germs out. In eczema that barrier is leaky, so skin dries out and reacts strongly to things that wouldn't bother other people. The hallmark is the itch–scratch cycle: itching leads to scratching, scratching damages the barrier further, and the damaged skin itches even more. Flares come and go, and the skin may thicken in areas that are scratched repeatedly.
**Why it happens:** A combination of genetics (including inherited differences in skin-barrier proteins), an over-reactive immune response, and environmental triggers. Common triggers include harsh soaps and detergents, fragrances, wool, dust mites, animal dander, cold dry weather, heat and sweating, stress, and skin infections. Food allergies drive eczema in a minority of young children, but most eczema is not a food-allergy problem.
**Signs to watch for:** Dry, itchy, red patches — in babies often on the cheeks and scalp; in older children in the elbow and knee creases; in adults often on the hands, face, and flexures. Itching frequently disturbs sleep. Scratched skin can become infected: increasing pain, weeping, yellow crusts, or rapidly spreading redness need prompt medical care.
**How doctors confirm it:** By a skin exam and history — the pattern of itch, dryness, and distribution is usually enough. Patch testing may be used to look for contact allergies adding to the problem, and a skin scraping or swab can check for fungal infection or bacterial infection when the picture is unclear.
**How it's treated:** The foundation is daily moisturisers and gentle, fragrance-free cleansing to support the barrier. Flares are treated with topical corticosteroid creams or ointments; some people worry about these, but doctors commonly advise using them exactly as directed — they are a standard, effective treatment when used correctly. Other approaches include non-steroid anti-inflammatory creams (calcineurin inhibitors), wet-wrap dressings for severe flares, light therapy, and, for severe eczema, immune-calming tablets or injectable biologic medicines. Antihistamines are sometimes used at night to aid sleep rather than to stop the itch itself.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully, since genetics plays a large role. Daily moisturising from early life, avoiding known triggers, and treating flares promptly can reduce their frequency and severity.

## Psoriasis
**Summary:** Psoriasis is a long-term, immune-driven condition that speeds up skin-cell production, producing thick, scaly, well-defined patches. It is not an infection and it is not contagious — you cannot catch it or pass it on.
**Image:** images/psoriasis.jpg
**What it is:** Normally, skin cells take about a month to form and shed. In psoriasis, immune-system signals speed that process up to just a few days, so cells pile up into raised plaques with silvery scale. It most often affects the elbows, knees, scalp, and lower back, and it can also affect nails, which may become pitted, thickened, or lift away. Severity varies widely, from a few small patches to widespread involvement.
**Why it happens:** The underlying cause is an immune system over-reacting in the skin, with a strong genetic component — psoriasis often runs in families. Flares can be triggered by infections (especially strep throat), skin injury (cuts, sunburn — new plaques may appear at the injury site), stress, cold weather, smoking, heavy alcohol use, and certain medicines.
**Signs to watch for:** Well-defined pink or red plaques with silvery scale, sometimes itchy or sore, and cracking or bleeding in skin folds or on palms and soles. Two associated problems matter: some people develop psoriatic arthritis — joint pain, stiffness, and swollen fingers or toes — which needs early treatment to protect the joints; and psoriasis is linked with a higher risk of heart and metabolic disease, so blood pressure, cholesterol, and weight deserve attention too. A sudden widespread flare with fever and feeling unwell is uncommon but needs urgent care.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by skin exam — the plaques look characteristic, and a dermatoscope can help. A skin biopsy is used if the diagnosis is uncertain. Doctors also check nails and ask about joint symptoms, and blood tests are done before starting stronger immune treatments.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider start with creams and ointments — topical corticosteroids, vitamin D–based creams, and preparations that soften scale. Light therapy (controlled ultraviolet treatment) helps more widespread disease. For severe psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, doctors use immune-calming tablets or injectable biologic medicines that target specific immune signals, with regular monitoring. Moisturisers and gentle skin care support all of these.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — it arises from genetics and the immune system. But avoiding smoking, limiting alcohol, protecting skin from injury and sunburn, and treating infections promptly can reduce flares, and good treatment can keep it well controlled.

## Fungal skin infections (ringworm and athlete's foot)
**Summary:** These infections are caused by fungi that live on skin, hair, and nails — despite its name, ringworm has nothing to do with worms. They are common, mildly contagious, and usually clear with antifungal treatment.
**Image:** images/fungal-skin-infections.jpg
**What it is:** The fungi involved (usually dermatophytes) feed on keratin, the protein in the outer skin, hair, and nails, and they thrive in warm, moist conditions. On the body, the infection is called ringworm because it often forms a ring-shaped patch with a clearer centre and a scaly, slightly raised edge. Between the toes it is athlete's foot — itchy, cracked, peeling skin. The same family of fungi can affect the groin ("jock itch"), the scalp (mainly in children), and the nails, which become thick, brittle, and discoloured.
**Why it happens:** The fungi spread through skin contact and via shared towels, clothing, footwear, and damp surfaces such as gym and pool floors. Tight shoes, sweaty feet, and not drying properly between the toes create the moist environment they like. Pets — especially kittens and puppies — can carry ringworm and pass it to people.
**Signs to watch for:** A gradually enlarging ring-shaped, scaly, itchy patch on the body; itching, peeling, and cracking between the toes; an itchy red rash in the groin folds; or round patches of scalp scaling with hair loss in a child. One important trap: using a steroid cream alone on a fungal rash can make it spread and look less typical while the infection worsens underneath, so a rash that "improves then rebounds" on steroid cream should be rechecked.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often by skin exam alone. To be sure, a doctor may take a skin scraping for examination under a microscope or culture, which identifies the fungus. Nail infections are usually confirmed with nail clippings before starting tablets.
**How it's treated:** Limited skin infections usually clear with antifungal creams applied for the full recommended course, including a week or so after the rash looks gone. Widespread infections, scalp infections, and nail infections generally need oral antifungal tablets, because creams don't reach hair roots or nail beds well. Doctors also advise washing and drying skin folds carefully and not sharing towels while treating.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Keep skin — especially between the toes and in groin folds — clean and dry; change sweaty socks; wear footwear in communal showers and locker rooms; don't share towels, socks, or shoes; and treat athlete's foot promptly so it doesn't spread to the groin or nails. Check pets with scaly patches and ask a vet if ringworm is suspected.

## Hives (urticaria)
**Summary:** Hives are itchy, raised welts that appear on the skin and typically fade within hours, sometimes replaced by new ones elsewhere. Most episodes are short-lived, but hives that keep coming for more than about six weeks are classed as chronic.
**Image:** images/hives.jpg
**What it is:** A hive forms when cells in the skin release histamine and other chemicals, making small blood vessels leak fluid into the surrounding tissue — producing a raised, pale or pink welt with intense itch. Individual welts usually disappear within 24 hours without leaving a mark. Swelling can also occur deeper, in the lips, eyelids, or hands (angioedema), which is usually less itchy and more achy or tight.
**Why it happens:** Acute hives are often set off by infections (especially viral illnesses in children), foods, medicines such as antibiotics or painkillers, or insect stings. Chronic hives are different: in most cases no allergy or external cause is ever found, because the process is driven from within the immune system — this is frustrating but common, and it does not mean an allergy has been missed. Cold, pressure, heat, sweating, and stress can trigger hives in some people.
**Signs to watch for:** Itchy welts that move around and fade within a day. The red flags matter more than the rash: swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, hoarseness, difficulty breathing or swallowing, dizziness, or feeling faint — these can be signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis). Call your local emergency number immediately. Also seek prompt care for hives with fever, joint pain, or welts that hurt, bruise, or last in one spot beyond a day or two, as these suggest other causes.
**How doctors confirm it:** Mainly by the history and a skin exam — the short-lived, shifting welts are characteristic. Allergy testing is only useful when the history points to a specific trigger; routine blanket allergy panels are usually unhelpful in chronic hives. Blood tests may be done in chronic cases to check for underlying inflammation or thyroid problems.
**How it's treated:** The mainstay is antihistamine tablets, which block the histamine driving the welts; doctors commonly consider regular daily dosing for chronic hives rather than taking them only during flares. A short course of oral corticosteroids is sometimes used for severe acute episodes. For chronic hives that don't respond, specialists may consider an injectable medicine that targets the allergic antibody pathway, or other immune-calming medicines. Anyone at risk of anaphylaxis may be prescribed an adrenaline auto-injector and shown how to use it.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only partly. If a specific trigger such as a food, medicine, or sting has been identified, avoiding it helps. Chronic hives usually can't be prevented, but they often settle on their own over time, and treatment can control symptoms in the meantime.

## Skin cancer (including melanoma)
**Summary:** Skin cancer develops when skin cells damaged over time — usually by ultraviolet light — begin to grow out of control. Melanoma is the type most likely to spread to other parts of the body, which is why checking your skin and acting early matters so much.
**Image:** images/skin-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** The common types are basal cell carcinoma (slow-growing, rarely spreads), squamous cell carcinoma (can spread if neglected), and melanoma, which arises from the pigment-producing cells and is the most dangerous. Melanoma often appears as a new or changing mole, but it can also look like a dark streak under a nail or a spot on the sole of the foot. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun and from tanning beds is the main cause, damaging DNA in skin cells over years. Risk is higher with fair skin that burns easily, many moles, a history of sunburns (especially in childhood), long-term sun exposure, a weakened immune system, and a family history of skin cancer. Importantly, skin cancer also occurs in darker skin — often in less sun-exposed places such as palms, soles, and under the nails — and it is frequently found later, so no one should assume they are immune.
**Signs to watch for:** Use the ABCDE check for moles: **A**symmetry (one half unlike the other), uneven **B**order, **C**olour changes or multiple colours, **D**iameter growing (roughly larger than a pencil eraser), and **E**volving — any change in size, shape, colour, or feel, or a new symptom such as itching or bleeding. Also watch for a sore that doesn't heal within a few weeks, a pearly or crusted lump, or any spot that simply looks different from all your others — the "ugly duckling."
**How doctors confirm it:** With a skin exam, often using a dermatoscope (a handheld magnifier that reveals patterns beneath the surface). The only sure way to confirm skin cancer is a biopsy — removing all or part of the suspicious spot to examine under a microscope. If melanoma is confirmed, further tests check whether it has spread.
**How it's treated:** Most skin cancers are treated by surgically removing the spot, usually a small procedure under local anaesthetic — this cures most early cases. Depending on the type and stage, doctors may also consider specialised surgical techniques that check margins layer by layer, freezing or scraping for selected superficial lesions, prescription creams for very early surface changes, and — for advanced melanoma — immunotherapy or targeted medicines. Follow-up skin checks are usually recommended afterwards.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Protect skin from the sun with shade, covering clothing, a hat, and sunscreen on exposed skin, reapplied through the day; avoid tanning beds entirely; and never let skin burn. Check your own skin every few months, including the back, scalp, and soles, and get any changing or non-healing spot looked at promptly — early skin cancer is usually curable.
