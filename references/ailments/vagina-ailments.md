**One emergency to know about before the list:** toxic shock syndrome is a rare but life-threatening reaction linked to tampon use. High fever, a widespread rash, and dizziness or fainting while using a tampon are warning signs — remove the tampon at once and call your local emergency number.

## Yeast infection (thrush)
**Summary:** Thrush is an overgrowth of Candida, a yeast that normally lives in the vagina in small amounts without causing any trouble. It causes itching, soreness, and a thick white discharge — and it is not a sexually transmitted infection.
**Image:** images/yeast-infection.jpg
**What it is:** The healthy vagina is home to a community of bacteria, mainly lactobacilli, which keep it mildly acidic and keep other organisms — including the Candida yeast — in check. Thrush happens when Candida multiplies beyond its usual small numbers and irritates the vaginal lining and the skin around the opening. It is very common, and most women will have it at least once. Because Candida is already present in the body, thrush is not caught from a partner and is not an STI.
**Why it happens:** Anything that disturbs the vaginal balance or the immune system can let Candida take over: a course of antibiotics (which reduce the protective bacteria), pregnancy, diabetes — especially if blood sugar is poorly controlled — a weakened immune system, and tight or synthetic clothing that keeps the area warm and damp. Sometimes it happens for no clear reason at all.
**Signs to watch for:** Intense itching around the vagina and vulva, soreness and stinging, a thick white discharge often described as like cottage cheese, usually with no strong smell, and discomfort during sex or when passing urine. The skin may look red and swollen, sometimes with small splits.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often from the symptoms and a pelvic exam alone. If there is doubt, a vaginal swab is checked under a microscope or sent for culture, and a pH test helps — in thrush the pH is usually normal, unlike in bacterial vaginosis.
**How it's treated:** Antifungal medicines — creams or pessaries used in the vagina, or a single antifungal tablet by mouth. Many cases can be treated with pharmacy products, but see a doctor if you are pregnant, under 16, if it keeps coming back, or if treatment doesn't clear it. Thrush that recurs often needs checking rather than endless repeat treatment, including a blood sugar test to look for undiagnosed diabetes.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Wear breathable cotton underwear, avoid scented products and douching, change out of damp swimwear or gym clothes promptly, and keep blood sugar well controlled if you have diabetes. Treating a partner is not normally needed, since thrush is not sexually transmitted.

## Bacterial vaginosis
**Summary:** Bacterial vaginosis (BV) happens when the normal balance of vaginal bacteria is disturbed — the protective lactobacilli fall and other bacteria multiply. It typically causes a thin grey discharge with a fishy smell, and is the most common cause of abnormal discharge in women of childbearing age.
**Image:** images/bacterial-vaginosis.jpg
**What it is:** In a healthy vagina, lactobacilli dominate and produce lactic acid, keeping the environment mildly acidic. In BV this balance tips: lactobacilli decrease and a mix of other bacteria normally present in small numbers grows instead. The result is inflammation-free but noticeable change in discharge and smell. BV is not classified as a sexually transmitted infection, although it is linked to sexual activity — it occurs more often with a new partner or without condoms, and rarely in women who have never been sexually active.
**Why it happens:** The exact trigger isn't fully understood, but anything that disturbs the vaginal environment raises the risk. Douching is a major one — washing inside the vagina flushes out the protective bacteria and makes BV more likely, not less; the vagina cleans itself and needs no internal washing. Scented soaps, bubble baths, and "feminine hygiene" sprays can have the same effect. Smoking is also associated with BV.
**Signs to watch for:** A thin, watery, grey-white discharge and a fishy odour, often stronger after sex or during a period. Notably, BV usually does not cause itching or soreness — if those are present, thrush or another infection is more likely. Many women with BV have no symptoms at all.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic exam and a vaginal swab are the usual tests. Doctors may check the vaginal pH (raised in BV, unlike thrush) and look at the discharge under a microscope. Because symptoms overlap with STIs, STI tests are often done at the same time.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics — usually metronidazole or clindamycin, given as tablets, vaginal gel, or cream. Symptoms usually settle quickly, but BV has a habit of coming back; recurrent BV may need a longer course. During pregnancy, BV is treated because it is linked to premature birth, so tell your doctor if you are pregnant.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Never douche, avoid scented products inside or around the vagina, use condoms, and don't smoke. If BV keeps returning, a doctor can discuss preventive options rather than repeated one-off treatments.

## Sexually transmitted infections of the vagina
**Summary:** Several infections spread through sexual contact affect the vagina and cervix, including trichomoniasis, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea. The most important fact about them is that they very often cause no symptoms at all — testing is the only way to know.
**Image:** images/vaginal-stis.jpg
**What it is:** *Trichomoniasis* is caused by a tiny parasite and is one of the most common STIs worldwide. *Chlamydia* and *gonorrhoea* are bacterial infections; they mostly infect the cervix but can involve the vagina and the tube that carries urine. Unlike thrush and BV, these are genuinely sexually transmitted — they pass between partners during vaginal, oral, or anal sex. All are curable with the right treatment; none clears reliably on its own.
**Why it happens:** Unprotected sex with a partner who has the infection. Because these infections so often cause no symptoms, a partner can pass one on without either person knowing anything is wrong. Having one STI also doesn't protect against others — they can occur together, so a full check covers several infections at once.
**Signs to watch for:** Frequently none. When symptoms do occur: an unusual discharge (trichomoniasis often causes a frothy, yellow-green discharge with a strong smell and soreness), burning when passing urine, pain during sex, bleeding between periods or after sex, and lower abdominal pain. Lower belly pain with fever can mean the infection has spread upward — that needs prompt medical care, and heavy vaginal bleeding or severe pelvic pain with fever is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Simple swab or urine tests identify all three infections accurately, and testing is quick and confidential at sexual health clinics and most doctors' offices. A vaginal pH test and looking at discharge under a microscope can point to trichomoniasis. Partners need testing too, and a full STI screen (including HIV and syphilis) is usually offered at the same visit.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics for chlamydia and gonorrhoea; metronidazole or a similar antibiotic for trichomoniasis. Current and recent partners must be treated at the same time, and sex should wait until treatment is finished, or the infection simply passes back and forth. A follow-up test is sometimes recommended to confirm the infection has gone — this matters especially in pregnancy.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, largely. Condoms used consistently greatly reduce the risk. Regular STI testing is the other half of prevention, because untreated infections do quiet damage: chlamydia and gonorrhoea can spread to the fallopian tubes and cause pelvic inflammatory disease, which can scar the tubes and affect fertility (see the pelvic inflammatory disease entry in the fallopian tubes file). Getting tested after a new partner, and before stopping condom use with one, is the practical rule.

## Vaginal dryness and thinning (menopause)
**Summary:** Around menopause, falling oestrogen levels make the vaginal tissues thinner, drier, and less elastic. Doctors call this the genitourinary syndrome of menopause. It causes soreness, discomfort during sex, and often urinary symptoms — and it is very treatable, not something that just has to be endured.
**Image:** images/vaginal-dryness.png
**What it is:** Oestrogen keeps the vaginal lining thick, soft, moist, and stretchy, and supports its protective bacteria. As oestrogen falls at menopause, the lining becomes thinner and drier, natural lubrication drops, and the vagina's acidity decreases, so irritation and urinary infections become more common. Unlike hot flushes, which usually fade, these changes tend to persist and slowly worsen without treatment — but they respond well to it.
**Why it happens:** Menopause is the main cause, whether natural or brought on by surgery or medical treatment. Similar symptoms can occur while breastfeeding, with some hormonal contraceptives, and with medicines that lower oestrogen, such as certain breast cancer treatments. Irritants — scented products, harsh soaps — can add to the soreness.
**Signs to watch for:** Dryness, itching, burning, or soreness around the vagina and vulva; pain or slight bleeding during sex; needing to pass urine more often or urgently; and repeated urinary tract infections. Any vaginal bleeding after menopause, though, should always be checked by a doctor promptly, whatever the likely cause.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the symptoms and a gentle pelvic exam — the tissues look paler, thinner, and less elastic. A vaginal pH test often shows reduced acidity. If there is bleeding or anything unusual, further tests such as an ultrasound or a sample of the womb lining may be needed to rule out other causes.
**How it's treated:** Vaginal moisturisers (used regularly) and lubricants (used during sex) help many women. The most effective treatment is low-dose vaginal oestrogen — a cream, tablet, pessary, or ring placed in the vagina. It restores the tissues while very little is absorbed into the rest of the body, so it is suitable for most women, including many who can't take whole-body hormone therapy; your doctor will advise if you have a history of breast cancer. Pelvic floor physiotherapy can help where pain has led to muscle tension.
**Can it be prevented?:** The underlying change can't be prevented, but its effects can be kept at bay. Regular sexual activity (with a partner or alone) helps maintain blood flow and elasticity, moisturisers work best started early and used consistently, and avoiding scented products reduces irritation. The main message: don't suffer in silence — effective help is available at any age.

## Pelvic organ prolapse
**Summary:** A pelvic organ prolapse is when weakened pelvic floor muscles and tissues let the bladder, bowel, or womb slip down and bulge into the vagina. It causes a feeling of heaviness or dragging and sometimes a visible or palpable bulge, and there are good options at every stage.
**Image:** images/pelvic-organ-prolapse.jpg
**What it is:** The pelvic organs are held in place by a sling of muscles and ligaments called the pelvic floor. When this support weakens, one or more organs can descend. A bladder prolapse (cystocele) bulges through the front vaginal wall; a bowel prolapse (rectocele) through the back wall; and the womb itself can descend — uterine prolapse, covered in its own entry in the uterus file. Prolapse is graded by how far the organ has dropped, and it is very common, especially after childbirth and menopause.
**Why it happens:** Pregnancy and vaginal childbirth are the biggest causes, through stretching and sometimes injury to the pelvic floor. Menopause weakens the tissues further as oestrogen falls. Anything that repeatedly raises pressure in the abdomen adds strain over the years: heavy lifting, chronic coughing, constipation and straining, and excess weight.
**Signs to watch for:** A dragging or heavy feeling in the pelvis, often worse by the end of the day or after standing, and better lying down. A bulge or lump that can be felt or seen at the vaginal opening. Bladder prolapses can make emptying the bladder fully difficult or cause leakage; bowel prolapses can make passing stool harder, sometimes needing pressure on the back vaginal wall to manage. Sex may feel different or uncomfortable.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic exam is the main test — the doctor looks and feels for a bulge while you bear down, and checks which organ is involved and how far it has descended. Bladder or bowel function tests, and occasionally scans, are added if symptoms suggest them. Many mild prolapses found on examination cause no symptoms and need no treatment.
**How it's treated:** It depends on severity and how much it bothers you. Pelvic floor muscle training (ideally with a pelvic floor physiotherapist) helps mild prolapse and prevents worsening. A pessary — a removable support ring or device fitted in the vagina — holds the organs up without surgery and suits many women. Surgery to repair the support is an option for more severe or troublesome prolapse; procedures vary by which organ is involved, and a hysterectomy is only part of it when the womb itself is the problem.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Regular pelvic floor exercises — during pregnancy, after childbirth, and long-term — genuinely protect the support tissues. Keeping a healthy weight, treating chronic cough and constipation, and using good lifting technique all reduce the strain. If you notice early dragging or bulging, getting assessed early works better than waiting until it advances.
