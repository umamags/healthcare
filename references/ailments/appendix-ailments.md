## Appendicitis
**Summary:** Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, usually caused by a blockage that lets bacteria multiply inside it. It's a genuine emergency: treated promptly it's a routine operation, but delay can let the appendix burst and spread infection through the belly.
**Image:** images/appendicitis.png
**What it is:** The appendix is a small, finger-shaped pouch off the large intestine, low on the right side of the belly. In appendicitis it becomes swollen and infected, and as pressure builds inside it, its blood supply gets squeezed. Left untreated, the wall can die and burst (perforate), spilling infected contents into the belly cavity — a dangerous complication called peritonitis. A burst appendix can also form an abscess, a walled-off pocket of infection.
**Why it happens:** Usually the opening of the appendix gets blocked — by a small hardened lump of stool, by swollen lymph tissue (often after a viral illness), or rarely by a parasite or a tumour. Bacteria trapped inside multiply and drive the inflammation. Appendicitis is most common in teenagers and young adults, but it can happen at any age, and it isn't caused by anything the person did or ate.
**Signs to watch for:** The classic pattern is a vague ache around the belly button that, over several hours, settles into the lower right side and steadily worsens, becoming sharper with movement, coughing, or bumps in the road. Loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting, and a low fever are typical. But presentations vary: young children may just seem unwell and off their food, in pregnancy the pain can sit higher because the appendix is pushed up, and older adults may have surprisingly mild signs. A rigid, board-like belly, severe pain, or high fever can mean the appendix has burst — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam (tenderness low on the right, including pain when pressure is released), blood tests showing infection markers, and a urine test to rule out lookalikes such as a urinary infection or kidney stone. Ultrasound is often used first in children and pregnant women; a CT scan is the most accurate test in adults. When the picture is unclear, doctors sometimes observe in hospital and re-examine or re-scan rather than rush — and sometimes operate on strong suspicion even if scans are inconclusive.
**How it's treated:** The standard treatment is prompt surgery to remove the appendix (appendicectomy), usually by keyhole surgery with small incisions and a quick recovery; open surgery is used if the appendix has burst or there are complications. Antibiotics are given around the operation. For uncomplicated appendicitis, doctors sometimes consider an antibiotics-first approach that can avoid surgery — though the appendicitis can return later. A burst appendix needs surgery plus antibiotics, and sometimes a drain for an abscess. Most people are back to normal life within a couple of weeks.
**Can it be prevented?:** Honestly, no — there is no proven way to prevent appendicitis. A fibre-rich diet supports general gut health but is no guarantee. The real protection is behavioural: don't ignore belly pain that keeps getting worse. Getting checked early is what turns a crisis into a routine fix.

## Tumours of the appendix
**Summary:** Tumours of the appendix are very rare, and most people who have one never know until it's found by accident. The commonest type is a slow-growing neuroendocrine tumour, and often the appendicectomy that discovered it is the only treatment needed.
**Image:** images/appendix-tumours.png
**What it is:** Very occasionally, a growth develops in the appendix. Most are neuroendocrine tumours (sometimes called carcinoid tumours), which typically grow slowly. Others, such as mucinous growths or adenocarcinoma, are rarer still. For the broader picture of how tumours are assessed and treated, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Like most tumours, they arise from random changes in cells. There are no clear risk factors and nothing about lifestyle is known to cause them.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none at all. Most are discovered when an appendix removed for suspected appendicitis is examined under the microscope in the laboratory. Occasionally a tumour causes appendicitis-like symptoms by blocking the appendix's opening.
**How doctors confirm it:** By laboratory examination of the removed appendix — this is how almost all are diagnosed. If a tumour is found, doctors may arrange scans or blood tests to check its type and whether it has spread.
**How it's treated:** It depends on the type and size. Small, slow-growing tumours confined to the appendix usually need no further treatment after the appendicectomy that found them. Larger or higher-risk tumours may mean further surgery — sometimes removing a section of bowel on that side — and follow-up with a specialist team.
**Can it be prevented?:** No known way, and nothing the person did causes them. Because they're usually found by chance and dealt with at the same time, the outlook for the common slow-growing type is generally very good.
