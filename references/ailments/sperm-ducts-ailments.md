## Epididymitis
**Summary:** Epididymitis is a painful inflammation of the epididymis — the coiled tube at the back of the testicle where sperm mature. It's usually caused by infection, and it typically builds up over a few days rather than striking suddenly.
**Image:** images/epididymitis.jpg
**What it is:** The epididymis is a tightly coiled tube sitting on the back of each testicle. When it becomes inflamed, the back of the scrotum grows tender, swollen, and sore. The inflammation sometimes spreads to the testicle itself. Left untreated, it can lead to a pocket of pus (an abscess), long-lasting pain, or scarring that damages fertility on that side — so it deserves prompt treatment, not a wait-and-see approach.
**Why it happens:** Most cases are bacterial infections that have travelled backwards along the tubes from the urinary tract or prostate. In younger, sexually active men the usual culprits are sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea. In older men, urinary infections and an enlarged prostate are more common causes. It can also follow a urinary procedure or catheter, and occasionally no infection is found.
**Signs to watch for:** Aching pain and swelling at the back of one testicle that builds over days, sometimes with redness and warmth of the scrotum, pain when passing urine, discharge from the penis, or fever. One warning matters above all: pain that starts suddenly and is severe is *not* assumed to be epididymitis — a twisted testicle (torsion) must be ruled out first, because it needs emergency surgery within hours (see the testes page). If in doubt, call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical examination, a urine test, and STI tests (usually a urine sample or a swab). A scrotal ultrasound checks blood flow and helps rule out torsion and other causes such as a hernia or tumour.
**How it's treated:** Antibiotics chosen for the likely cause, taken for the full course even once things feel better. Rest, a supportive underwear or scrotal support, cold packs, and pain relief help while it settles. If an STI is responsible, sexual partners need testing and treatment too, and sex should wait until treatment is complete and the doctor advises.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Safer sex and regular STI testing reduce the infection risk for younger men, and treating urinary infections promptly — rather than letting them linger — helps at any age.

## Epididymal cysts and spermatocele
**Summary:** These are small fluid-filled sacs that grow on the epididymis. They are very common, almost always harmless, and usually need no treatment at all — most men only notice one by chance.
**Image:** images/epididymal-cyst.jpg
**What it is:** An epididymal cyst is a smooth, round swelling filled with clear fluid. A spermatocele is the close cousin: the fluid is slightly milky because it contains sperm. The two feel the same, are found in the same place, and are managed the same way — the difference only shows if the fluid is ever examined. (See also the scrotum page, which covers these among scrotal lumps generally.)
**Why it happens:** Nobody knows exactly. The likely explanation is a tiny blockage in one of the small tubes of the epididymis, letting fluid collect behind it. They become more common with age and are not caused by anything a man did or didn't do, and they are not an infection or a cancer.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually nothing at all. The typical finding is a small, painless, pea-like lump at the top or back of the testicle that can be felt as separate from the testicle itself. Larger ones can cause a feeling of heaviness or a dull ache. Any new scrotal lump should still be checked once by a doctor, even though most turn out to be these.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often by examination alone — the lump sits on the epididymis, away from the testicle, and a torch shone through it (transillumination) glows because it's fluid, not solid. A scrotal ultrasound settles any doubt, showing a simple fluid-filled sac.
**How it's treated:** Usually nothing. If a cyst is large, uncomfortable, or worrying, a urologist can remove it surgically — but because surgery on the epididymis can affect sperm flow on that side, doctors generally advise leaving harmless cysts alone, especially in men who may still want children.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — there's no known way to prevent them, and none is needed. The sensible habit is simply to get any new lump examined once, for peace of mind.

## Blocked vas deferens
**Summary:** Here the testicles make sperm normally, but the vas deferens — the tube that carries sperm out — is blocked or missing, so sperm can't reach the semen. It's one of the mechanical causes of male infertility.
**Image:** images/blocked-vas-deferens.jpg
**What it is:** Ejaculate can look completely normal, because most of its volume comes from the prostate and seminal vesicles, yet contain few sperm or none at all. Doctors call this obstructive azoospermia when no sperm are present. Because sperm production itself is usually healthy, this is one of the more treatable forms of male infertility.
**Why it happens:** Causes fall into four groups. Scarring from a past infection, such as epididymitis, can narrow or seal the tube. The vas can be damaged accidentally during hernia surgery, particularly surgery in childhood. A vasectomy is a deliberate blockage. And some men are born without part or all of the vas on one or both sides — this often goes together with missing seminal vesicles (see the seminal vesicles page) and can be linked to carrying a cystic fibrosis gene change, even in men with no lung problems.
**Signs to watch for:** There are usually no symptoms at all — nothing hurts and nothing looks different. The problem surfaces when a couple has difficulty conceiving and tests are done. If both ducts and the seminal vesicles are absent, semen volume may be noticeably low.
**How doctors confirm it:** Semen analysis shows no sperm or very few, while hormone blood tests are typically normal — the pattern that points to a blockage rather than a production problem. Examination may reveal that the vas can't be felt. Ultrasound and, when the absent-vas form is suspected, genetic testing help complete the picture.
**How it's treated:** Options depend on the cause and the couple's wishes. A surgeon can sometimes reconnect or repair the tube — vasectomy reversal is the best-known example. Alternatively, sperm can be retrieved directly from the epididymis or testicle with a needle or small procedure and used for IVF treatment, where a single sperm is injected into an egg. A fertility specialist will talk through which route fits best.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treating genital and urinary infections early, and protecting against STIs, reduces scarring. The form present from birth can't be prevented — but as the treatment options show, it rarely means the end of the road for fatherhood.

## Post-vasectomy pain syndrome
**Summary:** A minority of men develop ongoing scrotal pain that lasts for months after a vasectomy. It's uncommon, but it is real — and it should be mentioned honestly to any man considering the procedure, not discovered afterwards.
**Image:** images/post-vasectomy-pain.jpg
**What it is:** Vasectomy is a safe and highly effective form of contraception, and most men recover within a week or two. In post-vasectomy pain syndrome, an ache in one or both testicles or in the epididymis persists or keeps returning beyond about three months. It ranges from an occasional niggle to pain that interferes with work, exercise, and sex.
**Why it happens:** The exact mechanism isn't settled, and more than one may be at work. Sperm continue to be produced after vasectomy and are absorbed by the body; in some men this creates a feeling of congestion or pressure in the epididymis. Inflammation, scar tissue around the cut ends of the vas, and irritation or trapping of the small nerves in the cord are the other leading explanations.
**Signs to watch for:** A dull ache or tenderness in the testicle or epididymis that lingers for months, often worse after ejaculation, prolonged sitting, or vigorous exercise. Pain that starts suddenly and severely, or comes with swelling, redness, or fever, is a different problem — that needs prompt medical assessment in its own right, and severe sudden pain is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Mainly by listening to the history and examining the scrotum and cord. A scrotal ultrasound is often done to rule out other causes such as infection, a cyst, or fluid around the testicle. There is no single test that proves the syndrome — it's a diagnosis made once other explanations are excluded.
**How it's treated:** Most cases settle with time and simple measures: supportive underwear, warm baths, avoiding activities that provoke it, and ordinary pain relief. If pain persists, a urologist may try medicines used for nerve pain or targeted injections. For the few men still struggling, surgical options exist — including reversing the vasectomy or a procedure that divides the small nerves carrying the pain signals — with honest discussion about success rates before choosing.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably. It can't be predicted who will be affected, which is exactly why it belongs in the conversation before a vasectomy rather than after. Choosing an experienced surgeon and following aftercare advice are sensible steps, but the fair summary is: the risk is small, it is not zero, and men deserve to know that upfront.
