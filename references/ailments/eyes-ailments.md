## Refractive errors (short-sightedness, long-sightedness, astigmatism)
**Summary:** Refractive errors mean the eye doesn't focus light sharply onto the retina, so vision is blurred at certain distances. They are among the most common vision problems in the world, and among the easiest to correct.
**Image:** images/refractive-errors.jpg
**What it is:** In a healthy eye, the cornea and lens bend incoming light so it lands exactly on the retina. In a refractive error, the shape of the eyeball or cornea puts the focus point slightly off, producing blur.
**Why it happens:** Mainly the natural shape and growth of the eye, often inherited. Short-sightedness (myopia) usually develops in childhood and often stabilises in early adulthood; long-sightedness may become more noticeable with age as the lens stiffens. Genes play the largest part, though heavy close work and little outdoor time in childhood are linked to myopia.
**Signs to watch for:** Blurry vision, squinting, headaches after reading or screen use, and eyestrain.
**How doctors confirm it:** A visual acuity chart test, then a refraction test ("which is clearer, one or two?") carried out by an optometrist or ophthalmologist to measure exactly what correction is needed.
**How it's treated:** Glasses or contact lenses correct most cases. Laser refractive surgery can reshape the cornea for suitable adults. Orthokeratology (special overnight lenses) is sometimes used to slow myopia progression in children.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully — genetics dominates. More outdoor time in childhood is linked to lower myopia risk, and regular eye exams catch changes early so children don't fall behind at school.

*Short-sightedness (myopia):* The eyeball is slightly too long, so distant objects focus in front of the retina and look blurred, while near vision stays clear.

*Long-sightedness (hyperopia):* The eyeball is slightly too short, so light focuses behind the retina. Young people may compensate by effort, but reading can cause strain and headaches; stronger cases blur all distances.

*Astigmatism:* The cornea or lens is curved unevenly — more like a rugby ball than a football — so vision is blurred or distorted at all distances. It often occurs together with myopia or hyperopia.

## Cataract
**Summary:** A cataract is a clouding of the eye's lens, which gradually blurs and dims vision like looking through frosted glass. It is extremely common with ageing and is the world's leading cause of treatable blindness.
**Image:** images/cataract.png
**What it is:** The lens sits behind the pupil and focuses light on the retina. Over time its proteins change and clump, making it cloudy. Light scatters instead of focusing, so images lose sharpness and contrast.
**Why it happens:** Ageing is the main cause. Risk is raised by diabetes, smoking, long-term exposure to ultraviolet sunlight, eye injury, previous eye surgery, and long-term use of steroid medicines. Some babies are born with cataracts, but this is uncommon.
**Signs to watch for:** Slowly worsening blurry or misty vision, glare from headlights and bright lights, colours looking faded or yellowish, frequent changes in glasses prescription, and difficulty reading even with glasses. There is no pain.
**How doctors confirm it:** A visual acuity chart test and a slit-lamp exam let the ophthalmologist see the clouded lens directly. A dilated retinal exam checks the back of the eye is otherwise healthy before surgery is planned.
**How it's treated:** In the early stages, stronger glasses and brighter lighting can help. Surgery is the only effective treatment: the cloudy lens is removed and replaced with a clear artificial lens. It is one of the most common operations in the world and usually restores vision well, though the artificial lens cannot fully mimic a young natural lens — reading glasses are often still needed afterwards.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully, since ageing is the main driver. Wearing sunglasses with UV protection, not smoking, keeping diabetes well controlled, and limiting alcohol may slow development.

## Glaucoma
**Summary:** Glaucoma is a group of conditions that damage the optic nerve — the cable carrying vision signals to the brain. It usually develops slowly and silently, stealing side vision first, and the vision it takes cannot be restored.
**Image:** images/glaucoma.png
**What it is:** The eye constantly makes a clear fluid that drains away through a fine mesh. If drainage is poor, pressure inside the eye can rise and gradually injure the optic nerve. Glaucoma can also occur with normal pressure. Because central vision is affected last, people often feel nothing is wrong until damage is advanced.
**Why it happens:** Risk rises with age, higher eye pressure, a family history of glaucoma, diabetes, high blood pressure, severe short-sightedness, and long-term steroid use. People of African, Asian, and Hispanic ancestry have higher rates of some types.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none early — this is the crucial point. Later signs include gradual loss of side (peripheral) vision, difficulty in dim light, and frequent bumps into objects. A rare acute form causes sudden eye pain, redness, blurred vision, halos around lights, headache, and nausea — that is an emergency: seek emergency care or call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** An eye pressure check (tonometry), examination of the optic nerve (often with pupil dilation), an OCT scan of the nerve fibre layer, and a visual field test that maps peripheral vision. Regular eye exams are how most cases are caught.
**How it's treated:** Treatment aims to lower eye pressure to protect the nerve — it cannot undo damage already done. Approaches doctors commonly consider include pressure-lowering eye drops, laser treatment to improve drainage, and surgery in more advanced cases.
**Can it be prevented?:** The disease itself cannot be fully prevented, but vision loss from it largely can be — through regular eye exams (especially with a family history), early detection, and steady use of prescribed treatment.

## Age-related macular degeneration
**Summary:** Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) damages the macula — the small central part of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision used for reading and recognising faces. Side vision is usually preserved, so AMD rarely causes total blindness.
**Image:** images/macular-degeneration.jpg
**What it is:** The macula's light-sensitive cells gradually break down with age. In the common "dry" form this happens slowly as waste deposits (drusen) build up under the retina. In the less common "wet" form, fragile new blood vessels grow under the macula and leak, causing faster, more severe damage.
**Why it happens:** Age is the strongest risk factor. Smoking roughly doubles risk, and genetics, high blood pressure, obesity, and a diet low in leafy greens and fish also contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** Blurred or fuzzy central vision, straight lines appearing wavy or bent (a classic early sign), a dark or empty patch in the centre of vision, colours seeming less vivid, and needing brighter light to read. Sudden distortion or a new central blind spot needs prompt assessment, as wet AMD benefits from early treatment.
**How doctors confirm it:** A dilated retinal exam to inspect the macula, an OCT scan showing detailed cross-sections of the retina, and sometimes a dye-based scan of the retinal blood vessels. An Amsler grid — a printed grid of straight lines — is often used at home to monitor for new distortion.
**How it's treated:** There is no cure. For wet AMD, injections into the eye that block abnormal blood-vessel growth (anti-VEGF medicines) can stabilise and sometimes improve vision, usually given as a series. For dry AMD, specific vitamin and mineral supplements may slow progression in some stages; low-vision aids, magnifiers, and good lighting help people keep reading.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully. Not smoking is the most important step. A diet rich in leafy green vegetables and fish, controlling blood pressure, and maintaining a healthy weight may lower risk.

## Diabetic retinopathy
**Summary:** Diabetic retinopathy is damage to the retina's tiny blood vessels caused by diabetes. It develops silently and is a leading cause of vision loss in working-age adults — but much of that loss is preventable with screening and blood-sugar control.
**Image:** images/diabetic-retinopathy.png
**What it is:** Persistently high blood sugar weakens the small vessels in the retina. They may leak fluid or blood, swell (macular oedema), or close off; in advanced stages, fragile new vessels grow and can bleed into the eye or pull the retina out of place (detachment).
**Why it happens:** It is a direct complication of diabetes — both type 1 and type 2. The longer someone has diabetes and the higher their blood sugar over time, the greater the risk. High blood pressure and high cholesterol add to the damage. Pregnancy can speed it up in women with diabetes.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none early — which is why screening matters. Later signs include blurred or fluctuating vision, floaters, dark spots or strings in vision, difficulty seeing at night, and in advanced disease, sudden vision loss from bleeding inside the eye.
**How doctors confirm it:** A dilated retinal exam is the core test; an OCT scan detects swelling in the macula, and retinal photographs are often used in screening programmes. People with diabetes are typically offered regular retinal screening even when their vision feels perfectly normal.
**How it's treated:** Early stages often need no eye treatment — just tighter diabetes control and monitoring. More advanced disease may be treated with injections into the eye, laser treatment to seal leaking vessels or shrink abnormal ones, and surgery if there is major bleeding or retinal detachment.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Keeping blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol well controlled greatly reduces risk, and attending regular retinal screening catches changes while they are still treatable. Not smoking also helps.

## Conjunctivitis ("pink eye")
**Summary:** Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva — the thin, clear layer covering the white of the eye — making the eye look pink or red. It is very common, usually mild, and most cases settle within one to two weeks.
**Image:** images/conjunctivitis.jpg
**What it is:** The conjunctiva becomes irritated or infected and its blood vessels swell, giving the pink appearance. Causes fall into three groups: viral (the most common, often alongside a cold), bacterial, and allergic (triggered by pollen, dust, or pet dander). Irritants like smoke or chlorine can cause a similar picture.
**Why it happens:** Viruses and bacteria spread easily through hand-to-eye contact, shared towels, and close contact — hence outbreaks in schools and families. Allergic conjunctivitis tends to recur in certain seasons and affects both eyes with itching. Contact lens wearers who over-wear lenses are at higher risk of infection.
**Signs to watch for:** Pink or red eye, grittiness or a sandy feeling, watering, discharge (watery in viral and allergic cases; sticky and yellow-green in bacterial cases, sometimes gluing the eyelids shut overnight), and itching — itching points strongly to allergy. Vision is usually unaffected; pain, light sensitivity, or blurred vision are not typical and should prompt a prompt visit to an ophthalmologist.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by the symptoms and a simple eye exam or slit-lamp check. Most cases need no laboratory tests; a swab is only taken if the cause is unclear or symptoms are severe or persistent.
**How it's treated:** Many cases clear on their own. Lukewarm compresses and lubricating eye drops ease discomfort; viral and allergic cases don't respond to antibiotics. Bacterial cases may be treated with antibiotic eye drops. Allergic cases may be treated with antihistamine or other anti-allergy drops. Contact lenses should be left out until the eye is fully better.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Wash hands often, don't share towels or eye cosmetics, avoid touching your eyes, and keep contact lens hygiene strict. Allergy sufferers can reduce exposure to their triggers during high-pollen periods.

## Dry eye
**Summary:** Dry eye happens when the eyes don't make enough tears, or the tears evaporate too quickly, leaving the surface dry and irritated. It is very common — especially with age, screens, and air conditioning — and ranges from a nuisance to a cause of blurred vision.
**Image:** images/dry-eye.svg
**What it is:** Tears are more than water: they have layers of water, oil, and mucus that keep the eye smooth, clear, and protected. If the oil glands in the eyelids (meibomian glands) clog, tears evaporate too fast; if the tear glands produce too little, the surface dries. Either way, the eye's surface becomes inflamed and uncomfortable.
**Why it happens:** Ageing and hormonal changes (more common in women, especially after menopause), long screen use — we blink far less when concentrating — dry or windy environments, contact lenses, some medicines (certain antihistamines, antidepressants, and blood-pressure drugs), and autoimmune conditions such as Sjögren's syndrome.
**Signs to watch for:** Stinging, burning, or a gritty feeling, redness, watery eyes (the eye over-compensates with poor-quality tears), stringy mucus, sensitivity to light, and vision that blurs then clears with blinking. Symptoms often worsen as the day goes on.
**How doctors confirm it:** By the symptoms plus a slit-lamp exam. Doctors may measure tear production, check how quickly tears evaporate, and examine the eyelid glands. A simple questionnaire about symptoms is often part of the assessment.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include lubricating drops or gels ("artificial tears"), warm compresses and eyelid cleaning for clogged oil glands, prescription anti-inflammatory eye drops for persistent cases, tiny plugs that slow tear drainage, and treating any underlying cause such as blepharitis or a medicine side effect.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Take screen breaks (the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds), blink deliberately during screen work, avoid direct air conditioning or fans on the face, use a humidifier in dry rooms, and wear wraparound sunglasses outdoors.
