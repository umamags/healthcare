Ailments of the spinal cord and spine — expanded from the spinal cord card.

## Slipped (herniated) disc
**Summary:** A spinal disc's soft centre bulges out through its tougher outer layer and may press on a nearby nerve. It most often causes back or neck pain with pain shooting into a leg or arm, and most cases improve on their own over weeks without surgery.
**Image:** images/herniated-disc.png
**What it is:** Discs are the cushions between the vertebrae (spine bones), each with a firm outer ring and a jelly-like centre. Despite the name, the disc doesn't really "slip" — part of its centre pushes out through a tear or weak spot in the ring. If the bulge presses on a spinal nerve, it can irritate or compress it, causing symptoms along that nerve's path. Herniated discs are most common in the lower back, then the neck.
**Why it happens:** Discs gradually lose water and flexibility with age, so everyday strains — lifting, twisting, a sudden awkward movement — can trigger a herniation. Risk is higher with heavy physical work, excess body weight, smoking (which reduces the discs' blood supply), and long hours of sitting.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain in the back or neck, often with sharp, shooting pain into the buttock and leg (or shoulder and arm), plus tingling, numbness, or weakness in the limb the nerve supplies. **Emergency — call your local emergency number now:** new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the genitals or inner thighs (the "saddle" area), or rapidly worsening leg weakness. Together these can signal cauda equina syndrome — the bundle of nerves at the cord's lower end being squeezed — which is a surgical emergency where hours matter.
**How doctors confirm it:** A neurological exam — checking strength, reflexes, and sensation — often points to which nerve is affected. MRI is the key scan for discs and nerves; CT or X-ray show bones better. Nerve conduction studies are sometimes added. Importantly, scans often show disc bulges in people with no pain at all, so a scan finding alone doesn't prove it's the cause — it has to match your symptoms and exam.
**How it's treated:** Most herniated discs settle within weeks with staying active, physiotherapy, and short courses of pain-relief or anti-inflammatory medicines. Doctors commonly consider targeted injections for persistent nerve pain, and surgery (removing the part of disc pressing on the nerve) when weakness is significant or worsening, when pain doesn't improve with conservative care, or urgently for cauda equina syndrome.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Staying active with a strong core, lifting with your legs, keeping a healthy weight, and not smoking all protect discs, though age-related wear can't be avoided entirely.

## Spinal stenosis
**Summary:** The spinal canal — the tunnel the cord and nerves run through — narrows with age, squeezing the nerves inside. It typically causes leg pain, heaviness, or numbness when walking that eases when you sit down or lean forward.
**Image:** images/spinal-stenosis.png
**What it is:** Stenosis means narrowing. In the spine, wear-and-tear changes — thickened ligaments, worn joints, disc bulges, and bony overgrowth — gradually reduce the space for the spinal cord or the nerves branching from it. It most often affects the lower back in older adults; in the neck it can compress the cord itself (sometimes called myelopathy), which is more serious.
**Why it happens:** It's mainly an age-related degenerative process developing slowly over years. Some people are born with a narrower canal and develop symptoms earlier. Previous disc problems, spine injuries, and arthritis of the spinal joints contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** The classic pattern is pain, heaviness, cramping, or numbness in the buttocks and legs that builds up while walking or standing and eases within minutes of sitting down or leaning forward — for example, leaning on a shopping cart. Symptoms are often in both legs. Neck stenosis can cause clumsy hands, balance problems, and stiffness in the legs. **Emergency — call your local emergency number now:** new loss of bladder or bowel control, saddle-area numbness, or rapidly worsening leg weakness (see the cauda equina warning under Slipped disc).
**How doctors confirm it:** A neurological exam plus MRI, which shows the narrowing and any nerve or cord compression; CT or X-ray add detail about the bones. Because the walking pattern can also be caused by poor circulation in the legs, doctors check for that too.
**How it's treated:** Physiotherapy and exercise programs that emphasise a slightly flexed (bent-forward) posture, weight management, and pain-relief medicines are the usual first steps; some people use a walking aid. Targeted injections are sometimes considered. Surgery to create more room (laminectomy) is an option when walking distance keeps shrinking, leg weakness progresses, or conservative care fails. Cord compression in the neck is usually assessed for surgery sooner because it can slowly worsen.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not fully — it's largely part of ageing. Staying active, maintaining a healthy weight, and not smoking slow the degenerative changes and keep symptoms milder.

## Sciatica
**Summary:** Sciatica is pain that travels along the sciatic nerve — from the lower back through the buttock and down the back of the leg, sometimes to the foot. It is a symptom of nerve irritation rather than a diagnosis in itself, most commonly caused by a herniated disc.
**Image:** images/sciatica.jpg
**What it is:** The sciatic nerves are the body's largest nerves, formed from several nerve roots leaving the lower spine. When one of those roots is pinched or inflamed — usually by a disc bulge, but also by stenosis or other narrowing — pain is felt anywhere along its path. It usually affects one leg only.
**Why it happens:** A herniated disc in the lower back is the commonest cause, especially in younger and middle-aged adults. In older adults, spinal stenosis is a frequent culprit. Less common causes include a slipped vertebra, injury, or pressure on the nerve deeper in the buttock. Risk factors mirror those for disc problems: heavy lifting, excess weight, smoking, and prolonged sitting.
**Signs to watch for:** Sharp, shooting, or burning pain down the back of the leg, often worse when coughing, sneezing, or sitting; tingling ("pins and needles"), numbness, or weakness in parts of the leg or foot. **Emergency — call your local emergency number now:** new bladder or bowel problems, numbness in the saddle area, weakness in both legs, or rapidly worsening weakness (see the cauda equina warning under Slipped disc).
**How doctors confirm it:** The history and a neurological exam — strength, reflexes, sensation, and tests that stretch the nerve to reproduce the pain — usually identify it. MRI is used when symptoms persist, weakness is present, or surgery is being considered; nerve conduction studies occasionally help separate nerve-root problems from nerve problems further down the leg.
**How it's treated:** Most episodes improve over a few weeks. Doctors commonly advise staying as active as you can, physiotherapy, and pain-relief or anti-inflammatory medicines; medicines used for nerve pain are sometimes considered. Targeted injections around the nerve root may help selected cases. Surgery is considered when there is significant or worsening weakness, cauda equina syndrome (an emergency), or pain that remains severe after a good trial of conservative care.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — the same measures that protect discs help: regular exercise and core strength, good lifting technique, a healthy weight, and not smoking. Even so, episodes can recur.

## Spinal cord injury
**Summary:** Damage to the spinal cord from trauma — road accidents, falls, sports injuries — can interrupt the signals between brain and body, causing loss of movement and feeling below the injury. The effects depend on where the cord is injured and how completely, and rehabilitation is a central, long-term part of care.
**Image:** images/spinal-cord-injury.jpg
**What it is:** The cord can be bruised, compressed, stretched, or — less often — severed by a fracture or dislocation of the spine. Injuries in the neck can affect the arms, trunk, and legs (and, at the highest levels, breathing), because everything below the injury is cut off from the brain. Injuries lower down mainly affect the legs and bladder/bowel function. Doctors describe injuries as "complete" (no movement or sensation below the level) or "incomplete" (some function preserved), and outcomes vary widely between individuals.
**Why it happens:** Road traffic accidents and falls are the leading causes worldwide; sports injuries (including diving into shallow water), violence, and work accidents account for others. In older adults, a fall onto a neck already narrowed by stenosis can injure the cord even without a fracture. Alcohol is a contributing factor in a share of injuries.
**Signs to watch for:** After significant trauma, any new weakness or paralysis, loss of sensation, or loss of bladder/bowel control may indicate cord injury. The safest response is not to move the person unnecessarily and to **call your local emergency number now** — trained responders immobilise the neck and spine before transport, because moving an unstable injury can make it worse.
**How doctors confirm it:** Emergency teams assess nerve function with a standardised neurological exam while protecting the spine. CT quickly shows fractures; MRI shows the cord itself, swelling, and compression. Repeated exams over the first days track whether function is returning.
**How it's treated:** Care begins with immobilisation and urgent hospital treatment. Surgery is often performed to stabilise the spine and relieve pressure on the cord. After the acute phase, rehabilitation is the core of recovery — a team effort involving physiotherapists, occupational therapists, rehabilitation doctors, nurses, and psychologists, focusing on strength, mobility aids and wheelchairs, bladder and bowel management, preventing pressure sores and blood clots, and adapting daily life. Some function can return over months, especially with incomplete injuries, though recovery is often partial; honest, individualised guidance from the treating team matters more than general predictions.
**Can it be prevented?:** Many injuries can. Road safety (seatbelts, helmets, no drink-driving or phone use), not diving into shallow or unknown water, protective equipment in contact sports and at work, and preventing falls in older adults (home safety, treating balance and vision problems) all reduce risk.

## Back strain (muscle and ligament strain)
**Summary:** Most back pain comes not from the spinal cord or discs but from strained muscles and ligaments around the spine. It's painful and limiting, but it usually settles within days to a few weeks — and staying gently active helps it heal faster than bed rest.
**Image:** images/back-strain.jpg
**What it is:** The muscles and ligaments supporting the spine can be overstretched or torn by an awkward lift, a sudden twist, or unaccustomed exertion. The result is localised back pain and stiffness, sometimes with muscle spasm. Unlike the other entries on this page, the cord and nerves are not involved — but because it feels similar at first, knowing the warning signs of more serious causes matters.
**Why it happens:** Lifting something heavy or with poor technique, sudden movements, sports injuries, poor posture held for long periods, weak core muscles, and excess weight all contribute. A bout often follows an activity the back wasn't conditioned for — a day of moving house or gardening.
**Signs to watch for:** Aching, stiffness, and tenderness across the lower back, sometimes spreading to the buttocks but not usually below the knee; pain eases with rest positions and gentle movement. The following are **not** simple strain and need prompt medical assessment: pain after significant trauma (a fall or accident), fever with back pain, a history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, pain that is severe at night, leg weakness, or any bladder/bowel change or saddle numbness — **call your local emergency number now** for those last signs (see the cauda equina warning under Slipped disc).
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the history and a physical and neurological exam alone — most straightforward strain needs no scan, since scans often show age-related changes that aren't the cause of pain. Imaging or blood tests are reserved for cases with red flags, nerve symptoms, or pain that doesn't improve after several weeks.
**How it's treated:** The mainstay is to keep moving — short walks and normal activities as tolerated, since prolonged bed rest delays recovery. Heat or cold packs, and short courses of pain-relief or anti-inflammatory medicines, are commonly used. Physiotherapy and graded exercise help recovery and reduce recurrence; doctors address contributing factors such as lifting technique and workstation setup.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely — regular exercise including core strengthening, good lifting habits (bend the knees, keep loads close), a healthy weight, and breaks from prolonged sitting all lower the risk, though an occasional strain can happen to anyone.
