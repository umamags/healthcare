## IBS (irritable bowel syndrome)
**Summary:** IBS is a long-term gut condition causing cramping pain, bloating, and diarrhoea, constipation, or both. It is a problem with how the gut works and communicates with the brain — not damage to the gut itself — and it does not increase the risk of bowel cancer.
**Image:** images/ibs.png
**What it is:** IBS is a disorder of gut–brain interaction: the bowel of someone with IBS is unusually sensitive and its contractions are poorly coordinated, but the bowel lining itself looks normal on camera tests. Symptoms tend to come and go, often flaring with stress, certain foods, or hormonal changes, and easing after passing stool. IBS is not the same as IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) below — despite the similar names, IBS causes no inflammation, no bleeding, and no lasting damage to the bowel.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't fully understood. Contributing factors commonly include a sensitive, reactive gut; altered communication between gut and brain; changes in gut bacteria after an infection (some people's IBS starts after a bout of gastroenteritis); stress and anxiety, which amplify gut signals; and sensitivity to particular foods.
**Signs to watch for:** Recurrent cramping or pain linked to bowel movements, bloating and gas, diarrhoea, constipation, or alternating between the two, and mucus in the stool. Blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, symptoms that regularly wake you at night, or new symptoms after about age 50 are not typical of IBS and need checking — see a doctor soon.
**How doctors confirm it:** Mainly by the pattern of symptoms, after basic tests rule out look-alike conditions. Doctors commonly do blood tests (including coeliac antibody tests and inflammation markers) and stool tests, and use colonoscopy only if there are warning signs or another condition needs excluding. There is no single test that proves IBS.
**How it's treated:** Approaches are individual and usually combine several things: dietary adjustments — a low-FODMAP diet (reducing certain fermentable carbohydrates) often helps and is best done with a dietitian so it doesn't become unnecessarily restrictive; medicines that calm cramping (antispasmodics); medicines that regulate bowel movements in either direction; and gut–brain approaches such as stress management, psychological therapies aimed at the gut, and sometimes low-dose medicines that calm gut nerve sensitivity.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably — but flares can often be reduced. Regular meals, identifying personal food triggers (without cutting out whole food groups unnecessarily), managing stress, and staying active all commonly help. After a stomach infection, there's no proven way to prevent post-infectious IBS.

## IBD — Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis
**Summary:** IBD (inflammatory bowel disease) is long-term inflammation of the gut caused by the immune system attacking it. It has two main forms — Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis — and, unlike IBS, it visibly damages the bowel and needs specialist treatment.
**Image:** images/ibd.png
**What it is:** IBD is not IBS: in IBD the immune system causes real inflammation, ulceration, and damage; in IBS the bowel looks normal. *Crohn's disease* can affect any part of the digestive tract from mouth to anus, often in patches, and can inflame the full thickness of the bowel wall, leading to narrowing, fistulas (abnormal tunnels), and abscesses. *Ulcerative colitis* affects only the large intestine and rectum, in one continuous stretch, and inflames the inner lining only. Both follow a relapsing pattern — flare-ups and quieter periods (remission).
**Why it happens:** The immune system overreacts to gut bacteria and environmental triggers in people who are genetically susceptible. Family history raises the risk. Smoking makes Crohn's disease worse (though, oddly, ulcerative colitis behaves differently — this is never a reason to smoke). Infections or antibiotics may trigger flares but don't cause the underlying disease.
**Signs to watch for:** Persistent diarrhoea, often with blood or mucus; abdominal pain and cramping; urgency to reach a toilet; tiredness; loss of appetite and weight; and in children, slowed growth. Flares can also bring fever, joint pains, skin rashes, and eye inflammation. Severe pain, heavy bleeding, or high fever during a flare need urgent care.
**How doctors confirm it:** Blood tests (inflammation markers, anaemia, nutrient levels) and stool tests, including a marker of gut inflammation (calprotectin), which helps distinguish IBD from IBS. Colonoscopy with biopsies is the key test. MRI or CT scans map the extent of disease — especially for Crohn's — and capsule endoscopy (a swallowed camera pill) can examine the small intestine.
**How it's treated:** Medicines aim to switch off inflammation and keep it off: anti-inflammatory medicines, corticosteroids for short-term control of flares, immune-calming medicines, and biologic therapies that target specific immune pathways. These control IBD but don't cure it. Surgery is sometimes needed — removing the colon in ulcerative colitis, or damaged sections and complications in Crohn's. Because long-standing colitis raises bowel-cancer risk, people with IBD are offered regular surveillance colonoscopies.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — there's currently no known way to prevent IBD. What can be done is to reduce harm: not smoking (especially important in Crohn's), taking maintenance medicines even when well, keeping vaccinations up to date, and attending surveillance appointments so problems are caught early.

## Coeliac disease
**Summary:** Coeliac disease is an immune condition in which eating gluten — a protein in wheat, barley, and rye — damages the lining of the small intestine. It is not a food allergy or a lifestyle preference, and the only treatment is a strict, lifelong gluten-free diet.
**Image:** images/coeliac-disease.jpg
**What it is:** When someone with coeliac disease eats gluten, their immune system attacks the small intestine and flattens the tiny finger-like projections (villi) that absorb nutrients. With the villi damaged, food — and the iron, calcium, and vitamins it carries — is poorly absorbed. The damage heals on a strict gluten-free diet, but returns if gluten is eaten again, even in small amounts and even without symptoms.
**Why it happens:** Coeliac disease runs in families and develops in people carrying certain genes (called HLA-DQ2 or DQ8) — but the genes alone aren't enough; most carriers never develop it. It can start at any age, sometimes after an illness, pregnancy, or other stress on the body. Close relatives of someone with coeliac disease have a higher chance and are often offered testing.
**Signs to watch for:** Symptoms vary widely: diarrhoea or constipation, bloating, abdominal pain, tiredness, and weight loss; in children, poor growth. Many people have few gut symptoms and instead show the effects of poor absorption — iron-deficiency anaemia, thinning bones, mouth ulcers, or an intensely itchy blistering rash. Some people have no symptoms at all and are found through family screening.
**How doctors confirm it:** A blood test for coeliac antibodies (tissue transglutaminase IgA), done **while you are still eating gluten** — testing after starting a gluten-free diet can give a falsely reassuring result. Diagnosis is usually confirmed with a biopsy of the small intestine taken during a camera test (gastroscopy). Gene testing is sometimes used to rule the condition out.
**How it's treated:** A strict, lifelong gluten-free diet — no medicine can substitute for it. That means avoiding wheat, barley, and rye in all forms and watching for hidden gluten in sauces, processed foods, and some medicines; a dietitian's help is valuable, especially at the start. Blood tests and sometimes a repeat biopsy check that the gut is healing, and deficiencies such as iron or vitamin D are corrected with supplements.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not at present — there's no proven way to stop coeliac disease developing in someone genetically susceptible. The preventable part is the damage: early diagnosis and a strict gluten-free diet protect the intestine, the bones, and long-term health, and screening close relatives finds silent cases.

## Diverticular disease
**Summary:** Diverticula are small pouches that push out through the wall of the large intestine, becoming more common with age. Most cause no trouble at all (diverticulosis), but if a pouch becomes inflamed or infected — diverticulitis — it causes pain and fever and needs medical care.
**Image:** images/diverticular-disease.svg
**What it is:** Two words worth separating: *diverticulosis* means the pouches are present — it is very common in older adults and usually silent; *diverticulitis* means one or more pouches have become inflamed or infected, which is the painful illness. Less commonly, a pouch can bleed, causing painless but sometimes heavy rectal bleeding, or an inflamed segment can narrow the bowel over time.
**Why it happens:** The pouches form at weak points in the bowel wall, where blood vessels pass through, under pressure from pushing stool along. Age is the biggest factor; a low-fibre diet, excess weight, smoking, lack of exercise, and heavy use of anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs) all raise the risk of diverticulitis. Older advice to avoid nuts, seeds, and popcorn has not held up — studies have not shown they trigger attacks, and doctors generally no longer recommend avoiding them.
**Signs to watch for:** Diverticulosis itself: usually none. Diverticulitis: steady pain, most often in the lower left abdomen, with fever, nausea, and a change in bowel habit (constipation or diarrhoea). Severe or worsening pain, vomiting, a rigid belly, or heavy bleeding are emergencies — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Examination and blood tests (looking for infection and inflammation). A CT scan is the usual test during an attack — it confirms diverticulitis and shows complications such as an abscess or perforation. Colonoscopy is typically done later, after things settle, to inspect the bowel and rule out other causes such as cancer.
**How it's treated:** Diverticulosis needs no treatment beyond a fibre-rich diet. Mild diverticulitis is often managed with rest, fluids, a temporary lighter diet, and pain relief; antibiotics are used selectively rather than automatically. More severe attacks need hospital care — intravenous fluids and antibiotics, drainage of any abscess — and surgery is considered for perforation, uncontrolled infection, repeated severe attacks, or narrowing of the bowel.
**Can it be prevented?:** The pouches themselves can't be undone, but attacks can be made less likely: a fibre-rich diet, regular exercise, maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, and avoiding unnecessary NSAID painkillers all commonly help.

## Bowel obstruction
**Summary:** A bowel obstruction is a blockage that stops stool and gas moving through the intestine. It is a medical emergency: the trapped bowel can swell, lose its blood supply, and perforate, so it needs hospital care immediately.
**Image:** images/bowel-obstruction.jpg
**What it is:** The blockage can be a physical barrier — scar tissue, a hernia, a tumour, or impacted stool — or, less often, the bowel can stop working temporarily (ileus) after surgery or severe illness even without a barrier. Above the blockage, the intestine swells with fluid and gas; the person cannot pass stool or wind, and vomiting follows. Without treatment, pressure and loss of blood flow can kill a section of bowel, which then leaks — a life-threatening complication.
**Why it happens:** The commonest cause in people who have had abdominal surgery is adhesions — bands of scar tissue that kink or trap the bowel. Other causes include hernias (a loop of bowel caught in the hernia), bowel tumours, severe diverticulitis or Crohn's narrowing, and occasionally a twisted bowel (volvulus) or hard, impacted stool.
**Signs to watch for:** Cramping abdominal pain that comes in waves, a swollen bloated belly, vomiting (which may become green or foul), and being unable to pass gas or stool. If this pattern appears, go to hospital or call your local emergency number now — don't wait to see if it settles. Fever, severe constant pain, or a rigid belly suggest the bowel is in danger.
**How doctors confirm it:** Examination, blood tests, and urgent imaging — a CT scan is the main test, showing the blockage, its location and cause, and whether the bowel wall is at risk. Plain X-rays can show the classic pattern of swollen loops and fluid levels. Doctors also check for dehydration and correct salt imbalances.
**How it's treated:** Hospital treatment starts with "drip and suck": intravenous fluids to correct dehydration, and a tube passed through the nose into the stomach to drain fluid and relieve pressure, with nothing to eat or drink. Some obstructions — especially from adhesions — settle with this alone. Surgery is needed if the blockage doesn't resolve, if a hernia or tumour is the cause, or if the bowel's blood supply is threatened; damaged bowel may need to be removed.
**Can it be prevented?:** Only partly. Treating hernias when advised, seeking prompt care for abdominal pain after previous surgery, and keeping constipation well managed all reduce risk. People who have had an obstruction before should treat early symptoms seriously and seek care quickly.

## Colorectal (bowel) cancer
**Summary:** Colorectal cancer is cancer of the large intestine or rectum. It usually develops slowly from small growths called polyps, which is why screening genuinely prevents it — polyps found early can be removed before they turn cancerous.
**Image:** images/colorectal-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Most bowel cancers start as adenomas — benign polyps on the bowel lining — that gradually accumulate changes over years and become malignant. Caught early, bowel cancer is highly treatable; the difficulty is that early cancer, like polyps, often causes no symptoms at all, which is exactly what screening programmes are designed to get around. For the full picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Risk rises with age and with a family history of bowel cancer or advanced polyps; some inherited conditions (such as Lynch syndrome) raise it a lot. Long-standing ulcerative colitis or Crohn's colitis also increases risk. Lifestyle factors matter: diets high in red and processed meat and low in fibre, excess weight, inactivity, smoking, and heavy alcohol use all contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** Blood in the stool or bleeding from the rectum; a lasting change in bowel habits — looser stools, more frequent stools, or new constipation; persistent abdominal pain or bloating; unexplained weight loss; and tiredness from iron-deficiency anaemia. Any of these lasting more than a few weeks should be checked — see a doctor soon. Heavy bleeding is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Screening uses stool tests that detect hidden blood (such as FIT), offered to people in the eligible age ranges in many countries, with colonoscopy to follow up a positive result. For symptoms, colonoscopy is the key test — the whole colon can be inspected, biopsies taken, and polyps removed at the same time. CT scans then help stage the cancer and plan treatment.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the stage: surgery to remove the affected section of bowel is the main treatment for localised cancer; chemotherapy is added when there's a risk of spread or spread has occurred; radiotherapy is used especially for rectal cancer; and targeted medicines or immunotherapy suit some advanced cancers. Small, very early cancers and polyps can sometimes be removed entirely during colonoscopy.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — and unusually effectively for a cancer. Screening prevents it directly by finding and removing polyps before they turn. Beyond that: a fibre-rich diet, limiting red and processed meat, maintaining a healthy weight, staying active, not smoking, and limiting alcohol all lower the risk. People with a strong family history or IBD should ask their doctor when their personal screening should start.
