## Coronary artery disease & angina
**Summary:** Coronary artery disease is the gradual narrowing of the arteries that feed the heart muscle itself, caused by fatty deposits (plaque) in the artery walls. Angina is the chest pain that happens when the narrowed arteries can't deliver enough blood — usually during exertion. It's both a symptom to manage and a warning sign that a heart attack may follow.
**Image:** images/coronary-artery-disease.jpg
**What it is:** Over years, plaque builds up inside the coronary arteries (a process called atherosclerosis), making them narrower and stiffer. When the heart works harder — walking uphill, climbing stairs, in cold weather — it needs more blood than the narrowed arteries can supply, and the result is angina: typically a pressing, squeezing, or heavy feeling in the centre of the chest that eases within a few minutes of resting. This predictable pattern is called stable angina. If the pain starts happening at rest, lasts longer, or feels different from usual (unstable angina), that's an emergency — it can be the run-up to a heart attack.
**Why it happens:** The risk factors for plaque build-up are well known: smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, excess weight (especially around the waist), physical inactivity, an unhealthy diet, increasing age, and a family history of early heart disease. Several of these together multiply the risk.
**Signs to watch for:** Chest pressure or tightness during effort that settles with rest; pain spreading to the arm, neck, jaw, or back; breathlessness on exertion; unusual fatigue. Pain that occurs at rest, wakes you from sleep, lasts more than a few minutes, or doesn't ease — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** History and examination, an ECG, blood tests for cholesterol and blood sugar, and a stress test (watching the heart while it works harder). An echocardiogram shows how the heart is pumping. A coronary angiogram — dye injected so the arteries show on X-ray — or a CT angiogram maps the narrowings directly.
**How it's treated:** Lifestyle change is the foundation: stopping smoking, regular activity, and a heart-friendly diet. Medication classes doctors commonly use include antiplatelet medicines (to reduce clotting), statins (to lower cholesterol), blood-pressure medicines, and anti-anginal medicines that ease the heart's workload or widen its arteries. For severe narrowings, approaches include angioplasty with a stent (opening the artery with a tiny balloon and mesh tube) or bypass surgery (routing blood around the blockage).
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Not smoking, staying active, eating plenty of vegetables and whole grains with less salt and saturated fat, and keeping blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar under control all slow or prevent plaque build-up. The earlier these habits start, the better they work.

## Heart attack
**Summary:** A heart attack (myocardial infarction) happens when a coronary artery suddenly blocks and part of the heart muscle, starved of blood, starts to die. This is an emergency: call your local emergency number immediately — do not drive yourself. The faster treatment starts, the more heart muscle can be saved.
**Image:** images/heart-attack.png
**What it is:** Most heart attacks begin with coronary artery disease. A plaque inside the artery cracks or ruptures, a blood clot forms on it, and the artery blocks — sometimes completely. Heart muscle beyond the blockage begins to die within minutes to hours, which is why emergency teams treat this as a race against time ("time is muscle"). A rarer type happens without a major blockage, for example from a severe spasm of the artery.
**Why it happens:** The underlying cause is the same plaque build-up as coronary artery disease, so the risk factors are the same: smoking, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, excess weight, inactivity, and family history. An attack can be triggered by heavy exertion or intense emotional stress, but many happen with no obvious trigger at all — including during sleep.
**Signs to watch for:** Chest pain, pressure, heaviness, or tightness, often spreading to the arm, neck, jaw, back, or stomach; sudden shortness of breath; breaking into a cold sweat; nausea; light-headedness or fainting. Call your local emergency number at once — don't wait to see if it passes, and don't drive yourself. Important: women, older adults, and people with diabetes more often have "atypical" attacks — breathlessness, extreme fatigue, upper-abdominal discomfort, or back or jaw pain, sometimes without dramatic chest pain. Some heart attacks are "silent" and only discovered later on an ECG.
**How doctors confirm it:** An ECG is done immediately — it often shows the attack in progress. Blood tests measure troponin, a protein released by damaged heart muscle; the test is usually repeated after a few hours. A coronary angiogram then locates the blockage so it can be treated.
**How it's treated:** Treatment begins before every result is back. Approaches doctors commonly use include antiplatelet and anticoagulant medicines to stop the clot growing, and — the priority — reopening the artery, usually with emergency angioplasty and a stent, or occasionally bypass surgery; clot-dissolving medicines are used where angioplasty isn't available quickly enough. Afterwards, long-term medicines typically include antiplatelets, statins, and other classes that protect the heart, plus cardiac rehabilitation — supervised exercise and education that improves recovery.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly, by preventing coronary artery disease: not smoking, controlling blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar, staying active, and maintaining a healthy weight. For someone having an attack, knowing the symptoms and calling immediately is what limits the damage.

## Heart failure
**Summary:** Heart failure means the heart can't pump efficiently enough to meet the body's needs — it does **not** mean the heart has stopped or is about to stop. It's a long-term condition that usually develops gradually, and with treatment most people can manage it and stay active.
**Image:** images/heart-failure.jpg
**What it is:** The heart muscle becomes either weakened and stretched (so each beat pushes out less blood) or stiff (so it can't relax and fill properly). Blood then backs up: fluid collects in the lungs, causing breathlessness, and in the legs and belly, causing swelling. The kidneys and other organs get less blood than they need. Heart failure is the end result of many different heart problems rather than a single disease.
**Why it happens:** Common causes include coronary artery disease and previous heart attacks (which scar the muscle), long-standing high blood pressure, valve disease, arrhythmias, and diabetes. Heavy alcohol use, some chemotherapy medicines, viral infections of the heart muscle, and congenital defects can also cause it. Sometimes no clear cause is found (called cardiomyopathy).
**Signs to watch for:** Breathlessness during activity and then at rest; waking at night gasping or needing extra pillows to breathe lying flat; swollen ankles, feet, or belly; rapid weight gain over a few days (fluid, not fat); persistent fatigue; a cough or wheeze that won't settle. Sudden severe breathlessness, especially with pink frothy cough — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Examination (listening to the lungs, checking for swelling and raised neck veins), a blood test for BNP or NT-proBNP (a hormone released when the heart is stretched), an ECG, and a chest X-ray. The key test is an echocardiogram, which shows how strongly the heart pumps (its ejection fraction) and how the valves are working. Further blood tests look for the underlying cause.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider include diuretics ("water tablets") to remove excess fluid, and several medicine classes that ease the heart's workload and improve long-term outcomes — including ACE inhibitors and related drugs, beta blockers, mineralocorticoid blockers, and SGLT2 inhibitors (originally diabetes medicines). A low-salt diet, monitoring weight daily, and treating the underlying cause (for example, fixing a valve or controlling blood pressure) all help. Some patients benefit from special pacemaker-type devices or defibrillators; in advanced cases, a heart pump or transplant may be considered. Cardiac rehabilitation and flu/pneumonia vaccination are usually advised.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Controlling blood pressure, preventing and treating coronary artery disease and diabetes, keeping alcohol modest, and not smoking all reduce the risk. Once heart failure has developed it can't usually be reversed, but early treatment greatly improves both symptoms and outlook.

## Arrhythmias
**Summary:** Arrhythmias are faults in the heart's electrical system that make it beat too fast, too slowly, or irregularly. The commonest sustained arrhythmia is atrial fibrillation (AFib), where the upper chambers quiver instead of beating properly — it's important because it raises the risk of stroke.
**Image:** images/arrhythmias.jpg
**What it is:** A healthy heartbeat is set by the heart's natural pacemaker and travels along fixed electrical pathways. In an arrhythmia, that signal starts in the wrong place or travels the wrong way. Types include AFib (fast, chaotic rhythm in the upper chambers; blood can pool and form clots that may travel to the brain), atrial flutter, other fast rhythms from the upper chambers, extra beats (usually harmless), and slow rhythms or heart block (the signal is delayed or blocked). A small number of arrhythmias are dangerous emergencies — ventricular fibrillation, where the heart quivers uselessly, causes cardiac arrest.
**Why it happens:** Arrhythmias become more likely with age, high blood pressure, and any other heart condition — coronary disease, heart failure, valve disease. An overactive thyroid, diabetes, sleep apnoea, heavy alcohol use (a recognised trigger, sometimes called "holiday heart"), caffeine, and some medicines can set them off. Sometimes they occur in a structurally normal heart with no cause found.
**Signs to watch for:** Palpitations — fluttering, racing, pounding, or "skipped" beats; an unusually fast or slow pulse; dizziness or fainting; breathlessness; chest discomfort; unusual fatigue. AFib often has no symptoms at all and is first noticed as an irregular pulse or on an ECG done for another reason. Fainting, severe breathlessness, or chest pain with a racing heartbeat — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** An ECG records the rhythm — but only for the seconds it's attached, so intermittent arrhythmias are chased with a Holter monitor (worn for 24–48 hours) or longer event recorders. An echocardiogram checks the heart's structure, and blood tests look for triggers such as thyroid problems. A stress test may show rhythm problems that appear with exertion.
**How it's treated:** It depends on the type. Approaches doctors commonly consider include medicines to slow a fast rate or keep a normal rhythm, anticoagulant medicines ("blood thinners") for people with AFib to prevent stroke, cardioversion (a controlled electric shock to reset the rhythm), ablation (threading a catheter to the heart to disable the faulty pathway), a pacemaker for slow rhythms, and an implantable defibrillator for dangerous fast rhythms. Treating triggers — thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, excess alcohol — is part of the plan.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Keeping blood pressure controlled, treating other heart conditions, limiting alcohol, and managing weight and sleep apnoea all reduce the chances. Some arrhythmias simply happen, especially with age — which is why noticing an irregular pulse and getting it checked matters.

## Valve disease
**Summary:** The heart has four valves that keep blood flowing in one direction. Valve disease means one or more of them doesn't open fully (stenosis) or doesn't close tightly and leaks (regurgitation). The heart has to work harder to compensate, and over time this can lead to heart failure.
**Image:** images/valve-disease.png
**What it is:** Think of each valve as a set of one-way doors. In stenosis, the doors become stiff, thickened, or narrowed, so blood struggles to get through. In regurgitation, the doors don't seal, so some blood flows backwards with every beat. The aortic valve (the main exit from the heart) and the mitral valve (between the left chambers) are the ones most commonly affected. A doctor often first suspects valve disease on hearing a heart murmur — an extra sound through a stethoscope.
**Why it happens:** The commonest cause in older adults is wear and calcium build-up over a lifetime, especially aortic stenosis. Rheumatic fever — heart damage following an untreated strep throat — remains a major cause in many parts of the world. Other causes include being born with a malformed valve (such as a bicuspid aortic valve, which has two flaps instead of three), infection of a valve (endocarditis), and enlargement of the heart stretching a valve so it leaks.
**Signs to watch for:** Often none for years while the heart compensates. As the valve problem worsens: breathlessness (at first on exertion, later at rest), chest pain during activity, palpitations, dizziness or fainting (a classic warning sign of severe aortic stenosis), swollen ankles, and growing fatigue. Sudden worsening breathlessness needs prompt medical care.
**How doctors confirm it:** A murmur heard on examination usually starts the process. The key test is an echocardiogram, which shows the valves opening and leaking in real time and measures how severe the problem is. An ECG and chest X-ray show the effects on the heart. Sometimes a stress test or coronary angiogram is added — the angiogram particularly before valve surgery, to check the arteries at the same time.
**How it's treated:** Mild disease often needs only monitoring — regular echocardiograms to watch for change ("watchful waiting"). Medicines such as diuretics and blood-pressure medicines can ease symptoms but cannot fix the valve itself. The definitive approaches are valve repair (reshaping the patient's own valve) or valve replacement, either through open-heart surgery or, for suitable patients, through a catheter threaded from the groin — the best-known example replaces the aortic valve this way (often called TAVI). For some patients at higher risk of valve infection, doctors may advise antibiotic cover before certain dental or medical procedures.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Having a strep throat treated promptly reduces the risk of rheumatic fever and its valve damage. Good dental hygiene lowers the risk of valve infection. Blood-pressure control helps. Age-related valve wear itself can't be prevented — but regular check-ups mean a worsening valve is usually caught before it causes serious harm.

## Congenital heart defects
**Summary:** Congenital heart defects are problems with the heart's structure that are present from birth. They range from tiny holes that close on their own to complex conditions needing surgery in infancy — and milder defects are sometimes only discovered in adulthood.
**Image:** images/congenital-heart-defects.png
**What it is:** During pregnancy the heart forms in the first weeks, and sometimes a step goes differently. In plain terms, defects include holes in the wall between the heart's chambers (septal defects), valves that are narrowed, missing, or malformed, major blood vessels that are connected in the wrong places or swapped, and — rarely — a heart that develops with effectively one pumping chamber instead of two. Some defects let oxygen-poor blood bypass the lungs, turning the skin blue-tinged (cyanotic); many others don't. Modern treatment means the great majority of children born with these defects now reach adulthood.
**Why it happens:** Usually, no single cause is found, and it's no one's fault. Known contributors include some genetic conditions (defects are more common in babies with certain chromosome conditions, such as Down syndrome), rubella infection during pregnancy, poorly controlled diabetes in the mother, drinking alcohol or taking certain medicines during pregnancy, and a family history of congenital heart disease. Most cases appear to be a mix of genetic susceptibility and chance.
**Signs to watch for:** In babies: poor feeding or sweating during feeds, fast or laboured breathing, blue-tinged lips or tongue, and slow weight gain. In children: tiring much faster than playmates, breathlessness, fainting, or a murmur heard at a routine check. In adults, a mild defect may cause no symptoms at all and be found incidentally — or show up as breathlessness, palpitations, reduced exercise capacity, or a newly heard murmur. A baby who is blue, struggling to breathe, or feeding very poorly needs urgent care — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Many serious defects are picked up on the routine pregnancy ultrasound scan, and newborn checks — including a painless oxygen measurement on the hand and foot (pulse oximetry) — catch others soon after birth. The key test at any age is an echocardiogram, which maps the heart's structure in detail. ECG and chest X-ray add information, and CT, MRI, or a catheter study is sometimes used to plan treatment.
**How it's treated:** Many mild defects (such as small holes) need only monitoring, and some close by themselves. Approaches doctors commonly consider include medicines to ease symptoms such as heart failure in babies, catheter procedures that close holes or stretch open narrow valves without open surgery, and operations to repair the structure — sometimes a single procedure, sometimes a planned series through childhood. Because repaired hearts still differ from typical hearts, lifelong follow-up with specialists in congenital heart disease is recommended, and some patients are advised antibiotic cover before certain procedures to protect against valve infection.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly not — most defects happen for reasons outside anyone's control. The risk can be lowered by rubella vaccination before pregnancy, good control of diabetes, avoiding alcohol during pregnancy, and reviewing medicines with a doctor when planning a pregnancy. Early detection through pregnancy and newborn screening is what makes the biggest difference to outcomes.
