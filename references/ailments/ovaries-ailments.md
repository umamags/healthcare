## Functional ovarian cysts
**Summary:** A cyst is a fluid-filled sac on the ovary, and simple "functional" cysts form as a normal part of the monthly cycle. They are very common, usually harmless, and most disappear on their own within a few cycles without any treatment.
**Image:** images/ovarian-cysts.jpg
**What it is:** Each month an egg grows inside a small fluid-filled structure called a follicle. Sometimes the follicle doesn't release its egg and keeps growing, or it seals up after ovulation and fills with fluid — either way, the result is a functional cyst. These are a normal by-product of ovulation, not a disease, and they are different from the cysts of PCOS and from endometriosis cysts covered below.
**Why it happens:** They arise from the normal workings of the cycle, so any woman of childbearing age can get them; they are much less common after the menopause. There is usually no underlying cause and nothing the person did or didn't do.
**Signs to watch for:** Most cause no symptoms at all and are found by chance on a scan done for another reason. Larger ones can cause a dull ache or pressure on one side of the pelvis, bloating, or discomfort during sex. A cyst that bursts (ruptures) can cause sudden sharp pain, and one that twists the ovary is covered separately below — sudden severe one-sided pelvic pain, especially with nausea, vomiting, or fainting, is an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic ultrasound is the main test — it shows the cyst's size and whether it looks simple (fluid only) or more complex. A pregnancy test is usually done to rule out other causes of pain, and blood tests may be added if the cyst's appearance needs clarifying.
**How it's treated:** Most need no treatment — "watchful waiting" with a repeat scan after a few weeks or months confirms the cyst has gone. Pain relief covers any discomfort in the meantime. A cyst is more likely to need a procedure (usually keyhole surgery to remove just the cyst) if it is large, keeps growing, doesn't settle after a few cycles, looks complex on scan, or causes repeated pain.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably — because they come from normal ovulation, they can't really be prevented, and they don't need to be. Hormonal contraception stops ovulation so fewer new cysts form, but it's not prescribed just for cysts that are already there.

## PCOS (polycystic ovary syndrome)
**Summary:** PCOS is a common hormone condition in which the ovaries work slightly differently, often causing irregular or missed periods, acne or extra hair growth, and difficulty becoming pregnant. It's closely linked with weight and insulin, and managing it well also protects long-term health.
**Image:** images/pcos.jpg
**What it is:** In PCOS the ovaries often contain many small follicles that started developing but stalled, so eggs are released irregularly or not at all — despite the name, these are follicles, not true cysts, and they aren't harmful in themselves. The ovaries and body also tend to make or respond to somewhat higher levels of androgens ("male-type" hormones that all women have in small amounts), which drive many of the symptoms. PCOS is a lifelong tendency rather than something that is cured, but its effects can be managed very effectively.
**Why it happens:** The exact cause isn't fully understood. It runs in families, so genes play a part, and insulin is central: many people with PCOS have insulin resistance, meaning the body needs more insulin to handle sugar, and that extra insulin pushes the ovaries to make more androgens. Excess weight makes insulin resistance and symptoms worse, though PCOS also occurs in people of healthy weight.
**Signs to watch for:** Irregular, infrequent, or absent periods; difficulty getting pregnant; acne that persists beyond the teens; extra hair on the face, chest, or tummy; thinning hair on the scalp; and weight that is easy to gain and hard to lose. Mood problems and poor sleep are also common.
**How doctors confirm it:** There is no single test. Doctors look for a pattern — typically at least two of: irregular ovulation, signs or blood-test evidence of excess androgens, and an ultrasound appearance of polycystic ovaries. Blood tests for hormones, blood sugar, and cholesterol are usual, and other causes of similar symptoms (thyroid problems, for example) are ruled out.
**How it's treated:** Lifestyle is the first-line treatment: regular activity and eating patterns that help weight and insulin make a real difference to cycles, skin, and fertility. Beyond that, treatment is tailored to what matters most right now — hormonal contraception to regulate periods and help skin and hair, medicines for excess hair or acne, fertility treatment to trigger ovulation when pregnancy is wanted, and medicines that improve insulin sensitivity in some cases.
**Can it be prevented?:** The tendency itself can't be prevented, but its long-term effects largely can. Because PCOS raises the lifetime risk of type 2 diabetes and heart disease, regular checks of blood sugar, cholesterol, and blood pressure — alongside healthy weight and activity — are a core part of care, not an optional extra.

## Endometriomas (endometriosis cysts)
**Summary:** An endometrioma is a cyst filled with old blood that forms on the ovary when endometriosis tissue grows there. Nicknamed "chocolate cysts" for their dark contents, they differ from simple functional cysts because they don't go away on their own, and they can affect fertility.
**Image:** images/endometriomas.jpg
**What it is:** Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue similar to the womb lining grows outside the womb. When it grows on or in an ovary, blood from that tissue collects month after month into a cyst — an endometrioma. The main endometriosis entry is in the uterus file; this entry covers the ovarian cysts specifically. Unlike a functional cyst, an endometrioma won't settle by itself, though it may stay stable for long periods.
**Why it happens:** They happen only as part of endometriosis, whose own causes are not fully understood (see the uterus file). Anything from one small cyst to cysts on both ovaries can develop over time, and endometriosis elsewhere in the pelvis often coexists.
**Signs to watch for:** Many cause no symptoms and are found on a fertility scan. Others cause ongoing pelvic pain, painful periods, pain during sex, or pain around ovulation. Large or bilateral cysts can reduce the ovary's egg reserve, which matters when planning pregnancy. Sudden severe one-sided pain is not typical of an endometrioma itself but can mean rupture or twisting — treat it as an emergency: call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic ultrasound usually has a characteristic appearance ("ground glass") that experienced sonographers recognise. MRI can add detail when surgery is being planned. The only completely certain confirmation is seeing and testing the tissue at laparoscopy, which is usually combined with treatment rather than done just to look.
**How it's treated:** It depends on pain, size, and pregnancy plans. Options doctors commonly consider include watchful waiting for small, painless cysts; hormone treatments that quieten endometriosis and pain (though they don't shrink cysts much or help fertility directly); and keyhole surgery to remove the cyst when it's large, painful, growing, or fertility treatment requires it. Surgery is weighed carefully because removing the cyst can also remove some healthy egg-containing tissue.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not specifically — endometriomas can't be prevented, but hormone treatment used for endometriosis can reduce the chance of them coming back after surgery. The practical protection is monitoring: periodic scans for known cysts and prompt review of new or worsening pain.

## Ovarian torsion
**Summary:** Ovarian torsion means the ovary has twisted on its supporting ligaments, cutting off its own blood supply. It's uncommon but a true surgical emergency — prompt keyhole surgery to untwist it can save the ovary, and hours matter.
**Image:** images/ovarian-torsion.svg
**What it is:** The ovary hangs from ligaments and shares a stalk with the fallopian tube, through which its blood vessels run. If the ovary flips or rotates — often because a cyst or mass makes it heavier and unbalanced — that stalk twists like a wrung cloth, first blocking the veins and then the artery. Starved of blood, the ovary becomes swollen and painful, and if the twist isn't undone in time the tissue can die.
**Why it happens:** A cyst or benign growth on the ovary is the usual trigger, because it changes the ovary's weight and balance; that's why torsion most often happens in the childbearing years when cysts are common. It can also occur in pregnancy, after fertility treatment that enlarges the ovaries, and occasionally in girls before puberty with no cyst at all.
**Signs to watch for:** The classic picture is sudden, severe pain on one side of the pelvis that comes on over minutes, often with nausea and vomiting; fainting can occur. The pain sometimes follows physical activity. This is one of the card's emergencies — call your local emergency number or get to emergency care immediately rather than waiting to see if it settles; pain that eases doesn't guarantee the ovary has untwisted.
**How doctors confirm it:** Doctors combine the history and examination with a pelvic ultrasound, often using Doppler to look at blood flow in the ovary. Scans can occasionally look normal even when torsion is present, so if suspicion is high, doctors proceed to laparoscopy — looking inside directly is the definitive test, and it doubles as the treatment.
**How it's treated:** Keyhole surgery (laparoscopy) as an emergency: the surgeon untwists the ovary and watches for its colour and blood flow to return, and removes any cyst that caused the twist, usually keeping the ovary. Even an ovary that looks dark and swollen at surgery often recovers, so removal is a last resort. The rare exception is tissue that is clearly dead or a mass that looks suspicious.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not directly — there's no way to stop an ovary twisting. The realistic protection is speed: treating sudden severe one-sided pelvic pain as an emergency gives surgery the best chance of saving the ovary. Anyone with a known large ovarian cyst should know this symptom and act on it immediately.

## Premature ovarian insufficiency
**Summary:** Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) is when the ovaries stop working normally before the age of 40, causing irregular or stopped periods and low estrogen. It isn't quite the same as an early menopause, because ovarian function can switch on and off — and it has important implications for bones, heart, and fertility.
**Image:** images/premature-ovarian-insufficiency.png
**What it is:** In POI the ovaries produce eggs and estrogen unreliably or not at all, well ahead of the usual age of menopause (around the early fifties). The key difference from menopause is that function can be intermittent — periods may occasionally return, and ovulation and even pregnancy can sometimes happen unexpectedly. It affects women in their thirties and sometimes their teens and twenties.
**Why it happens:** Often no cause is found. Known causes include genetic conditions affecting the X chromosome, autoimmune problems in which the immune system targets the ovary, and damage from chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or ovarian surgery. A family history of early menopause raises the likelihood.
**Signs to watch for:** Periods becoming irregular or stopping before 40 is the central sign, often with menopause-like symptoms — hot flushes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, poor sleep, low mood, and reduced sex drive. Difficulty becoming pregnant may be how it first comes to light.
**How doctors confirm it:** Blood tests showing menopause-range hormone levels (high FSH, low estrogen) on more than one occasion, alongside the period history. Doctors then look for a cause with further blood tests — genetic and autoimmune checks — and usually a bone-density (DEXA) scan, since low estrogen thins bones.
**How it's treated:** Hormone therapy is the mainstay: estrogen (with a progestogen if the womb is present) is normally recommended until at least the average age of natural menopause, not mainly for symptoms but to protect the bones and heart against years of estrogen shortage. Calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise support bone health. For fertility, spontaneous pregnancy occasionally happens but can't be relied on; options doctors discuss include egg donation and other paths to parenthood, along with psychological support, which matters — the diagnosis is often a shock.
**Can it be prevented?:** In most cases no — it's usually not caused by anything the person did, and lifestyle doesn't switch the ovaries off early. What can be prevented is the harm from untreated estrogen deficiency: starting hormone therapy promptly and keeping up bone and heart checks protects long-term health.

## Ovarian cancer
**Summary:** Ovarian cancer is cancer that starts in the ovary (or closely related tissue such as the fallopian tube). Its symptoms are famously vague — but they are not truly silent: persistent bloating, feeling full quickly, pelvic pain, and needing to urinate urgently are the pattern to act on, especially because there is no reliable screening test.
**Image:** images/ovarian-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Ovarian cancer means abnormal cells growing out of control in the ovary; most cases are of a type called epithelial ovarian cancer, and research now suggests many of these actually begin in the fallopian tube. It is most common after the menopause. Because its symptoms look like everyday digestive or bladder complaints, it is often found at a later stage — which makes acting on persistent symptoms genuinely important. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** The exact trigger is unknown. Risk is higher with a strong family history of ovarian or breast cancer, and with inherited gene faults such as BRCA1 and BRCA2, which can be identified by genetic testing; risk also rises with age and with conditions such as endometriosis. Having children, breastfeeding, and using hormonal contraception are associated with lower risk, but most people who develop ovarian cancer have no strong risk factor.
**Signs to watch for:** The card's list, and the key word is *persistent*: bloating on most days for weeks rather than coming and going; feeling full quickly or losing appetite; ongoing pelvic or tummy pain; and needing to urinate urgently or much more often. New IBS-like symptoms starting after 50, unexplained weight loss, and unusual tiredness are also reasons to get checked. These symptoms are far more often caused by something harmless — but if they persist for a few weeks, get them assessed rather than waiting.
**How doctors confirm it:** A pelvic examination and ultrasound are first steps, plus a blood test for CA-125 — a marker that can be raised in ovarian cancer but also in many harmless conditions (including endometriosis and fibroids), which is exactly why it is a marker, not a screening test: no blood test or scan has been shown to screen healthy women reliably, so symptom awareness is the real early-warning system. CT scans map the extent of disease, and diagnosis is confirmed on tissue, usually at surgery, under a gynaecologic oncologist.
**How it's treated:** Treatment usually combines surgery (removing the ovaries, tubes, womb, and any visible spread, tailored to the stage and the person's situation) with chemotherapy. Targeted medicines are used for some types, particularly where an inherited gene fault is present. Care is led by gynaecologic oncology teams, and clinical trials are often discussed. When cancer is found early and confined to the ovary, outcomes are much better — another reason persistent symptoms deserve prompt attention.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no proven way to prevent it and no screening programme to rely on, so prevention is about risk awareness: knowing your family history, asking about genetic testing if ovarian or breast cancer runs in the family, and — for people at very high inherited risk — discussing risk-reducing surgery to remove the ovaries and tubes with a specialist. For everyone, the most protective action is simple: report bloating, fullness, pelvic pain, or urinary urgency that doesn't settle within a few weeks.
