Ailments of the scrotum — expanded from the scrotum card.

One rule runs through this whole page: sudden, severe pain in the scrotum is treated as a twisted testicle (testicular torsion — covered in full on the testes page) until proven otherwise. Never wait to see if severe pain settles on its own — call your local emergency number or get to emergency care immediately, because saving a twisted testicle is a race against the clock.

## Hydrocele
**Summary:** A hydrocele is a collection of clear fluid around a testicle, inside the scrotum. It causes a smooth, usually painless swelling and is very common — in newborn babies it often disappears on its own, and in adults it is usually harmless, though it should still be checked once.
**Image:** images/hydrocele.jpg
**What it is:** Each testicle sits inside a thin sac. Normally a tiny amount of fluid lubricates that sac, but in a hydrocele extra fluid builds up, so one side of the scrotum slowly swells. The testicle itself is usually completely normal — it's the fluid around it that has increased. In babies, hydroceles are present at birth; in adults they tend to appear gradually, often after the age of 40.
**Why it happens:** In babies, a small passage between the belly and the scrotum that should close before birth stays open a little longer, letting fluid through — it nearly always seals itself in the first year or two. In adults, the cause is often unclear: the sac simply absorbs fluid more slowly than it makes it. Sometimes a hydrocele forms after injury, infection, or inflammation inside the scrotum, which is why a new one in an adult deserves a check.
**Signs to watch for:** A one-sided, smooth swelling that usually doesn't hurt, though a large one can cause heaviness, a dragging feeling, or discomfort in tight clothing. The size may vary a little through the day. Pain, redness, or a hard lump are not typical of a simple hydrocele and need prompt assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical examination, often including shining a light through the swelling (fluid glows; solid lumps don't). A scrotal ultrasound is the key test in adults — it shows the fluid clearly and, just as importantly, confirms the testicle underneath is healthy, because occasionally swelling like this can hide a problem in the testicle itself.
**How it's treated:** Most hydroceles need no treatment at all — if it isn't bothering you, it can simply be watched. Babies' hydroceles are usually just observed, since most resolve. If a hydrocele is large, heavy, or uncomfortable, a small operation to remove or fold back the sac is the lasting fix. Drawing the fluid off with a needle is sometimes offered, but the fluid usually comes back.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not really — there's no known way to stop one forming. Wearing supportive underwear and a protective cup for contact sports reduces scrotal injury generally. The practical advice is to know what's normal for you and get any new swelling examined once, so a simple hydrocele isn't masking something else.

## Varicocele
**Summary:** A varicocele is a bunch of enlarged veins inside the scrotum — the same kind of varicose vein that can appear in legs. It's very common, usually on the left side, often causes no symptoms at all, and in most men needs no treatment.
**Image:** images/varicocele.png
**What it is:** The veins that drain blood away from a testicle form a network inside the cord above it. When the valves in those veins don't work well, blood pools and the veins swell and tangle. A larger varicocele is often described as feeling like a "bag of worms" above the testicle. Because of how the veins are plumbed, the left side is affected far more often than the right.
**Why it happens:** Faulty or missing valves in the testicular vein let blood flow backward and pool, especially when standing. Varicoceles usually appear during or after puberty and may slowly become more noticeable over time. A varicocele that appears suddenly in an older man, or one on the right side alone, is unusual and should be checked with scans of the abdomen, since pressure on the vein higher up can occasionally be the cause.
**Signs to watch for:** Often nothing — many are found during a routine examination or a fertility check. When there are symptoms, they're typically a dull, heavy ache that builds through the day, is worse after standing, exercise, or hot weather, and eases when lying down. The swelling itself shrinks or disappears when you lie flat, and there may be visible veins under the skin.
**How doctors confirm it:** A physical exam while standing — and sometimes while straining, which makes the veins fill — is usually enough. A scrotal ultrasound confirms it and lets the doctor measure the testicle. In teenagers this matters: if the testicle on the varicocele side is noticeably smaller, that's a reason to treat rather than watch. If a varicocele might be affecting fertility, a semen analysis checks sperm numbers and movement.
**How it's treated:** A varicocele that causes no pain and isn't affecting fertility or testicle growth is usually just left alone. Repair is considered when there is persistent aching, when a teenager's testicle is smaller on that side, or when a couple is having fertility problems and semen results are abnormal. Repair means tying off the faulty veins, either with a small operation or by blocking the vein through a tube threaded from the groin or neck (embolisation). Supportive underwear and simple painkillers often ease a mild ache.
**Can it be prevented?:** No — it's a matter of vein anatomy, not lifestyle. The fertility link deserves an honest explanation: varicoceles are common in men with no fertility problems at all, but they are found more often in men being treated for infertility, and pooled warm blood around the testicle is thought to lower sperm production in some men. Repair improves semen results in selected cases, which is why it's offered for that specific situation rather than to everyone.

## Epididymal cyst / spermatocele
**Summary:** An epididymal cyst is a small, fluid-filled sac on the epididymis — the coiled tube that sits behind the testicle. A spermatocele is the same sort of cyst when it contains sperm. Both are extremely common, harmless, and usually found by chance.
**Image:** images/epididymal-cyst.jpg
**What it is:** The lump map helps here. A cyst feels like a smooth, round, pea-to-grape-sized lump sitting behind or just above the testicle — and crucially, it's separate from the testicle itself; you can feel the testicle distinctly below it. That separateness is the main clue distinguishing it from a lump growing within the testicle, which is the kind that must never be ignored. (The epididymis itself, and how sperm move through it, are covered on the sperm-ducts page.)
**Why it happens:** The epididymis is a single long tube coiled into a small space. A tiny section can become blocked or balloon out, forming a sac that slowly fills with clear fluid — or, in a spermatocele, with fluid containing sperm. Why this happens in some men and not others isn't clear; it becomes more common with age and is not caused by anything you did, by sexual activity, or by injury in most cases.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually a completely painless lump you notice while washing, which may stay the same size for years or grow very slowly. Larger cysts can cause a feeling of heaviness or a mild ache. A lump that is painful, grows quickly, feels hard, or can't be separated from the testicle in your own examination doesn't fit this pattern and needs a prompt appointment.
**How doctors confirm it:** Often a physical examination is enough — the position (behind the testicle, separate from it) and the smooth, fluid feel are characteristic. If there's any doubt at all, a scrotal ultrasound settles it quickly: it shows a simple fluid-filled sac and confirms the testicle itself is normal. No blood tests are usually needed.
**How it's treated:** Most are left entirely alone — they are not cancer, do not turn into cancer, and do not affect fertility in almost all cases. Removal is only considered if a cyst is large, growing, or causing persistent discomfort, and it's a small operation. Doctors are generally cautious about operating, because surgery on the epididymis carries a small risk of scarring that could affect sperm flow on that side — a real consideration for men who may want children. Drawing the fluid off with a needle is rarely recommended, as cysts usually refill.
**Can it be prevented?:** No known prevention exists. The useful habit is the same as for all scrotal lumps: know what your scrotum normally feels like, and have any new lump examined once. A cyst that has been examined and confirmed is nothing to keep worrying about.

## Inguinal hernia
**Summary:** An inguinal hernia is a bulge where a loop of bowel (or fatty tissue) pushes through a weak spot in the groin. In men, the bulge can travel down into the scrotum, making one side swell. Surgery is the only lasting fix — and a hernia that becomes trapped is an emergency.
**Image:** images/inguinal-hernia.png
**What it is:** In men, the cord carrying the blood vessels and sperm duct passes through a tunnel in the groin wall (the inguinal canal). That tunnel is a natural weak point. If tissue pushes through it, it creates a bulge in the groin crease; larger hernias slide down the tunnel and into the scrotum itself. It's the same canal the testicle descended through before birth.
**Why it happens:** A combination of a weak spot in the abdominal wall and pressure from inside the belly. Straining at stool with constipation, a long-standing cough, heavy lifting, and excess weight all raise the pressure; the weakness itself may have been there since birth. Hernias become more common with age as tissues lose strength.
**Signs to watch for:** A bulge in the groin or a swollen scrotum that appears when you stand, cough, or lift, and often disappears completely when you lie down. Many people can gently push it back. There may be a dragging ache or heaviness, especially by the end of the day. The emergency signs are different: a bulge that becomes painful, firm, and won't go back in, with nausea or vomiting — that's a trapped hernia (see below).
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually a physical examination, standing and lying down, sometimes while you cough. If the diagnosis is uncertain — or the bulge is only in the scrotum — an ultrasound (or occasionally another scan) distinguishes a hernia from a hydrocele, varicocele, or other causes of scrotal swelling.
**How it's treated:** Surgery is the only way to repair a hernia, and it's one of the most common operations performed. The surgeon pushes the tissue back and strengthens the weak spot, usually with a mesh patch, through either keyhole or open surgery. Small hernias causing no symptoms are sometimes just watched, but they tend to grow, and many doctors advise planned repair. The situation is completely different if the hernia becomes trapped (incarcerated): the bowel is stuck, its blood supply can be cut off, and this needs emergency surgery — if you have a painful bulge that won't go back, with nausea or vomiting, call your local emergency number or go to emergency care now.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Treating constipation and chronic cough, lifting with good technique, and maintaining a healthy weight all reduce pressure on the groin. They can't remove a weak spot that was there from birth, though — and no truss or support belt fixes a hernia, so a growing bulge still deserves a surgical opinion.

## Skin cysts and irritation
**Summary:** The skin of the scrotum commonly grows small, harmless cysts and is prone to itching and irritation from sweat, friction, and products. Most of this is minor and settles with simple care — but sores that don't heal or spreading redness should always be checked.
**Image:** images/scrotal-skin-cysts.jpg
**What it is:** Scrotal skin is thin, warm, and folded — a good environment for small blocked glands. The commonest lumps are sebaceous (epidermoid) cysts: small, firm, round bumps under or in the skin, sometimes several at once, filled with a soft cheesy material. They're on the skin itself rather than inside the sac, and you can usually move the skin over them. Irritation (dermatitis) shows as itching, redness, flaking, or a raw feeling, often in the folds where scrotum meets thigh.
**Why it happens:** Cysts form when a tiny skin gland or hair follicle blocks and slowly fills — no one knows exactly why some people get many. Irritation comes from sweat and heat, friction from clothing or exercise, scented soaps and body washes, laundry products, and sometimes fungal infection in the groin folds (the same family of fungus as athlete's foot). Scratching makes any of these worse and can break the skin, letting infection in.
**Signs to watch for:** Small skin lumps that stay unchanged are typical and usually harmless. Get checked if you notice a sore or ulcer that doesn't heal within a few weeks, a wart-like or hard growth, rapid growth of any lump, spreading redness, pain, swelling, discharge, or a cyst that becomes red, hot, and tender — that usually means it's infected. Persistent itching that doesn't settle with simple measures also deserves a look, since a few skin conditions need specific treatment.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually just by looking and feeling — a doctor can tell a skin cyst from a lump inside the scrotum by examination, and an ultrasound is used if there's any uncertainty. For stubborn irritation, the doctor checks whether fungus is involved (sometimes with skin scrapings) and reviews what products touch the area.
**How it's treated:** Small cysts that don't bother you need nothing; they never turn into cancer. A bothersome or repeatedly infected cyst can be removed with a small procedure under local anaesthetic — squeezing or trying to drain one yourself risks infection and scarring. Irritation usually settles by removing the trigger: plain unscented washing, thorough drying, loose breathable underwear, and a simple moisturiser or barrier cream. Antifungal creams are used if fungus is the cause, and short courses of medicated creams are sometimes prescribed for dermatitis.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Keep the area clean and dry, change out of sweaty clothing after exercise, choose unscented products, and avoid scratching. There's no way to guarantee cysts won't form, but leaving them untouched — no squeezing — prevents most infections.

## Fournier's gangrene
**Summary:** Fournier's gangrene is a rare but very serious bacterial infection that destroys skin and tissue in the scrotum and the area around it (the perineum). It spreads fast and is always an emergency: the treatment is immediate surgery plus antibiotics.
**Image:** images/fourniers-gangrene.jpg
**What it is:** This is a severe infection of the skin and the layers beneath it in the genital and perineal area. It's sometimes called a "flesh-eating" infection, but what's really happening is that bacteria invade the tissue planes under the skin, damaging blood vessels so the tissue dies. It is uncommon, but it's one of the few scrotal conditions where hours genuinely matter.
**Why it happens:** Usually a mix of bacteria enters through a small break in the skin — a minor cut, an insect bite, a sore, or after a procedure — or spreads from a nearby infection. Most cases happen in people whose defences are lowered, especially with diabetes (the biggest risk factor), heavy alcohol use, or a weakened immune system from illness or immune-suppressing medicines. It can, rarely, occur in people with no obvious risk factor.
**Signs to watch for:** Redness and swelling of the scrotum that spread visibly and quickly, severe pain — often worse than the skin changes seem to explain — fever, and feeling generally very unwell. The skin may develop blisters, dark purple or black patches, or a crackling feeling under the skin. Any of this, especially with diabetes, is an emergency — call your local emergency number or go straight to emergency care; do not wait for a routine appointment.
**How doctors confirm it:** This is a clinical emergency diagnosis — doctors act on what they see and how ill the person is rather than waiting for tests. Blood tests and scans (usually CT) help show how far the infection has spread and guide surgery, but they must not delay treatment.
**How it's treated:** Emergency surgery to cut away all infected and dead tissue, strong antibiotics given into a vein, and intensive supportive care. More than one operation is common, as surgeons re-check the area until the infection is fully controlled. Later, reconstructive surgery may be needed once healing is underway. Early treatment is what saves lives and limits how much tissue is lost.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not reliably, but risk can be lowered: good control of diabetes, prompt cleaning and attention to any cut, sore, or boil in the genital area, and not ignoring early spreading redness or swelling. The key message is awareness — this infection is rare, but recognising the pattern (fast-spreading redness + severe pain + fever) and acting immediately is what changes the outcome.
