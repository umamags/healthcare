## Asthma
**Summary:** Asthma is a long-term condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrow too easily, causing episodes of wheezing, coughing, and breathlessness. It is very common, usually manageable, and dangerous mainly when attacks are severe or untreated.
**Image:** images/asthma.jpg
**What it is:** The muscles around the airways tighten, the airway lining swells, and sticky mucus builds up — so air has trouble getting in and out. Between episodes, breathing may feel completely normal. Asthma often starts in childhood but can appear at any age.
**Why it happens:** The tendency often runs in families, especially alongside allergies such as hay fever and eczema. Attacks are set off by triggers: pollen, dust mites, animal dander, cold air, exercise, chest infections, smoke, strong smells, and some medicines (aspirin-type painkillers affect some people). Knowing your own triggers is half the battle.
**Signs to watch for:** Wheezing or a whistling sound when breathing out, a cough that is worse at night or early morning, chest tightness, and breathlessness that comes and goes. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: if you are too breathless to finish a sentence, your reliever inhaler isn't helping, or your lips or face look blue or grey.
**How doctors confirm it:** A history of the symptom pattern plus spirometry — blowing hard into a machine that measures how much air you can push out and how fast. Sometimes spirometry is repeated after an inhaler to see if airflow improves, or a peak-flow diary is kept over a few weeks. Chest X-ray is usually normal in asthma.
**How it's treated:** Two kinds of inhalers do different jobs. Reliever inhalers (bronchodilators) relax the airway muscles quickly during symptoms. Preventer inhalers — usually containing an inhaled corticosteroid — are taken daily to calm the underlying inflammation so attacks don't start. Severe asthma may need add-on tablets or injections prescribed by a specialist. A written asthma action plan helps you adjust treatment early when symptoms stir.
**Can it be prevented?:** Asthma itself can't reliably be prevented, but attacks often can. Avoid tobacco smoke, reduce exposure to your known triggers, keep up with flu vaccines, and take preventer inhalers as prescribed — even when you feel well.

## COPD
**Summary:** COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is long-term lung damage that makes breathing steadily harder over years. It combines two problems — chronic bronchitis and emphysema — which usually occur together.
**Image:** images/copd.jpg
**What it is:** In chronic bronchitis, the airways stay inflamed and produce mucus day after day, causing a long-lasting productive cough. In emphysema, the walls of the tiny air sacs (alveoli) break down, the lungs lose their springiness, and air gets trapped — so oxygen exchange suffers and every breath out is hard work. Most people with COPD have a mix of both; doctors use "COPD" for the combined condition.
**Why it happens:** The usual cause is years of breathing in harmful smoke or particles: cigarette smoke is the biggest factor worldwide, followed by smoke from cooking and heating fires in poorly ventilated homes, and dusty or chemical-heavy workplaces. A rare inherited condition (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency) can cause emphysema in younger non-smokers.
**Signs to watch for:** Breathlessness that creeps up — first on hills or stairs, later on the flat; a daily cough with mucus lasting months; frequent chest infections, especially in winter; wheezing and chest tightness. See a doctor soon for any of these, especially with a smoking history. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: severe breathlessness, bluish lips, or new drowsiness or confusion.
**How doctors confirm it:** Spirometry is the key test — in COPD the airflow obstruction doesn't fully reverse after a bronchodilator inhaler, which is how it differs from asthma. A chest X-ray or CT scan may show over-expanded lungs or damaged air sacs, and pulse oximetry or blood tests can check oxygen levels.
**How it's treated:** Stopping smoke exposure is the single most effective step — it slows further damage at any stage. Bronchodilator inhalers ease breathlessness; some inhalers add an inhaled corticosteroid. Pulmonary rehabilitation (supervised exercise plus education) genuinely improves stamina and quality of life. Flu and pneumonia vaccines guard against flare-ups, and people with advanced disease may need home oxygen.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Not smoking — or quitting — is the main protection, along with ventilating smoky kitchens and using protective equipment in dusty jobs. Damage already done can't be reversed, but quitting stops the decline from accelerating.

## Pneumonia
**Summary:** Pneumonia is an infection of the lung tissue itself, in which the air sacs fill with fluid or pus instead of air. It ranges from a mild illness treated at home to a life-threatening emergency, especially in older people and young children.
**Image:** images/pneumonia.jpg
**What it is:** Germs — most often bacteria or viruses, occasionally fungi — reach the deep lung and trigger inflammation. Affected air sacs can't pass oxygen into the blood properly, which is why pneumonia causes breathlessness and sometimes low blood oxygen.
**Why it happens:** Common bacterial causes include pneumococcus; viruses such as influenza and other respiratory viruses can cause pneumonia directly or open the door to a bacterial infection afterwards. Risk is higher at the extremes of age, with smoking, with chronic lung or heart disease, diabetes, or a weakened immune system. Aspiration — food, drink, or vomit entering the airways — is another route, particularly in people with swallowing problems.
**Signs to watch for:** Cough (sometimes with yellow, green, or rust-coloured mucus), fever and chills, breathlessness, and sharp chest pain that worsens when you breathe in. Older people may show confusion instead of fever. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: severe breathlessness, bluish lips, coughing up blood, or new confusion.
**How doctors confirm it:** Listening to the chest often reveals crackles over the affected area. A chest X-ray is the usual confirming test, showing the patch of infection; pulse oximetry and blood tests gauge severity, and sputum or blood cultures sometimes identify the exact germ.
**How it's treated:** Bacterial pneumonia is treated with antibiotics, usually taken at home if illness is mild. Viral pneumonia doesn't respond to antibiotics, though antiviral medicines help in some cases, such as influenza. Rest, fluids, and fever control support recovery; severe cases need hospital care with oxygen and intravenous antibiotics. Most people improve within days but tiredness can linger for weeks.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Vaccines exist against some causes — pneumococcal and flu vaccines in particular — and are recommended for older adults and people with chronic conditions in many countries. Not smoking, hand hygiene, and treating flu promptly also lower the risk.

## Tuberculosis
**Summary:** Tuberculosis (TB) is a bacterial infection, usually of the lungs, spread through the air. It remains one of the world's biggest infectious killers — and it is completely curable with a full course of antibiotics taken exactly as prescribed.
**Image:** images/tuberculosis.jpg
**What it is:** The TB bacterium (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) spreads when a person with active lung TB coughs, sneezes, or speaks, releasing tiny airborne droplets that others breathe in. Infection can stay dormant for years (latent TB, with no symptoms and no spread) and later become active disease that damages the lungs and makes the person infectious.
**Why it happens:** Anyone can catch TB, but risk rises with close, prolonged contact with an infectious person, crowded or poorly ventilated living conditions, and a weakened immune system — including HIV infection, diabetes, malnutrition, or medicines that suppress immunity.
**Signs to watch for:** A cough lasting more than 2–3 weeks is the headline sign — especially with fever, drenching night sweats, unexplained weight loss, tiredness, and coughing up blood. Any of these deserves a prompt check-up, both for your own sake and to protect people around you. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: coughing up a lot of blood, or severe breathlessness.
**How doctors confirm it:** A sputum test examines coughed-up mucus for TB bacteria — modern rapid molecular tests can detect TB and some drug resistance within hours. A chest X-ray looks for the characteristic lung changes; sometimes several sputum samples, a CT scan, or bronchoscopy samples are needed. Close contacts are usually tested too.
**How it's treated:** Standard drug-susceptible TB is treated with a combination of several antibiotics, typically for about six months. The crucial point: take every dose for the full course, even after you feel better — which often happens within weeks. Stopping early, or taking medicines irregularly, lets the toughest bacteria survive and breeds drug-resistant TB, which is far harder and longer to treat. Many health services use directly observed or video-supported therapy to help people finish.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. The BCG vaccine, given in childhood in many countries, protects young children against the most severe forms, though it is less reliable against adult lung TB. Good ventilation, promptly treating active cases, testing close contacts, and treating latent TB in high-risk people all reduce spread.

## Bronchitis
**Summary:** Bronchitis here means acute bronchitis — a short-lived inflammation of the airways, usually following a cold or flu, that settles within a few weeks. It is different from the chronic bronchitis that forms part of COPD.
**Image:** images/bronchitis.png
**What it is:** A virus infects and inflames the lining of the large airways, which swell and produce extra mucus, triggering a troublesome cough. Don't confuse it with chronic bronchitis: that is a long-term, day-after-day productive cough lasting months, caused mainly by years of smoke exposure, and it is covered under COPD in this series. Acute bronchitis, by contrast, comes on with an infection and goes away.
**Why it happens:** The same viruses that cause colds and flu are the usual culprits; bacteria are a much less common cause. It spreads through coughs and sneezes and on hands and surfaces, and it strikes more often in winter. Smokers and people with asthma may feel it harder and recover more slowly.
**Signs to watch for:** A cough — dry at first, then bringing up clear, white, or coloured mucus — often with a sore chest from coughing, mild wheeze, tiredness, and a low fever. The cough typically lasts one to three weeks, occasionally longer. See a doctor sooner if you cough up blood, have chest pain, run a high fever, or belong to a higher-risk group (very young, older, immune-weakened, or living with chronic lung disease). Emergency — call your local emergency number now: severe breathlessness or bluish lips.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually by listening to your story and your chest — in an otherwise healthy person, tests are often unnecessary. A chest X-ray may be done if pneumonia is suspected, and pulse oximetry can check oxygen levels.
**How it's treated:** Because a virus is usually responsible, antibiotics don't help acute bronchitis and aren't routinely used. Treatment is supportive: rest, plenty of fluids, and simple painkillers or fever reducers if needed; cough medicines offer limited benefit. Inhalers are sometimes used briefly if there is wheezing. The inflammation resolves on its own as the infection clears.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly — the same habits that fight colds: hand washing, covering coughs, not smoking (smoke-damaged airways catch bronchitis more easily), and keeping up with flu vaccination.

## Lung cancer
**Summary:** Lung cancer is uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells in the lung, forming a tumour that can spread to other parts of the body. It is one of the most common and most serious cancers worldwide — and it can affect non-smokers as well as smokers.
**Image:** images/lung-cancer.png
**What it is:** Most lung cancers start in the cells lining the airways or air sacs. Doctors broadly divide them into non-small cell lung cancer (the majority) and small cell lung cancer (faster-growing), because the types are treated differently. Early lung cancer often causes no symptoms at all, which is why it is frequently found late — and why persistent symptoms should never be ignored. Causes, diagnosis, and treatment are covered in more depth in our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** Smoking is by far the biggest risk factor, responsible for most cases — risk rises with how much and how long a person has smoked, and falls after quitting. But non-smokers get lung cancer too: secondhand smoke, radon gas in homes, air pollution, and workplace exposures such as asbestos and certain dusts and fumes all contribute.
**Signs to watch for:** A new cough that won't settle, or a long-standing cough that changes; coughing up blood, even small streaks; breathlessness; chest pain; repeated chest infections that keep returning to the same spot; unexplained weight loss and tiredness. See a doctor promptly for any of these. Emergency — call your local emergency number now: coughing up a lot of blood or severe breathlessness.
**How doctors confirm it:** A chest X-ray is usually the first step, followed by a CT scan for detail. Confirmation needs tissue: bronchoscopy (a camera into the airways) can take samples, or a needle biopsy is done through the chest wall. Further scans check whether the cancer has spread, and the tumour's type guides treatment.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider, depending on type and stage, include surgery to remove the tumour, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted medicines matched to the tumour's genetic changes, and immunotherapy. Earlier-stage cancers are treated with cure in mind; in advanced disease, treatment focuses on control and quality of life, with palliative care alongside. Our cancer section explains these treatments in plain language.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely. Not smoking is the single most powerful step, and quitting at any age cuts risk substantially. Avoid secondhand smoke, test homes for radon where that's locally advised, and use proper protective equipment around asbestos and industrial dusts. Screening with low-dose CT is offered to some high-risk long-term smokers in some countries — ask your doctor whether it applies to you.
