Ailments of the teeth — expanded from the teeth card.

## Tooth decay (cavities)
**Summary:** Tooth decay happens when bacteria in the mouth turn sugars into acid that gradually eats through the tooth, forming a hole (cavity). Early decay is painless and can be reversed; deep decay cannot — it needs a filling or more.
**Image:** images/tooth-decay.jpg
**What it is:** Bacteria in the mouth form a sticky film (plaque) on teeth. When you eat or drink something sugary, these bacteria feed on the sugar and produce acid. Repeated acid attacks dissolve minerals from the enamel — at first leaving a chalky white spot, later breaking the surface down into a cavity. If decay reaches the softer dentin underneath it spreads faster, and if it reaches the pulp (the nerve and blood supply) it can cause pain and infection.
**Why it happens:** Frequent sugar is the main driver — how often matters even more than how much, because every sugary snack or drink restarts an acid attack. Other factors include poor cleaning (plaque left undisturbed), dry mouth (saliva protects teeth), receding gums exposing softer root surfaces, and frequent acidic drinks. Babies can get decay from falling asleep with a bottle.
**Signs to watch for:** Early decay has no symptoms at all, which is why check-ups matter. Later signs include sensitivity to sweet, hot, or cold foods, a visible hole or dark spot, food getting stuck, and eventually toothache. Pain that lingers after hot or cold, or pain that starts on its own, suggests the pulp is involved.
**How dentists confirm it:** A visual and tactile exam (checking each tooth surface), plus X-rays — bitewing X-rays are especially good at showing decay hidden between teeth, where you can't see it. If pain suggests nerve involvement, a pulp vitality test (such as a cold test) checks whether the pulp is alive and healthy, inflamed, or dead.
**How it's treated:** Very early decay can be reversed with fluoride treatment and better cleaning, with no drilling. Once a cavity has formed, the dentist removes the decayed tissue and places a filling. Large cavities may need a crown to rebuild the tooth. If decay has reached the pulp, root canal treatment (often by an endodontist) or extraction are the options dentists commonly consider.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes. Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, clean between teeth daily, and cut down how often you have sugary or acidic food and drink. Protective coatings (sealants) on children's back teeth and regular check-ups — which catch decay while it's still reversible — help too.

## Gum disease (gingivitis and periodontitis)
**Summary:** Gum disease starts as gingivitis — red, swollen gums that bleed easily — which is fully reversible. If it progresses to periodontitis, the infection damages the ligament and bone holding the teeth, and that damage cannot be undone, only stopped.
**Image:** images/gum-disease.png
**What it is:** Plaque that isn't cleaned away irritates the gums, causing gingivitis. Over time, plaque hardens into tartar (calculus) and the gum pulls away from the tooth, forming a "pocket" where bacteria thrive beyond the reach of a toothbrush. In periodontitis, the body's inflammatory response to these bacteria gradually destroys the ligament and bone supporting the tooth; teeth loosen and, in advanced cases, are lost. A periodontist is the specialist who treats this.
**Why it happens:** Plaque build-up is the cause; smoking is the strongest risk factor and also masks bleeding, hiding the disease. Diabetes makes gum disease more likely and harder to control — and the relationship runs both ways, since inflamed gums make blood sugar harder to manage. Gum disease is also linked with heart disease and other conditions, though it hasn't been shown to directly cause them. Crowded teeth and poor diet add to the risk.
**Signs to watch for:** Gums that bleed when you brush or floss, redness and swelling, persistent bad breath, and gums receding so teeth look longer. Later: loose teeth, teeth drifting apart, and changes in how your teeth fit together. Periodontitis is often painless until advanced, so bleeding gums are the warning to act on — not something to ignore because "it doesn't hurt."
**How dentists confirm it:** Gum-pocket probing — gently measuring the depth of the space between gum and tooth with a fine probe at several points around each tooth. Shallow readings are healthy; deep pockets indicate periodontitis. X-rays show how much supporting bone has been lost. Together these let the dentist stage how advanced the disease is.
**How it's treated:** Gingivitis reverses with thorough daily cleaning and a professional clean. Periodontitis is managed, not cured: professional scaling and root planing (deep cleaning below the gumline, by a dentist, hygienist, or periodontist) removes plaque and tartar from the pockets, and meticulous home care keeps bacteria levels down. Advanced cases may need gum surgery. Antibiotics are occasionally used alongside cleaning for specific situations, but cleaning — not medicine — is the core treatment.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, in most cases. Brush twice daily, clean between teeth once a day, don't smoke, keep diabetes controlled, and have regular professional cleaning so tartar doesn't accumulate. Catching gingivitis early is the key — at that stage it's still fully reversible.

## Tooth abscess
**Summary:** A tooth abscess is a pocket of pus caused by a bacterial infection, usually at the tip of a tooth's root. It will not heal on its own, and antibiotics alone can't cure it — the infected tooth itself has to be treated.
**Image:** images/tooth-abscess.jpg
**What it is:** Most abscesses form when decay or a crack lets bacteria into the pulp; the pulp dies and infection spreads out of the root tip into the surrounding bone. Abscesses can also form in a deep gum pocket (a periodontal abscess). The body walls the infection off as a pus-filled swelling, but the source — the dead or infected tissue inside the tooth — remains.
**Why it happens:** Untreated deep decay is the commonest cause. Others include a cracked tooth, a failed or leaking filling, gum disease with deep pockets, and injury that kills the pulp even if the tooth looks intact. Anything that lets bacteria reach the pulp or deep gum tissues can start one.
**Signs to watch for:** A throbbing, persistent toothache, pain when biting, sensitivity to hot and cold, a swollen gum or a small pimple-like "gum boil," a bad taste if it drains, swollen face or jaw, and fever. Get emergency care — call your local emergency number — if there is facial swelling with fever, or any trouble swallowing or breathing: the infection can spread into the neck and become life-threatening.
**How dentists confirm it:** An exam, tapping on the tooth (an abscessed tooth is usually tender to tapping), and X-rays, which can show a dark area of bone loss around the root tip. A pulp vitality test helps confirm whether the pulp is dead.
**How it's treated:** The infection's source must be dealt with. Approaches dentists commonly consider are draining the abscess (through the tooth or a small cut in the gum), root canal treatment to remove the infected pulp and save the tooth (often by an endodontist), or extracting the tooth if it can't be saved. Antibiotics may be prescribed when infection is spreading or the person is unwell — but they support dental treatment; they don't replace it. Over-the-counter painkillers can ease pain while you wait to be seen.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly, yes — because most abscesses start as treatable decay or cracks. Prompt treatment of cavities and toothache, good daily cleaning, and regular check-ups stop problems long before they reach the pulp.

## Cracked or broken tooth
**Summary:** Teeth crack or break from hard bites, accidents, and grinding. A crack often hurts sharply when you bite down or let go — and even when it barely hurts, it lets bacteria work their way in, so it needs a dentist's assessment.
**Image:** images/cracked-tooth.png
**What it is:** Damage ranges from craze lines (tiny surface cracks in enamel, usually harmless) and chips, to cracks running into the root and split teeth that can't be saved. A crack flexes slightly when you bite, which irritates the pulp, and bacteria seep in along it over time.
**Why it happens:** Biting something unexpectedly hard (an olive stone, ice, a popcorn kernel), falls and sports injuries, teeth grinding or clenching (often during sleep), and teeth weakened by large old fillings or previous root canal treatment. Teeth used as tools — opening packets, holding pins — crack too.
**Signs to watch for:** The classic pattern is a sharp pain when biting down, and especially as you release the bite, that comes and goes and is hard to pin to one tooth. Sensitivity to cold or sweet things is common. Sometimes there are no symptoms until the pulp becomes infected. See a dentist soon rather than waiting for it to settle — cracks don't heal, and they tend to extend. **If an adult tooth is knocked out completely, minutes matter:** hold it by the crown (the white top), never the root; rinse it briefly without scrubbing; if you can, push it gently back into its socket and bite on a clean cloth; otherwise store it in milk or inside your cheek. Get to a dentist within the hour — reinsertion often succeeds.
**How dentists confirm it:** Exam with magnification and strong light, which can reveal cracks running across the tooth, and bite tests — biting on a small stick on each cusp in turn to find the one that hurts on release. A pulp vitality test checks the nerve's health. X-rays are useful for breaks and root fractures, but hairline cracks frequently don't show on X-ray at all, so diagnosis often rests on symptoms and these clinical tests.
**How it's treated:** Small chips can be smoothed or repaired with tooth-coloured bonding. A significant crack usually needs a crown to hold the tooth together. If the crack has reached the pulp, root canal treatment (often by an endodontist) may be needed first. A tooth cracked deep below the gumline or split through the root usually can't be saved and is extracted, after which replacement can be planned.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Wear a mouthguard for contact sports, avoid chewing ice and very hard foods, don't use teeth as tools, and talk to your dentist about a night guard if you grind your teeth. Treating large, weakened fillings with crowns before they crack also prevents some cases.

## Impacted wisdom teeth
**Summary:** Wisdom teeth (third molars) come through last, often in the late teens or twenties, and sometimes there isn't room — the tooth stays partly or fully trapped ("impacted"). Many impacted wisdom teeth never cause any trouble and don't automatically need removing.
**Image:** images/impacted-wisdom-teeth.jpg
**What it is:** An impacted tooth may sit sideways, slant toward its neighbour, or emerge only halfway through the gum. The commonest immediate problem is pericoronitis: when a tooth is half-erupted, a flap of gum lies over it, trapping food and bacteria underneath where a brush can't reach, and the gum becomes inflamed and infected.
**Why it happens:** It's a space problem — the jaw is simply too small for a third set of molars, so the teeth can't line up properly. Partial eruption also creates hard-to-clean spots, which is why decay commonly develops in the wisdom tooth itself or the back of the molar in front of it.
**Signs to watch for:** Often nothing at all — impacted teeth are frequently found only on an X-ray taken for other reasons. Problems can include episodes of pain, swelling, bad taste, and stiffness or difficulty opening the mouth (from pericoronitis), repeated infections in the same spot, or decay in the neighbouring tooth. Severe swelling with fever, or swelling that makes swallowing difficult, needs urgent care.
**How dentists confirm it:** An exam plus a panoramic X-ray, which shows all four wisdom teeth, their angle, and how close their roots sit to the nerve in the lower jaw — important information if removal is being considered. If a tooth is being monitored, repeat X-rays at intervals check for silent changes such as decay or cysts.
**How it's treated:** A quiet, healthy impacted tooth is often simply monitored — removal isn't automatic. During a flare-up of pericoronitis, treatment focuses on cleaning under the gum flap, salt-water rinses, and sometimes antibiotics. Removal by a general dentist or an oral surgeon is commonly considered when infections keep recurring, decay can't be repaired, the neighbouring tooth is being damaged, or a cyst forms. The decision weighs symptoms and X-ray findings against the small risks of surgery, including altered sensation if roots lie close to a nerve.
**Can it be prevented?:** The impaction itself can't be prevented — jaw size is inherited. But problems can be minimised: keep partially erupted wisdom teeth as clean as possible, and have regular check-ups with periodic X-rays so any change is caught early rather than at the first painful infection.

## Tooth loss
**Summary:** Adult teeth are lost mainly to advanced decay, gum disease, and injury. A missing tooth is more than a gap — neighbouring teeth drift, chewing suffers, and the jawbone under the gap gradually shrinks — which is why dentists usually discuss replacing it.
**Image:** images/tooth-loss.jpg
**What it is:** Tooth loss is the end point of other problems on this page rather than a disease in itself. Losing a tooth changes how the remaining teeth meet: the teeth beside the gap tip into it, the opposing tooth can over-erupt into the space, and the bite becomes uneven, straining other teeth and the jaw joint.
**Why it happens:** The leading causes are periodontitis (gum disease destroying the bone that holds teeth), extensive decay that leaves a tooth unrestorable, abscesses, and trauma. Some teeth are also planned extractions — for example, hopeless teeth removed as part of wider treatment.
**Signs to watch for:** The warning signs are those of the underlying causes: a loose tooth, deep gum pockets, recurrent abscesses, or persistent toothache. After a tooth is lost, notice food trapping in the gap, remaining teeth shifting, difficulty chewing on that side, and changes to speech or appearance — especially with front teeth. People often shift to softer, less varied food, which can quietly affect nutrition.
**How dentists confirm it:** An exam and X-rays assess the gap, the health of remaining teeth and gums, and how much jawbone is available — bone quantity and general health are what determine which replacement options are realistic. The dentist will also check the causes of the loss are under control, so the same thing doesn't happen to the next tooth.
**How it's treated:** Replacement options dentists commonly consider are: an **implant** — a titanium post placed in the jawbone, topped with a crown (placed by a dentist or oral surgeon); a **bridge** — a false tooth supported by crowns on the neighbouring teeth; or a **denture** — a removable appliance replacing one, several, or all teeth. Each has trade-offs in longevity, effect on neighbouring teeth, treatment time, and cost, which varies widely — and not every option suits every person. Leaving a gap can occasionally be acceptable (for example, some back teeth), but the drifting risk should be understood first.
**Can it be prevented?:** Yes, in most cases — tooth loss is largely the preventable end of decay and gum disease. Fluoride toothpaste, daily interdental cleaning, limiting sugar, not smoking, treating gum disease early, and wearing a mouthguard for sport protect against almost all of it.

## Mouth sores that don't heal
**Summary:** Most mouth sores heal within days. One that hasn't healed after 2 weeks must be checked promptly — occasionally it's an early sign of oral cancer, and early treatment makes a real difference.
**Image:** images/non-healing-mouth-sores.jpg
**What it is:** From the teeth point of view, the commonest culprit is rubbing: a sharp or broken tooth edge, a rough filling, or an ill-fitting denture irritating the cheek, tongue, or gum until an ulcer forms. Such sores usually settle once the cause is fixed. A sore that persists, or has no obvious cause, needs investigation.
**Why it happens:** Constant friction from a tooth or denture injures the lining faster than it can heal. Most rubbing sores are harmless — but oral cancer can also begin as a non-healing sore, lump, or patch, which is why persistence is what matters.
**Signs to watch for:** Any sore, lump, or patch (red, white, or mixed) not healed in 2 weeks, even if completely painless. Painlessness is not reassurance — early oral cancer often doesn't hurt.
**How dentists confirm it:** The dentist examines the sore, fixes any sharp tooth or denture cause, and reviews it shortly after. If it still hasn't healed, referral for a biopsy (a small sample examined in a laboratory) is how oral cancer is confirmed or ruled out.
**How it's treated:** Rubbing sores heal once the cause is removed — smoothing the tooth or adjusting the denture. Anything suspicious follows the pathway in the oral cancer entry (see the mouth ailments page and our cancer section).
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Get sharp teeth and rubbing dentures fixed promptly, and treat the 2-week rule as a hard deadline: never watch and wait beyond it.
