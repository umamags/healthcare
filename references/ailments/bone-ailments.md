## Fractures (broken bones)
**Summary:** A fracture is a crack or break in a bone, usually from a fall, blow, or accident. Most heal well with the bone held still in a splint or cast, though some breaks need surgery. A limb that looks deformed, has bone showing through the skin, or turns pale, cold, or numb below the injury is an emergency.
**Image:** images/fractures.jpg
**What it is:** Bones are strong but can break under enough force. A *closed* fracture means the skin over the break is intact; an *open* (compound) fracture means the bone has pierced the skin or a wound reaches the bone — this carries a high risk of infection and is always an emergency. Breaks can be a hairline crack, a clean snap, or the bone shattered into pieces. Repeated stress can also crack a bone gradually — these stress fractures are common in runners and soldiers. Children heal faster than adults, and their bones sometimes bend or crack only partway through ("greenstick" fractures).
**Why it happens:** Usually a single injury — a fall, a sports collision, a road accident. Weaker bones break with much less force: in osteoporosis, a minor fall that would normally cause a bruise can break a hip or wrist. Long-term overuse without enough rest causes stress fractures.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain at the site (often sharp and worse when you try to move or bear weight), swelling, bruising, and sometimes an obvious bend or deformity. Get emergency help — call your local emergency number — if a limb looks deformed, bone is visible through broken skin, you cannot move the limb or bear weight, or the hand or foot below the injury turns pale, cold, or numb, which can signal damaged circulation or nerves. Don't try to straighten a deformed limb yourself; keep it still and supported.
**How doctors confirm it:** An X-ray shows most fractures and how the pieces line up. CT scans help with complex breaks (such as around joints or in the pelvis), and MRI can reveal stress fractures and hidden breaks that an early X-ray misses. Blood tests are sometimes needed before surgery or to check for underlying bone weakness.
**How it's treated:** The bones must be held in the right position while they knit together — with a splint or cast for many breaks (immobilisation), after the doctor realigns the pieces if needed. Displaced, open, or complex fractures often need surgery to fix the bone with plates, screws, or rods. Pain-relief medicines help in the early days. Once the bone has healed enough, physiotherapy and gradual exercise matter a great deal: joints stiffen and muscles weaken quickly in a cast, and rehab restores strength and movement.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Preventing falls (good lighting, handrails, sturdy footwear, vision checks) prevents many fractures, especially in older adults. Strong bones — built through weight-bearing exercise, enough calcium and vitamin D, not smoking — break less easily. Protective gear and sensible training loads reduce sports fractures.

## Osteoporosis
**Summary:** Osteoporosis means bones have gradually become thin, porous, and fragile, so they break with little force — sometimes from a minor fall or even a cough. It causes no pain or symptoms while it develops, which is why it's often only discovered after the first fracture.
**Image:** images/osteoporosis.jpg
**What it is:** Bone is living tissue that constantly breaks down and rebuilds. In osteoporosis, breakdown outpaces rebuilding, so bones lose density and their internal structure weakens. It is most common in older adults and in women after the menopause, when falling oestrogen levels speed up bone loss. The fractures that result from weakened bone — typically of the wrist, hip, and spine — are called fragility fractures. Crushed (compressed) vertebrae in the spine can quietly cause loss of height and a stooped posture.
**Why it happens:** Ageing is the main driver, but risk rises with low body weight, family history, long-term steroid medicines, some other medicines and medical conditions, low calcium or vitamin D, inactivity, smoking, and heavy alcohol use. Men get osteoporosis too, though less often and usually later in life.
**Signs to watch for:** Usually none at all until a bone breaks — osteoporosis is called a silent disease for this reason. Clues can be gradual loss of height, a developing stoop, or back pain from a vertebral fracture. A bone that breaks from a fall at standing height or less should always prompt a bone-health check.
**How doctors confirm it:** A DEXA scan is the standard test — a quick, painless, low-dose X-ray scan, usually of the hip and spine, that measures bone density and compares it with a healthy young adult's (the result is often given as a "T-score"). Blood tests check calcium, vitamin D, and other causes of bone loss. Doctors also assess overall fracture risk, including previous fractures and fall risk.
**How it's treated:** Approaches doctors commonly consider combine lifestyle and medicines. Enough calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing and balance exercise, not smoking, and limiting alcohol help preserve bone. For people at higher fracture risk, doctors may prescribe bone-protecting medicines — classes that slow bone breakdown (such as bisphosphonates) or, in some cases, medicines that help build new bone. Preventing falls is half the battle: home safety, good lighting, sturdy footwear, vision checks, and balance training all reduce the chance of the fall that causes the fracture.
**Can it be prevented?:** Largely, yes — though not completely. Building strong bones in youth and maintaining them with exercise, adequate calcium and vitamin D, and avoiding smoking gives the best protection. If you have risk factors, screening (DEXA) catches thinning early, when treatment works best.

## Osteoarthritis
**Summary:** Osteoarthritis is the commonest joint problem: the cartilage cushioning a joint gradually breaks down and the whole joint changes, causing pain and stiffness — typically in the knees, hips, and hands. It is not an inevitable part of ageing, and keeping active and managing weight do more for it than any pill.
**Image:** images/osteoarthritis.jpg
**What it is:** It's often described as "wear and tear," but that's an oversimplification. Osteoarthritis is a whole-joint process: cartilage thins and roughens, the bone beneath thickens and may grow small spurs, the joint lining can become mildly inflamed, and the surrounding muscles weaken. The result is a joint that moves less smoothly and aches under load. It usually develops slowly over years and affects one or a few joints rather than the whole body.
**Why it happens:** Risk rises with age, previous joint injury, being overweight (extra load on knees and hips, plus metabolic effects), repetitive heavy use of a joint, joint misalignment, and family history. It is not simply caused by using your joints — normal activity, including running in moderation, does not wear healthy joints out.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain in the joint during or after activity, stiffness after rest that eases within about 30 minutes of moving (a key contrast with rheumatoid arthritis, where stiffness lasts much longer), a grinding or clicking sensation, reduced range of movement, and sometimes mild swelling. Pain that wakes you at night, a joint that gives way, or rapid swelling after injury should be assessed.
**How doctors confirm it:** Usually from the story and a physical examination — tenderness, movement, and muscle strength. An X-ray can confirm it, showing narrowed joint space and bone changes, though X-ray appearance and pain levels don't always match: some people with "bad" X-rays have little pain. Blood tests are typically normal and are mainly used to rule out other types of arthritis.
**How it's treated:** The core treatment is not a medicine: regular exercise (strengthening the muscles around the joint and general aerobic activity) and weight loss if overweight consistently reduce pain and improve function. Physiotherapy, supportive footwear, and aids such as walking sticks help. Medicines doctors commonly consider include pain-relief and anti-inflammatory classes (creams and gels applied to the joint, or tablets used cautiously), and sometimes injections into the joint. When damage is severe and daily life is significantly limited, joint replacement surgery (most often hip or knee) is a very effective late option.
**Can it be prevented?:** Not entirely, but risk can be lowered: keeping a healthy weight, strengthening muscles, staying active, and getting joint injuries properly treated and rehabilitated all help protect joints over a lifetime.

## Rheumatoid arthritis
**Summary:** Rheumatoid arthritis is an autoimmune disease: the immune system mistakenly attacks the lining of the joints, causing painful, swollen, stiff joints — often the same joints on both sides of the body. Unlike osteoarthritis, it affects the whole body, and early treatment with disease-modifying medicines can prevent permanent joint damage.
**Image:** images/rheumatoid-arthritis.jpg
**What it is:** In rheumatoid arthritis the immune system inflames the synovium (joint lining), which thickens and can gradually erode cartilage and bone. It typically starts in the small joints of the hands, wrists, and feet, symmetrically — both sides together. Because it is a body-wide (systemic) inflammation, it can also cause profound tiredness and, over time, affect the lungs, eyes, heart, and blood vessels. It most often begins in middle age and is more common in women.
**Why it happens:** The immune system misfires for reasons that aren't fully understood. Genes set the susceptibility, and environmental factors — smoking is the clearest one — can trigger the disease in susceptible people. It is not caused by wear, cold weather, or anything the person did wrong, and it is not contagious.
**Signs to watch for:** Painful, swollen, warm joints, and morning stiffness lasting more than an hour that eases as you move (this long stiffness is a hallmark that distinguishes it from osteoarthritis). Symptoms fluctuate in flares and quieter periods. General tiredness, low-grade fever, and loss of appetite can accompany active disease. New joint swelling and stiffness lasting more than a few weeks should be checked promptly — early treatment changes outcomes, so don't wait for it to "settle."
**How doctors confirm it:** A rheumatologist combines the examination pattern with blood tests: inflammation markers and specific antibodies (such as rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP) that are often present. X-rays may be normal early on; ultrasound and MRI can show inflammation and early erosions sooner. Blood tests also establish a baseline for monitoring treatment.
**How it's treated:** The goal is to switch off the immune attack early and keep it off. Disease-modifying anti-rheumatic medicines (DMARDs) are the foundation — doctors commonly start them as soon as the diagnosis is made, because there is a "window of opportunity" in the first months when treatment best prevents irreversible damage. If conventional DMARDs aren't enough, targeted and biologic medicine classes that block specific parts of the immune response are considered. Anti-inflammatory medicines and short courses of steroids can bridge until DMARDs take effect. Physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and joint protection help maintain function, and regular monitoring watches for side effects and disease activity.
**Can it be prevented?:** There is no proven way to prevent it, though not smoking lowers the risk and may improve how well treatment works. The most effective "prevention" is of damage, not disease: recognising the pattern early and starting disease-modifying treatment promptly.

## Bone infection (osteomyelitis)
**Summary:** Osteomyelitis is an infection inside a bone, usually caused by bacteria. It is uncommon but serious: it needs urgent hospital assessment, a long course of antibiotics, and sometimes surgery to remove infected bone.
**Image:** images/bone-infection.jpg
**What it is:** Bone is normally well protected, but germs — most often staphylococcus bacteria from the skin — can sometimes reach and infect it. Once established inside bone, infection is hard for the immune system and medicines to clear, because damaged bone loses its blood supply. Infection can be sudden (acute, over days) or smouldering (chronic, over months, sometimes with draining wounds and recurring flare-ups). In children it often affects the long bones of the arms and legs; in adults, the spine, pelvis, and feet are common sites.
**Why it happens:** Germs reach bone in three main ways: through the bloodstream from an infection elsewhere; directly, through an open fracture, deep wound, or during surgery; or by spreading from nearby infected tissue — the classic example is a diabetic foot ulcer that deepens until it reaches bone. Risk is higher with diabetes, poor circulation, a weakened immune system, implanted metalwork, and injecting drug use.
**Signs to watch for:** Deep, localised bone pain and tenderness, fever, and redness, warmth, or swelling over the area. A wound near bone that won't heal, or starts draining pus, is a warning sign — as is any foot ulcer in a person with diabetes that probes deeply or keeps recurring. In young children, look for fever with refusal to use a limb. Severe pain with high fever and feeling very unwell is an emergency — call your local emergency number.
**How doctors confirm it:** Blood tests show inflammation and, often, the germ itself in blood cultures. MRI is the most sensitive imaging test for bone infection; X-rays are quick but may look normal for the first week or two. To identify the exact germ and choose the right antibiotic, doctors may take a sample of bone or deep tissue (biopsy or during surgery).
**How it's treated:** This is urgent, hospital-based care. Antibiotics are the mainstay — usually started through a drip (intravenously) and continued for several weeks, switching to tablets once things improve. Surgery is often needed to remove dead or infected bone and drain pus, because antibiotics alone can't sterilise bone that has lost its blood supply. Any underlying cause (such as a diabetic ulcer or infected metalwork) has to be dealt with too, and healing is monitored with repeat blood tests and imaging.
**Can it be prevented?:** Partly. Prompt cleaning and care of wounds, good management of diabetes with regular foot checks, treating skin and other infections early, and sterile surgical technique all reduce risk. Anyone with diabetes who develops a foot ulcer should have it assessed quickly — early treatment stops most infections before they reach bone.

## Bone cancer
**Summary:** Cancer that starts in a bone is rare, and it mostly affects children, teenagers, and young adults. Far more often, cancer found in an adult's bone has spread there from a cancer elsewhere — so persistent, unexplained bone pain, pain that's worse at night, or a new lump should always be checked.
**Image:** images/bone-cancer.jpg
**What it is:** Primary bone cancer — cancer beginning in bone itself — is uncommon. Its main types include osteosarcoma (usually in the long bones near the knee or shoulder, mostly in young people whose bones are growing) and Ewing sarcoma (in children and young adults). This is different from *secondary* bone cancer, where a cancer that started elsewhere — commonly breast, prostate, or lung — spreads to bone; in adults, secondary spread accounts for most cancer found in bones and is managed as part of that original cancer. For the broader picture, see our cancer section.
**Why it happens:** For most primary bone cancers, no cause is found. Known risk factors are few: rapid bone growth in adolescence (which fits where and when osteosarcoma appears), previous radiotherapy, some inherited genetic conditions, and certain pre-existing bone conditions in older adults. It is not caused by injury — though an injury sometimes draws attention to a bone that was already weakened.
**Signs to watch for:** Pain in a bone that persists for weeks, doesn't settle with rest, and is often worse at night; a lump or swelling over a bone; and occasionally a fracture from a minor knock because the bone is weakened. In adults, new bone pain alongside a known cancer elsewhere also needs prompt review. Most bone pain has far more ordinary causes — but pain that is persistent, unexplained, and not improving deserves an X-ray and a doctor's assessment.
**How doctors confirm it:** An X-ray of the painful bone is the usual first step, followed by MRI to map the area in detail and CT or other scans to check whether anything has spread. The diagnosis is confirmed by biopsy — a sample of the abnormal bone examined under a microscope — which should be done or guided by the specialist team that would provide treatment. Blood tests help assess general health and, in secondary cancer, may point to where the cancer started.
**How it's treated:** Care is led by a specialist team. Approaches doctors commonly consider depend on the type: for primary bone cancers, chemotherapy (before and/or after surgery), surgery to remove the tumour — often saving the limb by reconstructing the bone, though amputation is occasionally necessary — and sometimes radiotherapy (more often for Ewing sarcoma). Secondary bone cancer is treated by treating the original cancer, plus bone-strengthening medicines and radiotherapy to control pain and reduce fracture risk. Pain relief and rehabilitation are important parts of care at every stage.
**Can it be prevented?:** Mostly not — there is no known lifestyle change that prevents primary bone cancer, and screening isn't offered because it's so rare. What makes the biggest difference is early detection: getting persistent, unexplained bone pain or a new lump checked promptly, rather than waiting months to see if it goes away.
