1. What causes kidney problems?

What kidneys actually do: filter waste from blood, balance fluids and salts, regulate blood pressure, help make red blood cells
The two biggest drivers: diabetes and high blood pressure — explain why they damage kidneys over time
Other causes: infections (UTIs spreading upward), kidney stones, autoimmune conditions like lupus, inherited conditions (polycystic kidney disease), certain medications (NSAIDs like ibuprofen or naproxen with long-term use), severe dehydration, urinary blockages
2. Types of kidney disease

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) — stages 1–5 explained simply
Acute kidney injury — sudden and often reversible with prompt treatment, though severe or repeated episodes can cause lasting damage and lead to CKD
Diabetic kidney disease, glomerulonephritis (inflammation of the filters), polycystic kidney disease
Kidney stones, kidney infections, nephrotic syndrome, end-stage renal disease
3. Symptoms & screening

The tricky part: kidney disease is often silent until it's advanced
What to watch for: swelling in ankles/feet/face, changes in urination (frequency, color, foamy urine), fatigue, persistent high blood pressure, flank pain, nausea, itchy skin, metallic taste
Red flags needing urgent care: very little urine output, severe swelling, shortness of breath, confusion
Screening tests in plain terms: eGFR blood test, urine protein test (ACR), blood pressure checks — and who should get screened regularly (people with diabetes or hypertension, annually; for type 1 diabetes screening typically starts after 5 years, for type 2 from diagnosis)
4. After a diagnosis

Your care team: nephrologist, renal dietitian, primary care doctor
Slowing progression: blood pressure and blood sugar control, key medications — ACE inhibitors/ARBs are commonly prescribed when albuminuria or high blood pressure is present (they require kidney-function and potassium monitoring after starting, should never be combined with each other, and are avoided in pregnancy)
The renal diet: why sodium, potassium, phosphorus, protein, and fluids all matter — with the caveat that limits are individualized by CKD stage, lab values, and dialysis status, so they should be set with a renal dietitian rather than taken from a generic list
If it progresses: dialysis options (hemodialysis vs. peritoneal dialysis) and transplant evaluation, explained without jargon

5. Financial preparation

US-specific and important: Medicare covers end-stage renal disease regardless of age — coverage typically starts in the 4th month of dialysis (as early as the 1st month with home-dialysis training). Worth a dedicated callout: there's a separate 30-month coordination period during which Medicare pays secondary to employer health plans.

Financial assistance: American Kidney Fund grants (Safety Net grants are modest — about $200/year), hospital charity care, negotiating bills, medication assistance programs
